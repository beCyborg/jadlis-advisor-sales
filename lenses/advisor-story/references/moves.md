# advisor-story — moves

Layer: oral storytelling in the deal. Sources: Smith, *Sell with a Story* (canon) and Bosworth & Zoldan, *What Great Salespeople Do* (second-source). Both are practitioner storytelling books; where they agree, that is a same-school echo, not independent confirmation. Evidence ceiling for the whole lens: practitioner-n-many. Neuroscience justifications printed in the second source (mirror neurons, hemispheres, limbic-first decisions, 7/38/55) are not carried here as fact — see myths-and-demotions.md.

## Stage 0 — Seller posture before the call

### [STORY:REDEFINE-SELL] Restate the call objective as a change decision
**Move:** In call planning, swap "what problem do I solve and how do I prove it" for "what change is this person deciding whether to make, and what would make that change feel safe." Plan the emotional prerequisite as its own step, not as warm-up.
**APPLY WHEN:** Buyer is nominally interested but keeps deferring; the status quo, not a competitor, is the opponent.
**AVOID WHEN:** Transactional reorder or renewal where the change decision is already made and the work is logistics.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** assertion — the second source's redefinition, synthesized from outside disciplines, no sales-outcome data [Bosworth].

### [STORY:DROP-PERFECT] Stop performing the all-answers expert
**Move:** Keep the domain expertise, drop the posture of having every answer; say plainly when you do not know and answer from having been there rather than from authority.
**APPLY WHEN:** Buyer is generally skeptical of vendors; you catch yourself rehearsing to look flawless before a high-stakes meeting.
**AVOID WHEN:** It substitutes for preparation — product, pricing, market and process must still be known cold; "I don't know" on basics reads as incompetence.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one therapist's failure with a shelter client, framed as a personal turning point [Bosworth].

### [STORY:NO-MEMORY-NO-DESIRE] Enter the call without a predetermined outcome
**Move:** Park both the old agenda (what you already believe about the account) and the new one (the deal you want). Commit to what emerges in contact, and when a question you are about to ask serves your agenda rather than your understanding, do not ask it.
**APPLY WHEN:** Discovery and early consultative calls; renewals where you carry heavy priors; relationship repair; any call entered with a forecast number in mind.
**AVOID WHEN:** Stages with hard forcing functions — pricing, mutual close plan, contract deadlines; also note it is a mindset instruction with no verification mechanism, so compliance is unmeasurable.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting/saas · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** assertion — imported from a therapy-dialogue model and maxims attributed to Bion and Shneidman, not research findings [Bosworth].

### [STORY:MOTIVESTORY] Rehearse one benefit-to-others motivation story
**Move:** Recall a specific time you made an outsized difference for a customer; reconstruct their problem, your reactions, the fix; pin the exact climax moment when you knew it mattered to them. Replay the run-up before calls you would otherwise skip.
**APPLY WHEN:** Slumps, end-of-week call avoidance, high-rejection outbound, any role with a defensible end-user benefit.
**AVOID WHEN:** The only story you can find is what the job did for your own career — that variant showed no effect; also weak where the product has no credible beneficiary.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — leans on Grant's 2008 randomized call-center study plus interviewee self-reports; the study measured fundraisers reading a story, not sellers telling one [Smith].

### [STORY:DETACHFRAME] Pre-call reframe that removes neediness from your voice
**Move:** Before the call, run an internal story in which you do not need this deal — or the softer version, in which you are the buyer's assistant helping them find what is best. Target is a tone that reads as consultant, not as audible quota.
**APPLY WHEN:** High-stakes single deals, thin pipeline where desperation would leak, founder-led selling.
**AVOID WHEN:** Detachment would license under-preparation or genuine indifference to the account's outcome.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=service/consulting/saas · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — borrowed from a fictional character in another author's novel plus the author's own trial [Smith].

### [STORY:TYPE-A-CURB] Rein in your own tempo
**Move:** Slow down deliberately — no multitasking, no finishing the buyer's sentences, no racing for the finish line. Match the buyer's pace, especially with a reserved buyer.
**APPLY WHEN:** You are the fast, competitive, answer-for-everything type and the buyer is quiet or deliberate.
**AVOID WHEN:** The buyer is high-tempo themselves and reads slowness as low competence.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** assertion — personality-type characterisation offered without measurement [Bosworth].

### [STORY:MISTAKE-LIST] Build a mistake inventory as pre-work
**Move:** Write down three mistakes you made, three your company made, three your clients made, and commit to being willing to tell them. Then name one mistake you could never tell anyone, ask why, and ask how you would react if a friend confessed the same.
**APPLY WHEN:** Preparing a seller for vulnerability-based calls; onboarding a rep who defaults to feature-pitching.
**AVOID WHEN:** The list becomes a talk track to recite; client mistakes must never be tellable in a form that identifies or shames a real customer.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — a standing workshop exercise [Bosworth].

### [STORY:SLOW-REVEAL] Practise disclosure on a safe listener first
**Move:** Tell someone you care about something you are not proud of, reveal it slowly instead of dumping it, and when they answer with curiosity, notice deliberately what forms.
**APPLY WHEN:** The rep accepts vulnerability intellectually but freezes attempting it with a buyer; the rep has no vocabulary for their own emotional states.
**AVOID WHEN:** The person carries an active shame issue they are not resourced to handle — no safety guidance is supplied and the exercise is not therapy.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — end-of-chapter workshop exercise [Bosworth].

### [STORY:COOLDOWN-CUE] Keep a visible cue that interrupts your own defensiveness
**Move:** Keep a written reminder of the behaviour you intend to run in your line of sight; when you notice yourself getting defensive or wanting to rush, look at it and switch back to listening.
**APPLY WHEN:** The buyer's opening feels confrontational; you catch yourself thinking they do not get it; anxiety is pushing you to compress the call.
**AVOID WHEN:** The cue substitutes for preparation, or the resistance is substantive — a real objection needs an answer, not calmer listening.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — the author's self-report from a single call [Bosworth].

## Selection and inventory — what to build before you need it

### [STORY:SIXTEST] Six-marker test before anything is called a story
**Move:** Check a planned narrative for stated time, stated place, a named main character, an obstacle acting as villain, a goal the audience would call worthy, and a sequence where something happens. Two or three markers means you hold a pitch — rebuild it or stop calling it a story.
**APPLY WHEN:** Prep for any "what's our story?" call; auditing a deck, case study or customer-story asset before reuse.
**AVOID WHEN:** The asset is deliberately a spec sheet, price justification or capability claim — those work and need no storifying.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** assertion — the author's criteria, credited as inspired by Callahan's story test; no outcome data attached [Smith].

### [STORY:BUYERWANT] Build the five story types professional buyers name
**Move:** Keep ready a company founding story, a who-we-are/values story, a product origin story, a how-it-is-made story, and an integrity story — the recurring answers when procurement people were asked what they want to hear.
**APPLY WHEN:** First substantive meeting; vendor qualification or supplier review; the buyer says the category looks identical.
**AVOID WHEN:** Late-stage price and terms negotiation, or transactional reorders where the buyer already knows the company.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=product/saas/service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — qualitative interviews with sales and procurement people across 50 named organizations; frequencies not reported [Smith].

### [STORY:LESSON-FIRST] Fix the objective, or the belief, before selecting anything
**Move:** State the single thing you want this buyer to think, feel or do — the second source phrases it as the one belief they must hold, moral-sized, e.g. that there is a better way — then select a story that delivers exactly that and discard the ones that do not. Applies to rapport small talk too.
**APPLY WHEN:** Every planned story; auditing a story that rambles or has no payload.
**AVOID WHEN:** The stated point is a product claim in disguise — a point is a belief, not a feature or a differentiator; the failure mode is skipping the step, not applying it.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise/stage
**Evidence:** assertion plus practitioner-n-many — the author's seminar experience (Smith) and workshop participants reporting the pre-set point as what improved their storytelling (Bosworth); same-school echo, not independent confirmation.

### [STORY:MIRROR-HERO] Pick the hero closest to the buyer
**Move:** Choose the protagonist your buyer can most easily see themselves in — a peer customer, supplier, boss, even competitor — state the similarity explicitly early, and keep several versions so you can match the person in front of you.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any story with candidate options; buyers who need proof that people in their exact position succeeded.
**AVOID WHEN:** The only available hero is aspirational and unreachable (celebrity CEOs, sports icons) — the Superman problem; also avoid repeatedly casting yourself as hero.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise/stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — recurrent across interviewees; the negative case comes from a procurement professional describing a seller who always starred in his own stories [Smith].

### [STORY:SURFACE-STRUGGLE] Make the struggle visible, and prefer the internal one
**Move:** Before telling, ask what made the outcome hard and what the protagonist could have done instead; narrate the rejected options and the internal debate. Name the one specific obstacle between opening and climax, and prefer an obstacle inside the protagonist — temptation, insecurity, a wrong belief — over an external one.
**APPLY WHEN:** Stories that end too tidily; testimonials that read as luck; a draft that reads flat or self-congratulatory.
**AVOID WHEN:** The struggle would be invented — the standard is an honest struggle; and where the admitted flaw is the exact risk the buyer is insuring against on this deal.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise/stage
**Evidence:** assertion plus practitioner-n-many — an illustrative example from another author's book (Smith) and the repeated white-card exercise across workshops (Bosworth); same-school echo.

### [STORY:DUMBASS-BEAT] Keep the beat where you were plainly wrong
**Move:** Locate the moment you were wrong, seduced or resistant to change — real shame and internal conflict included — and keep it in the told version rather than a struggle that flatters you. A story without one reads as a brag.
**APPLY WHEN:** Early call with a defensive, arms-crossed buyer; building a story meant to earn trust; when you need the buyer to admit something embarrassing first.
**AVOID WHEN:** The flaw undermines your competence on the exact thing being bought; a committee scoring you on risk in late-stage procurement; the disclosure exceeds what the relationship can hold or exposes a third party.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one crashed sales call and one workshop rewrite; the emotional-intelligence framing attached to it is invoked, not evidenced [Bosworth].

### [STORY:STORY-SEARCH-SFC] Search by success, failure, or moment of clarity
**Move:** With the objective fixed, list every remembered instance where someone did that thing well, failed at it, or first learned the lesson; keep the list for reuse and pick per call.
**APPLY WHEN:** Building a story bank; preparing for a specific meeting with a defined objective.
**AVOID WHEN:** Selection criteria drift to entertainment value — humour and surprise belong to the craft stage, not the selection stage.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — taxonomy derived from the author's interview corpus and mapped onto the book's own examples [Smith].

### [STORY:FICTION-FLAG] Openly fabricated but plausible story, used last
**Move:** Search your own past and colleagues' experience first; fabrication is the last resort. If you invent, signal it in the first words — suppose, let's say, imagine for a minute — keep it to what routinely happens, and do not use it to smuggle unverifiable specifics.
**APPLY WHEN:** You know the general pattern but cannot access or disclose a particular case; sensitive situations; a hypothetical the buyer has not yet experienced.
**AVOID WHEN:** Any framing lets the buyer take it as a true case — the stated cost is total loss of credibility; also weak if the scenario is implausible.
**Deal-gate:** motion=inbound/founder-led/any · acv=any · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting/info-product · market=stage/consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the author's standing answer to a recurring seminar question plus a fundraising consultant's hypothetical scene [Smith].

### [STORY:THREE-BASE] Carry a minimum repertoire of three
**Move:** Keep Who I Am (your journey and why you do this), Who I Represent (your company's journey), and Who I've Helped (a buyer's change after buying from you) available at all times; they cover the curiosity, connection and trust rungs in both long enterprise cycles and one-call cycles.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any first contact, any new territory, any rep ramp.
**AVOID WHEN:** The buyer already knows you and your firm — then a bespoke story built for the specific belief is the right tool.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the workshop's standing baseline across clients [Bosworth].

### [STORY:WISH-LIST] Maintain a running story wish list
**Move:** Take the 25-story appendix list as the baseline, tick off what you already have, add situation-specific ones you need, prioritize, and keep the list live as gaps appear.
**APPLY WHEN:** Territory or role start, new product launch, or a rep review where the same one or two stories keep getting reused everywhere.
**AVOID WHEN:** It becomes a completion exercise — the list is a demand signal, not a quota to fill before selling; it also never asks what a marginal story displaces in a fixed-length call.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the author's own framework, used across his training clients [Smith].

### [STORY:25-STORY-MAP] Audit inventory against the wish-list taxonomy
**Move:** Check coverage across seven buckets — introducing yourself; stories told to yourself; rapport about you; rapport about your company; main pitch; objections; closing; after the sale — and treat the empty bucket as the diagnosis.
**APPLY WHEN:** Diagnosing why a rep stalls at a specific stage — the missing bucket usually maps to the stalled stage.
**AVOID WHEN:** Used as a mandatory 25-item completion target rather than a gap diagnostic.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the book's organizing taxonomy, derived from the author's interview corpus [Smith].

### [STORY:CARD-BUILD] Build on five cards, never chronologically
**Move:** Yellow card = the point. Red = the ending or resolution. Green = working back from those, where the journey began. White = the struggle and the moments you were plainly wrong. Blue = the turning point. Order the build point → resolution → setting → complication → turning point.
**APPLY WHEN:** Constructing any story in the inventory; rebuilding a story that rambles or has no payload; team story-building sessions.
**AVOID WHEN:** You cannot name the point first; the buyer asked directly for data; the cards become a script to memorize; or the resolution is invented rather than recalled — the method assumes a true event.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the workshop's core construction drill run across many attendees; the claim that colour aids memory is the authors' observation only [Bosworth].

### [STORY:HANDWRITE] Draft by hand, in keywords
**Move:** Write the cards by hand in bullets and keywords, ignoring grammar and complete sentences, so the editing and spell-checking impulse does not interfere with recall of sensory and emotional detail.
**APPLY WHEN:** First draft of any personal story; reps who freeze trying to phrase things correctly.
**AVOID WHEN:** Distributed teams needing shared artifacts; treat this as an anti-perfectionism device only.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** assertion — the offered rationale is the book's brain-hemisphere framing, which this lens does not carry; the practice stands or falls on preference [Bosworth].

### [STORY:BLUE-CARD] Test the turning point against the resolution
**Move:** Hunt for the key decision, event or action that flipped the expected outcome — not the moment the problem was fixed. Reaction and resolution belong on the resolution card.
**APPLY WHEN:** A story reads like a report; listeners nod but nothing lands.
**AVOID WHEN:** No such moment exists — some stories legitimately have none.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the recurring diagnostic in workshop story-building [Bosworth].

### [STORY:BOARD] Lay the cards out as a physical plot
**Move:** Once all five cards exist, arrange them chronologically as a visible storyboard; with repetition the layout becomes mental and practised tellers assemble the sequence live.
**APPLY WHEN:** Preparing a repertoire before a cycle starts; coaching a rep whose stories ramble.
**AVOID WHEN:** Mid-call improvisation by someone who has never done it on paper.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — workshop participants reportedly internalize the cards after a few stories [Bosworth].

### [STORY:NO-RESOLUTION] Keep one story that ends in failure
**Move:** Retain a story with no fix at the end — the absence of resolution is the point, and it maximizes displayed vulnerability.
**APPLY WHEN:** The belief the buyer must reach is that going it alone fails; an arms-crossed audience braced for a chest-beating pitch.
**AVOID WHEN:** The rung requires proof of capability or de-risking — late stage, committee, procurement scoring.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one coaching call and the meeting that followed [Bosworth].

### [STORY:INTERROGATE-DRAFT] Interrogate a weak draft card by card
**Move:** Coach a flat story by asking in order: what is the point, what is the resolution, what is the beginning — then push on the missing middle, asking whether there was a time the teller was not yet the person they claim to be, until a genuine failure period surfaces.
**APPLY WHEN:** Coaching or self-editing; the draft describes traits ("customer advocate") instead of change.
**AVOID WHEN:** Public coaching with the teller's boss present and genuinely damaging material surfacing — the documented case notes the teller performed partly to avoid looking weak in front of her VP.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — a single reconstructed workshop dialogue [Bosworth].

### [STORY:WHYIDO] The "why I do what I do" story
**Move:** Tell the decision moment that put you in this job — what you gave up, what convinced you, what it says about what you value. The second source builds the same story on the cards: point = why you do this, resolution = your current role, complication = your own mistakes, turning point = when you decided to become who you are now. A usable version admits there was a time you were less capable or less committed.
**APPLY WHEN:** First or second meeting; new territory or new rep on an inherited account; introducing yourself to a new stakeholder mid-deal.
**AVOID WHEN:** You cannot tell it truthfully, or the honest version is "the money"; also when the vulnerable beat is disproportionate to the relationship — a hostile committee, a formal panel — or raises doubt on the exact axis being evaluated.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=any · market=consultative/stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote plus practitioner-n-many — one rep's career-change account recalled years later by the buyer (Smith) and the standard workshop build (Bosworth); same-school echo.

### [STORY:FOUNDING] Founding story instead of company boilerplate
**Move:** Replace the credentials paragraph — rank, years, countries, volumes — and the About Us timeline with the founder's originating dissatisfaction and the moment the idea arrived. The second source extracts it by interviewing senior executives for the one story carrying the guiding belief, including the period the company lost its way. Deliberately do not explain what the company sells.
**APPLY WHEN:** Introductions, vendor overviews, "so who are you?"; enabling a sales force; brand-new territory where the firm is unknown.
**AVOID WHEN:** The buyer already knows the company well or asked for product and pricing specifics; and do not budget an hour for the extraction — the documented case took nearly a full day with five executives and open conflict.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=product/saas/service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many plus practitioner-anecdote — founding stories were among the types procurement interviewees asked for (Smith); one 2011 client engagement (Bosworth); same-school echo.

### [STORY:NARROW-REP] Narrow the company story to one unit and one point
**Move:** Do not attempt a comprehensive corporate history. Pick the business unit you actually sell, pick the one chapter of its history that makes the point you need, and tell the missteps through the founder's or an executive's point of view to keep a human face on it.
**APPLY WHEN:** You represent a large multi-product vendor; the buyer has no idea which part of your company matters to them.
**AVOID WHEN:** Solo operator or single-product company — there the personal story and the company story collapse into one.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=product/saas · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the standard workshop exercise, illustrated by one large-vendor case [Bosworth].

### [STORY:CO-BUILD-HELPED] Co-build the customer story with the customer
**Move:** Make the main character an individual customer and their personal journey, not their company's: their belief, what they can do today, how their journey started, their struggles the old way, the moment they switched. Where possible build it with them — ask for one emotion word per beat and rehearse by telling it back so they correct and fill gaps.
**APPLY WHEN:** The buyer needs proof that someone like them changed; reference-hungry mid-funnel stages.
**AVOID WHEN:** You lack the client's permission, or details are identifiable and sensitive; when the buyer wants quantified outcomes rather than narrative.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=low/mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=product/saas/service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — a standard workshop exercise; the co-build step is the authors' standing recommendation, with no comparison against third-person versions [Bosworth].

### [STORY:RIGHT-TOOL] Build the bespoke story the point needs, and go first with it
**Move:** When neither the personal nor the company story fits, name the belief the buyer must reach — for example that doing this on the cheap is false economy — search your own history for a time you made that same mistake, and tell that first.
**APPLY WHEN:** The buyer has visibly failed at something and is defensive about it; you need them to name the real cause without being told.
**AVOID WHEN:** You have no comparable failure — fabricating one is the obvious misuse mode; also when the buyer's failure is a live internal blame fight.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=low/mid/high · stakeholders=few · offer=service/consulting/saas · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one cafeteria meeting that expanded into three opportunities [Bosworth].

### [STORY:MAP-BUY-CYCLE] Map the buyer's actual gates before choosing stories
**Move:** Write the real sequence of gates the buyer passes before signing — curious about a new approach, expresses needs, willing to brainstorm with someone trusted, sees the new way, gets a committee to consider change, sees value against the cost of the status quo, sees an implementation path, commits — condense into stages, and compare with your current sales process.
**APPLY WHEN:** Building a repeatable motion; onboarding reps; diagnosing where deals stall or die at a particular stage.
**AVOID WHEN:** Treated as universal boilerplate — the source supplies two very different real cycles (eight rungs versus four); when the cycle is genuinely unknown, build it from interviews rather than inventing rungs.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=saas/product/service · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — cycles built with two real firms and used as the first exercise in every workshop, framed on Rackham's buyer-behaviour research [Bosworth].

### [STORY:LADDER-RUNG-STORY] One point, one story, per rung
**Move:** For each gate ask what belief must be established there, and build a story whose point delivers exactly that. A needs-rung story is designed to make the buyer volunteer needs; a solved-problem story is designed to make them discuss the right solution. Fly rung to rung rather than aiming at the close.
**APPLY WHEN:** Multi-step cycles where reps skip to the product pitch and lose the thread.
**AVOID WHEN:** One-call transactional sales collapse the ladder — do not manufacture eight rungs where four exist.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=low/mid/high · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — client-built ladders in both B2B and B2C engagements, no win-rate comparison [Bosworth].

## Introduction and first contact

### [STORY:SIMPLEWHAT] The hypothetical mini-case instead of the jargon answer
**Move:** Answer "what do you do?" with an invented mini-case: a fictional protagonist in an industry you serve, a plausible chain of events, the vivid failure state you prevent, then one plain sentence about what you do about it. Accept roughly 30 seconds more airtime; once fluent, ask their industry first and re-skin it live.
**APPLY WHEN:** Networking, conference intros, first email or cold call.
**AVOID WHEN:** The listener is deep in evaluation and wants specifics, references or numbers; audiences where an invented scenario reads as evasive.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/founder-led/inbound · acv=any · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** assertion — example lifted from another author's book; time estimate and effect claim stated without support [Smith].

### [STORY:HELPEDWHO] "Whom I've helped", stretched to the nearest adjacent case
**Move:** Tell one concrete engagement — situation, what you did, outcome framed as what it did for that customer's internal position rather than a cost number. When their need sits outside your track record, take the closest case you have and tell it in the terms of their question. Goal is a follow-up meeting, not a decision.
**APPLY WHEN:** Speed-networking; a buyer whose remit only partly overlaps your experience; "have you done this before?"
**AVOID WHEN:** You would imply capability you lack — the adjacent case must be told honestly as a one-off.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/founder-led · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting/saas · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one conference speed-dating episode that produced a demo [Smith].

### [STORY:LEAD-WHY] Open with the belief, not the outcome
**Move:** In the first seconds of contact, state the belief that is your story's point rather than the result you produced. Claiming you lifted a named client's revenue by 30 percent sounds like every other seller; naming the belief that drove him earns the next two minutes.
**APPLY WHEN:** Cold or warm outreach, trade shows, any first contact where you have seconds.
**AVOID WHEN:** The buyer explicitly asked for numbers or is already deep in evaluation — a belief-first opener there reads as evasive.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/inbound · acv=any · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=any · market=transactional/consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the authors' repeated prospecting practice; the claim that very few people turn them down is unquantified [Bosworth].

### [STORY:STORY-OFFER-COLD] Offer a story to a stranger at first contact
**Move:** Introduce yourself, acknowledge you have not met, and ask whether you may share a story about someone comparable to them; if they accept, tell a two-minute version.
**APPLY WHEN:** Cold approach at events; hostile or bored openings where a pitch would be refused outright.
**AVOID WHEN:** Time-boxed formal agenda slots where the ask itself reads as stalling; a buyer already annoyed by an earlier failed pitch.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound · acv=any · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=any · market=transactional/consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one trade-show script and one cafeteria opener [Bosworth].

### [STORY:DETHREAT-ROLE] Remove the seller signal from your role framing
**Move:** Present yourself through a helping role rather than a selling one — in title, opening line and stated reason for the meeting — so first contact does not fire the defensive reflex.
**APPLY WHEN:** First touch, inbound floor or chat, cold meetings where the buyer expects to be pitched.
**AVOID WHEN:** It becomes concealment of commercial intent — misrepresenting why you are there is punished once discovered; also fails where the buyer wants a vendor rep who can quote and commit.
**Deal-gate:** motion=inbound/outbound · acv=micro/low/mid · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=product/saas · market=transactional/consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one seller swapping sales-associate cards back to customer-support cards, with buyer behaviour reportedly reverting within weeks [Bosworth].

### [STORY:MICRO-AGENDA] Meet the analytical brain first with a one-sentence plan

### [STORY:MISTAKE-STORY] Open with a story in which you were the one who erred

### [STORY:THREEASKS] Target three specific buyer stories, not "discovery" in general
**Move:** Enter discovery with three named extraction targets — a get-to-know-you personal story, a problem story told as a sequence of events, and a favourite-supplier story. Treat the third as the spec for winning behaviour in this account.
**APPLY WHEN:** First and second meetings; account planning against an incumbent; relationships you intend to hold for years.
**AVOID WHEN:** Short transactional cycles with no supplier history worth narrating, or a hard 15-minute slot.

### [STORY:STORYQ] Ask questions that can only be answered with a narrative
**Move:** Four question shapes instead of a checklist: open-ended over closed ("when did you know for sure you had a real problem?"); ask about a specific event in time and never ask for "a story"; name a concrete symptom you suspect and let them recount the last occurrence; day-in-the-life, past or future.
**APPLY WHEN:** Discovery, needs qualification, vision-building before a proposal.
**AVOID WHEN:** You need a discrete fact — budget, headcount, renewal date — where closed questions are correct and the canon says keep using them.

### [STORY:SILENCE] Leave the vacuum for them to fill
**Move:** Stop talking and let the pause run; if the filler is not your voice it will be theirs, and given enough room the filler becomes a story rather than a clipped statement. **APPLY WHEN:** Any discovery call; right after an event-shaped question; when the buyer is only getting fragments in edgewise; after an emotionally loaded disclosure.
**AVOID WHEN:** Video or phone calls with lag where silence reads as a dropped connection; a signalled hard time limit; and after a price statement in negotiation, where a held pause is a recognised squeeze rather than an invitation.

### [STORY:GOFIRST] Prime reciprocity by telling the matching story yourself
**Move:** If a category of story is not coming, tell yours in that exact category first — your hometown story to get theirs, your IT annoyance to get theirs. Then stop: the moment they interrupt with their version, hand over the floor.
**APPLY WHEN:** Buyer is terse, guarded, or answering only in facts; early rapport stages.
**AVOID WHEN:** You are already the dominant talker — this is exactly the failure mode where storytelling turns the seller into a presenter and discovery dies.

### [STORY:GO-FIRST-DISCLOSE] Go first with your own failure to unlock theirs
**Move:** Before asking a buyer to admit a problem, name something imperfect on your side — your mistake, your company's, a limit of your knowledge — tell what it cost you, let it sit without pivoting to a pitch, then stop and pass the torch. Their reciprocated story is the admission you needed.
**APPLY WHEN:** Early-stage call where the buyer must concede the status quo is broken; discovery producing polite non-answers; you need acknowledgment rather than information.
**AVOID WHEN:** The disclosure is manufactured or does not match the specific admission you want; the flaw disqualifies you on the buyer's actual criteria; procurement or legal-stage conversations where the record matters; a credibility-first first meeting with an executive you have never met.

### [STORY:ABORT-INTERROGATION] Kill the question sequence at the first irritation signal
**Move:** Watch for the shutdown cluster — answers shrinking to a word, arms crossed, attention on a device, the buyer starting to question you. On that signal stop the diagnostic sequence outright and switch to a short first-person story.

### [STORY:WHOLE-STORY-INVITE] Answer "how can you help?" by asking for their whole story
**Move:** When the buyer hands you the floor, decline the opening to present: say you think you could help but would understand their position far better if they told you the whole story — then let them run, prompting only with short questions about what you just heard.

**AVOID WHEN:** Late-stage calls where specifics are wanted; short transactional calls; buyers who read open-ended invitations as dodging a direct question.

### [STORY:ARC-NOT-PAIN] Collect challenges as story complications, not as elicited pains

### [STORY:QUEST-FROM-HEAR] Build every question from what you just heard
**Move:** Drop pre-sequenced question lists; derive each question from something the buyer just said or showed, so questioning becomes a sub-activity of listening rather than the reverse.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any discovery conversation, especially where your organisation trains a fixed question sequence.
**AVOID WHEN:** Compliance or qualification data must be collected verbatim — collect it separately and say so, rather than disguising it as curiosity.

### [STORY:SHORT-Q] Keep verbal prompts extremely short
**Move:** Prefer two-to-four-word prompts — then what, why, tell me more about that — because short questions feel spontaneous while long ones feel rehearsed. For genuine confusion, ask plainly for help understanding.
**APPLY WHEN:** The buyer's narrative is moving and you only need to keep it moving.

### [STORY:BREADCRUMB] Follow the crumb, not the stated topic
**Move:** Treat every anomaly — an object thrown, a shift in tone, crumpled papers, a change in eye contact — as a crumb and ask about the crumb instead of the stated subject. Name what you observed neutrally, offer a tentative emotion label, and let them correct or escalate it.

**AVOID WHEN:** You have no relationship yet and the observation would feel invasive; or when you would use the surfaced emotion as leverage rather than to understand.

### [STORY:EMO-LADDER] Three-step emotion sequence, phrased as observations
**Move:** In order: offer a tentative label and invite correction; ask how much of that feeling there is, to get degree; then ask where it comes from, framed as a request for permission to understand. Use observations that invite an answer rather than direct questions.
**APPLY WHEN:** The buyer has surfaced something emotionally loaded but is speaking in flat business language.
**AVOID WHEN:** The buyer has shown no signal of wanting to go there — having one's story tended unsettles people unused to it; also avoid running all three steps mechanically, which recreates the scripted-sequence failure this layer condemns.

### [STORY:REFLECT-BACK] Reflect the story back with its emotions and check whether you got them
**Move:** Synthesize what was said and shown and paraphrase it back including the emotions, not only the events; close the reflection with a direct check that you understood them, and add an honest personal connection line where one exists.
**APPLY WHEN:** End of any substantial discovery block; before moving to the next story or stage.
**AVOID WHEN:** Your paraphrase would be a covert reframe toward your product — the buyer hears the tilt and the move converts into the persuasion attempt it was meant to replace; skip the connection line if the feeling is manufactured.

### [STORY:TEND-STORY] Tend the buyer's story instead of questioning it
**Move:** As listener, slow the exchange down, respond with curiosity that names the emotion you heard and asks for more, and stay in that role instead of redirecting to your agenda.
**APPLY WHEN:** The buyer begins telling you something personal or a difficult account of their situation.
**AVOID WHEN:** Performed as a listening technique rather than genuine attention — the reported effect depended on sincerity; it can also surface far more emotion than a business setting can hold.

### [STORY:TEND-DRILL] Rehearse story-tending off-target before using it on a buyer

**APPLY WHEN:** Building the skill; also the live pattern for the listening half of any discovery call.
**AVOID WHEN:** Emotion-probing a buyer who signalled they want a transactional exchange; and using the "do I get you" check to fake alignment on facts you have not understood.

### [STORY:EYE-LISTEN] Listen with your eyes, not only your ears

### [STORY:WHOLE-BRAIN-LISTEN] Synthesize what is said, how it is said, and what is not said

### [STORY:CUE-READ] Read posture and hand-to-face cues as prompts, never as conclusions

### [STORY:NO-PREMATURE] Withhold the perfect solution

**AVOID WHEN:** The buyer is late-cycle and explicitly asking for a recommendation — withholding then reads as stalling.

### [STORY:INFO-TO-STORY] Use what you learn to pick the next story, not to pitch

**AVOID WHEN:** The buyer has moved into evaluation or procurement and is explicitly requesting specifics — trading stories then reads as evasion.

### [STORY:ELICIT] Ask one specific personal question that invites their story
**Move:** When the counterpart is stiff and reading from prepared material, interrupt with a concrete, personal, non-business question about something you already know a little about, and let them narrate.
**APPLY WHEN:** Formal first meetings, scripted executive briefings, high-status counterparts who default to talking points.
**AVOID WHEN:** The personal detail is sensitive or sourced from surveillance-grade research; the source's own political example carries the caution that the resulting warmth may have been the other side's tactic.

### [STORY:SHARED-LOW-POINT] Disclose your own worst period, hedged as not identical

**APPLY WHEN:** The counterpart is defensive or cornered, or has just rejected a logical or authority-based argument; power asymmetry runs in your favour and needs neutralizing.
**AVOID WHEN:** Used as an opener with a buyer who is already engaged and businesslike; when the low point is manufactured or exaggerated; when the disclosure shifts the burden of consoling you onto them.

### [STORY:OPT-OUT-INVITE] Invite disclosure with the refusal pre-approved
**Move:** Ask whether they want to tell you what is going on and, in the same breath, say it is fine if they do not — while stating plainly that you would like to know. Use only after your own disclosure has landed.
**APPLY WHEN:** The counterpart has signalled an opening but volunteered nothing; sensitive topics such as budget pressure, internal politics, a failed prior project.
**AVOID WHEN:** Deployed cold as a rapport gambit with no disclosure of your own; and when you would not actually accept the refusal.

### [STORY:PASS-TORCH] Pass the torch after every story
**Move:** End your story and deliberately create room for theirs — either by going silent or with a short prompt inviting their side ("that is how it went for the other CIO — what is your story?").
**APPLY WHEN:** Immediately after any story you tell; whenever you need the buyer's situation in their own words rather than in your diagnostic template.
**AVOID WHEN:** You intend to keep talking anyway — a prompt followed by your own continuation reads as rhetorical and kills reciprocity.

### [STORY:POST-STORY-SILENCE] Hand the floor back and hold the pause
**Move:** After the story, stop and do nothing until the other side speaks; read any question they ask about your story as permission to continue. After a genuinely strong emotional resolution, skip the lesson and the recommendation entirely — following a climax with an ask raises the odds it reads as manipulation.
**APPLY WHEN:** Discovery and objection-handling, where the buyer's own conclusion binds harder than yours; immediately after any vulnerable disclosure.
**AVOID WHEN:** The buyer's likely reading is wrong or damaging, or the story is merely illustrative — then state the lesson; time-boxed calls with hard stops; asynchronous channels where silence carries no information. The canon concedes this is the hardest of the three exits to execute.

### [STORY:MINI-STORY-TURN] Replace a stale check-in question with a short admission plus a turn
**Move:** Instead of opening with questions, tell a short story about something recent that includes one admission of not being good at something, then hand over with an open invitation about what has been going on with them.
**APPLY WHEN:** The other side answers in one word; recurring check-ins that have gone stale; any relationship where your questions have started to feel like an interrogation.
**AVOID WHEN:** The buyer is in a hurry with a specific agenda item; when your story would run longer than their likely answer.

### [STORY:STORY-TRADE] Trade stories, escalating from business to personal
**Move:** When the buyer answers your story with one of their own, listen without steering, then offer a second story that is more personal and only loosely deal-relevant; keep alternating and do not convert the exchange back into qualification until the buyer raises a requirement themselves.
**APPLY WHEN:** Your first story landed and the buyer reciprocated; meetings with a real time budget — the observed exchange ran about thirty minutes before the requirement surfaced.
**AVOID WHEN:** Short slotted calls; buyers or cultures that treat personal disclosure at work as intrusive; any audience that has not opted into the format.

## Objections, skepticism and stalls

### [STORY:ANTI-STORY-SWAP] Replace the buyer's internal anti-story
**Move:** Probe until you can articulate the small, low-value scenario the buyer is privately running about your ROI, then tell the story of a comparable account that answered "what would you do with that capacity back?" with something tied to their primary revenue source — and stop talking.
**APPLY WHEN:** The objection is "the savings won't be worth it"; the buyer quantifies the benefit against a trivial internal task.
**AVOID WHEN:** The objection is budget or authority rather than value perception; also risky if it shames the buyer into an answer they cannot defend internally later.

### [STORY:PRICE-BENEFIT-STORY] Price story framed as buyer protection
**Move:** When defending price or an up-front payment model, drop cost-justification and tell a story where your pricing structure let you act in the customer's interest against your own revenue.

**APPLY WHEN:** Senior buyer with a public position; ideas-selling and internal influence; any contrarian claim that would be face-losing to accept.
**AVOID WHEN:** You never held their view — a fabricated conversion narrative is dishonest and fragile under questioning; also wasteful when the buyer has no stake in the opposing view.

### [STORY:PREEMPT-OBJECTION] Disarm the recurring objection before it is raised
**Move:** Identify the objection you get in nearly every call and place a story addressing it earlier in your own flow, conceding the uncomfortable truth outright and showing that the consequence the buyer fears is absorbed by you, not them.
**APPLY WHEN:** The same objection appears in almost every call; the fear is about third-party reaction — suppliers, staff, partners — rather than your product.
**AVOID WHEN:** The objection is rare — pre-empting plants a doubt that was not there; and when your story does not honestly resolve the fear.

**APPLY WHEN:** An opportunity has gone quiet or is parked at one stage; status-quo bias rather than a competitor is the obstacle.
**AVOID WHEN:** The stall has a mechanical cause — budget freeze, reorg, a missing legal step — since a belief story does not move a procurement calendar; also when your diagnosis of the stall is an untested guess.

## Closing

### [STORY:DELAY-REGRET] Cost-of-waiting story

## Committee, champion and multithreading

### [STORY:RECAP-EMAIL] Write the prospect's story back and ask for corrections
**Move:** Close the call by saying you will send a summary so you can confirm you understood who they are; then write their story, not your pitch, in the five elements — point, setting, complication, turning point, resolution — and ask whether you understood correctly before proposing the next meeting. The artifact is a forwardable script your contact can send upward.
**APPLY WHEN:** After a call where the buyer opened up; multi-silo deals where your contact must persuade others; you want a low-friction next step that also qualifies.
**AVOID WHEN:** You did not actually get the story — a generic recap exposes that you were not listening; the disclosures were confidential or politically sensitive and would damage her if forwarded; heavily regulated or record-averse accounts.

### [STORY:REPLY-TEMPERATURE] Qualify on the emotional register of the reply

**AVOID WHEN:** Cultural or seniority norms make terse email normal; the contact is a low-writing-volume buyer; treating one cold reply as a verdict on an otherwise engaged account.

**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — standing guidance for complex sales in the authors' workshops [Bosworth].

### [STORY:TRIBAL-LEADER-HUNT] Qualify on the ability to move an idea, not on budget authority
**Move:** Screen each contact on three tests — will this person hear your story, do they think the current way is worth changing, and will they carry that belief to other groups. Ask who inside the account knows how to advance a new idea, and invest in whoever passes, even several levels below an executive you could reach directly.
**APPLY WHEN:** Enterprise or multi-silo deal; disruptive offering; you have an entry point but no access to power; a top-down attempt already dead-ended.
**AVOID WHEN:** Procurement-driven or formally gated purchases where an unsponsored bottom-up motion is disqualified; small solo-buyer deals; a cycle shorter than a grassroots movement needs; deal size that genuinely requires an executive signature early.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/channel/expansion · acv=high · stakeholders=committee · offer=saas/service/consulting · market=enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — repeated in the authors' workshops as revised advice, illustrated by one contrast case; explicitly a retraction of their own earlier call-high rule [Bosworth].

### [STORY:NO-STEPPING-STONE] Do not ask your champion for access to the decision maker
**Move:** Avoid openly using a lower-level contact as a route to the signer; ask instead whether they are in a tribe and whether they lead one, and work on making them the owner of the story.
**APPLY WHEN:** You have engaged a midlevel champion who is beginning to connect you to peers.
**AVOID WHEN:** The buying process formally requires you to identify and meet the approver; the champion has explicitly offered the introduction; deadline pressure makes an indirect route unaffordable.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/expansion · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=enterprise
**Evidence:** assertion — stated as a mechanism with an imagined internal monologue, no case or count [Bosworth].

### [STORY:ENTRY-ALTITUDE] Set entry altitude from three variables
**Move:** Decide how high to connect from the size of the potential transaction, how disruptive the offering is, and how many people it affects — rather than defaulting to the C suite. Read late executive interest as a warning that tribes below have probably been talking to competitors first.
**APPLY WHEN:** Planning account entry; deciding whether to escalate.
**AVOID WHEN:** You need it to decide a borderline case — the calibration is offered without thresholds, so it does not resolve close calls.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=enterprise
**Evidence:** assertion — presented as the authors' judgment, no data attached [Bosworth].

## After the sale

### [STORY:WHAT-WORKED-WELL] "What's worked well in the past" guidance story
**Move:** Instead of telling a customer which option to take, describe how a comparable customer handled the same choice, including their concrete workaround; let them copy it or self-select something else.
**APPLY WHEN:** Post-purchase usage decisions where a bad choice degrades the experience; customers who would resist being told they are the weaker case.
**AVOID WHEN:** Safety-critical or compliance-driven instructions — indirectness is wrong when the answer must be unambiguous.
**Deal-gate:** motion=expansion · acv=micro/low/mid · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=service/product · market=transactional/consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — a travel company's named practice, reported by its leadership-development manager [Smith].

### [STORY:SERVICE-LEGEND] Recirculate outrageous service stories to existing customers
**Move:** Collect your most extreme customer-service incidents and retell them to current customers through calls, emails, newsletters or invoice notes — not to instruct them what to request, but to set the expectation of the service level they would give up by leaving.
**APPLY WHEN:** Retention-focused motion; commoditized category where switching is easy; you actually have such incidents.
**AVOID WHEN:** The incident is a one-off you cannot reproduce at scale — an expectation you fail is worse than none; and where buyers select purely on price.
**Deal-gate:** motion=expansion · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=service/product · market=transactional/consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one trainer's personal hotel incident, generalized by the author into a loyalty tactic [Smith].

### [STORY:CALL-DEBRIEF] Capture the call as a war story
**Move:** After significant calls, write up what happened including the failures, name the lesson, and circulate it to peers and successors on the account — with the reliability and promptness of an expense report.
**APPLY WHEN:** Teams selling to the same buyer set; onboarding new reps; any tactic whose outcome surprised you.
**AVOID WHEN:** Not flagged as risky by the canon — but the material exists only if pride is set aside, which is the stated obstacle.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one seller's failed in-store demonstration, shared internally as a lesson [Smith].

## Structure and editing

### [STORY:HOOK-ONE-LINE] One-sentence transition in, lesson-framed
**Move:** Enter with a single sentence tied to what was just said — a promise of the best example you have seen, the lesson you once learned, the way another client used the thing you just described — then start narrating immediately. Delete any opener that announces a story is coming.
**APPLY WHEN:** The story answers a question or objection just raised, or is the next logical piece of your pitch; any live conversation where the story must feel like dialogue.
**AVOID WHEN:** Apologizing for the story, asking permission, or previewing the ending — each is a distinct value-destroying opening. Exception: a deliberately fabricated story must be announced. Note this contradicts ASK-PERMISSION, which the second source treats as its state-change device.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise/stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the author's seminar experience plus another storytelling author's demonstration routine [Smith].

### [STORY:CONTEXT-4W] Compressed context block
**Move:** Open with when and where it happened, who the protagonist is, what they wanted, and only the background needed for their later behaviour to make sense — a couple of sentences, not a section. The second source frames the same beat as people plus stakes: the listener must know who these people are and what they stand to lose, then you cut to the action.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any told story; especially remarkable ones, where a stated time and place pre-empts "did this really happen?"
**AVOID WHEN:** Front material answers none of who/when/where/why — cut it; equally, leaving one unanswered creates a nagging question that blocks listening; and setting that starts covering everything before the incident loses the listener.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — a pattern extracted from the author's interview examples (Smith) and taught with paired before/after examples (Bosworth); the credibility effect is asserted, not measured; same-school echo.

### [STORY:HERO-RECAST] Recast the protagonist
**Move:** Where the story allows, retell it from a different character's perspective to match the buyer, replace a company protagonist with a named person or job title, and move the product out of the hero role in favour of the customer.
**APPLY WHEN:** Your existing story library stars your product or your company; buyer demographics differ from the original protagonist.
**AVOID WHEN:** You do not know the other perspective well enough — the canon expects research, not guesswork.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one in-book story retold from a spouse's viewpoint plus a consumer-goods advertising shift [Smith].

### [STORY:ARRIVE-LATE] Arrive late, leave early in the conflict
**Move:** Start the conflict as close to its climax as the story allows and stop as soon as the point is made; drop intermediate attempts and aftermath that add no lesson.
**APPLY WHEN:** The conflict section runs long; stories that lose the room in the middle.
**AVOID WHEN:** Trimming removes the visible struggle itself — conflict is meant to be the longest section, so cut detail, not difficulty.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** assertion — principle attributed to a screenwriter's memoir and a TED-coaching book, applied by the author to sales stories [Smith].

### [STORY:FEELING-TEST] Resolution completeness test
**Move:** End the story only when the audience knows how to feel about what happened — state the outcome, what changed as a result, and if needed how the protagonist judged the decision in hindsight.
**APPLY WHEN:** Stories that stop at the factual outcome and land flat.
**AVOID WHEN:** No contraindication given; overrunning past the emotional close conflicts with ARRIVE-LATE.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** assertion — a test derived from an educational philosopher's book, presented as a claim, not a tested criterion [Smith].

### [STORY:LESSON-EXIT] State the lesson, then the ask
**Move:** Close with a first-person framing of what you took from the episode — what I learned from that was, that's when I realized — which signals the story is over and steers interpretation without dictating it; add the recommended action only where the story naturally supports one.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any story whose meaning could be read several ways; stories delivered immediately before an ask.
**AVOID WHEN:** The story does not lead to an ask — an attached recommendation reads as crass; and a lesson stated as an instruction to the buyer loses storytelling's indirect advantage.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — transition phrases drawn from the interview corpus [Smith].

### [STORY:LEARNED-CLOSE] End on the lesson and then say nothing at all
**Move:** Close with what you took away from the episode, then stop — no follow-up question, no invitation to respond, no transition to your product — and let the silence sit for several seconds. This is the stricter variant of LESSON-EXIT: the second source forbids the attached ask entirely.
**APPLY WHEN:** Immediately after any personal story; whenever you would otherwise reach for a diagnostic question.
**AVOID WHEN:** Group calls where an internal colleague fills the silence, or video calls with lag where a pause reads as a dropped connection — pre-brief your own side that the silence is intentional.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one observed call where reciprocation followed within seconds [Bosworth].

### [STORY:TEMPLATE-AUDIT] Audit an existing story against the template row by row
**Move:** Drop the story you already tell into the structure template in outline form, top to bottom, then list every row you cannot fill from the text; those gaps become an interview list for the person it happened to. Fill in outline, convert to sentences afterwards.
**APPLY WHEN:** A war story you already use that lands unevenly; adopting a colleague's story you did not live through.
**AVOID WHEN:** Mid-call — this is prep work; also when the protagonist is unavailable to answer the gap questions.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the author's repeated coaching pattern with storytelling clients [Smith].

### [STORY:LESSON-TO-END] Move the moral out of the opening
**Move:** If the first paragraph states the takeaway, cut it and place it in the lesson slot at the end; then check whether the stated lesson is too narrow (a literal rule about one artifact) or too broad (a platitude that does not follow) and rewrite it to name the mechanism the events actually demonstrate.
**APPLY WHEN:** Reviewing a rep's story before a call; any story whose punchline is spoiled up front.
**AVOID WHEN:** Deliberately using flashback, where a fragment of the outcome is front-loaded on purpose.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — demonstrated on the single story-clinic case [Smith].

### [STORY:RELEVANCE-CUT] Nine-question relevance test for every passage
**Move:** For each paragraph ask whether it answers any of: why am I telling you this, where and when, who is the hero and what does he want, what background explains motivation, what was the challenge, what did you do, how did it end, what did you learn, what should I do. Passages answering none get deleted — most often the teller's own résumé.
**APPLY WHEN:** The story runs long; you catch yourself explaining your own background.
**AVOID WHEN:** The background detail is the motivation — then it belongs in context.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — named as a common storytelling mistake the author sees repeatedly [Smith].

### [STORY:CONFLICT-HUNT] Interview for the real turning point
**Move:** Ask the protagonist which moment was hardest for him, not which looks most dramatic; expect an internal struggle or a private confrontation rather than the visible blow-up, and write the story around that moment.
**APPLY WHEN:** The conflict section reads as one adjective; you are building a story from someone else's experience.
**AVOID WHEN:** The protagonist is unreachable and you would have to invent the internal state — that crosses into fabrication.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — the story-clinic case, where a hallway rebuke outranked both the yelling and the firing [Smith].

### [STORY:CUT-BY-TEMPLATE] Shorten through the template, not the prose
**Move:** To cut, go back to the filled template and remove material section by section — delete something in every section while leaving something in every section — rather than deleting sentences from the written text, which tends to wipe out a whole structural element.
**APPLY WHEN:** Producing the two-minute and one-minute versions of a long story.
**AVOID WHEN:** Any cut that empties a whole section — that leaves the story structurally incomplete.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — one worked example cut from ~580 to 280 to 150 words, plus the author's stated preference over sentence-level trimming [Smith].

### [STORY:MINI-STORY] Compress hook, context and challenge into one sentence
**Move:** For very short stories, deliver hook, context and challenge in the opening sentence, put the actions in the next two, and end with the result plus a one-line recommended action. Make the hero someone facing exactly the listener's situation — that relatability is the hook.
**APPLY WHEN:** Answering an objection or question mid-meeting when you have seconds, not minutes.
**AVOID WHEN:** The story needs the listener to care about the character before the payoff lands.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=micro/low · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=service/product · market=transactional/consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — a 70-word story from a working practitioner, dissected against the structure [Smith].

## Emotion

### [STORY:EMO-MAP] Map the emotion beat by beat, then prioritize one or two
**Move:** Walk the filled template or the ordered cards and annotate each beat with the emotion the character or the listener should feel — the second source writes one or two circled emotion words at the bottom of every card except the point card — then pick the one or two beats with the most leverage and invest the work there.
**APPLY WHEN:** Preparing a story you will reuse; coaching a rep's story before a committee meeting; any story you intend to tell aloud more than once.
**AVOID WHEN:** Every beat gets amplified — spreading emotion evenly flattens the story and raises the manipulation risk; and when the words are manufactured rather than recalled, which produces performed delivery.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — a course exercise used across client classes (Smith) and the standard second pass of the card exercise (Bosworth); same-school echo.

### [STORY:EMO-SCALE] Encode intensity visually on the card
**Move:** Write strong emotions large or in capitals and weak ones small, circling each — the card is a visual prompt, so scale the emotion visually rather than describing it in prose.
**APPLY WHEN:** Rehearsing delivery; stories that read evenly and land flat.
**AVOID WHEN:** Written collateral or email adaptations, where card mechanics do not transfer.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** assertion — offered as a memory aid with no support beyond the authors' preference [Bosworth].

### [STORY:TELL-ME] Name the emotion outright (baseline tier)
**Move:** State the character's feeling in plain words — she was livid over the price increase — choosing a stronger and more specific word than the generic one.
**APPLY WHEN:** Low-stakes beats, or when you are short on time and the emotion still needs to register.
**AVOID WHEN:** The beat is the emotional climax — telling wastes it.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the lowest rung of a good/better/best set the author teaches [Smith].

### [STORY:SHOW-ME] Show behaviour, let the listener infer the feeling
**Move:** Replace the emotion word with observable behaviour — the red-faced tirade, the chair kicked over, the woman composing herself behind the minivan, the phone cord wound around a finger.
**APPLY WHEN:** Mid-strength beats; any moment where naming the feeling would sound like editorializing.
**AVOID WHEN:** The behaviour is ambiguous enough that listeners could infer the wrong emotion.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — recurring across the collected practitioner stories [Smith].

### [STORY:AUDIENCE-POSITION] Choose the listener's position relative to the hero
**Move:** Decide deliberately whether the listener knows more than the hero (tension, irony), less (curiosity), or exactly as much at the same moment (the listener feels what the hero felt). For the equal position, re-sequence so the listener learns each fact only when the hero did.
**APPLY WHEN:** You want a specific emotion, not just more emotion; strongest at the single most important beat.
**AVOID WHEN:** Re-sequencing would withhold something the buyer needs to evaluate your proposal.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — attributed to a screenwriting author's work, applied by the author to sales stories [Smith].

### [STORY:CHAR-KNOW] Make the character likeable before anything bad happens
**Move:** Give two or three concrete human details about who the person is and why you liked them before the loss, firing or rescue — anonymous characters produce no emotional response when hurt.
**APPLY WHEN:** The payload depends on the listener caring about an outcome.
**AVOID WHEN:** Time-boxed one-minute versions where character build-up would crowd out the resolution.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — a screenwriting author's framing plus the author's own reworked examples [Smith].

### [STORY:INTIMACY-WATCH] Read the room's intimacy threshold and pull back on cue
**Move:** Treat the emotional intimacy a group will accept as a live variable; watch for uncomfortable expressions, lost eye contact, strained voices, subject changes, and when they appear cut the emotional load rather than pushing harder.
**APPLY WHEN:** Cross-cultural or cross-border selling; unfamiliar committees; any first meeting.
**AVOID WHEN:** Never a reason to strip emotion entirely — the canon's position is that the payoff is worth the calibrated risk.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — one trainer's seminar experience (a US motivational video rejected in Paris as too sappy) plus other trainers' observations [Smith].

### [STORY:FLAT-DELIVERY] Emotional content, neutral delivery
**Move:** Let the story carry the emotion and keep your own voice and face closer to neutral the more emotional the material is; never perform the feeling.
**APPLY WHEN:** Every emotionally loaded story told to a buyer.
**AVOID WHEN:** Stage storytelling, where performance is the point. Note the direct conflict with ACT-OUT, which tells you to relive the beats physically — this layer does not resolve it; pick by room and channel.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — one radio producer's stated approach plus the author's rule that a sales call is not an acting class [Smith].

### [STORY:ACT-OUT] Relive the vivid beats instead of narrating them
**Move:** Physically act out the story's vivid moments rather than reporting them.
**APPLY WHEN:** Small-room, in-person tellings; stories whose turning point is a physical or emotional moment.
**AVOID WHEN:** Video calls, formal boardrooms, or cross-cultural settings where the performance reads as theatrical or manipulative; and wherever FLAT-DELIVERY's constraint applies.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** assertion — stated as technique with no case attached [Bosworth].

### [STORY:CHECK-IN] Recall the beat's emotion word before and during the telling
**Move:** Before each beat, silently recall the emotion word you assigned to it; the words themselves are never spoken aloud.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any live telling, especially by reps whose delivery is monotone.
**AVOID WHEN:** The recalled emotion is one you cannot regulate in front of this buyer.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the authors' standing workshop instruction; the claim that naming a state to yourself changes tone, face and posture is backed only by an unnamed "research indicates", so treat the mechanism as unproven [Bosworth].

### [STORY:EMO-DRILL] Two congruence drills
**Move:** Drill one — say that you are fine while sending the opposite signal through tone, face and posture, and observe which channel the partner believes. Drill two — act out one card's emotion word and have a partner guess it, then tell a whole story without using any emotion words and have them name the words.
**APPLY WHEN:** Rep enablement, pre-call rehearsal, coaching delivery rather than content.
**AVOID WHEN:** Drill one is run on an unwitting colleague — that is mild deception; keep it inside a training pair.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — printed as standard workshop exercises [Bosworth].

### [STORY:MANIP-CHECK] Two-test screen for improper emotional manipulation
**Move:** Before using an emotional story, check that the purpose serves the buyer's benefit and not only yours — selling something they demonstrably do not need or cannot afford makes any emotional content illegitimate — and that the emotional payload does not pull attention from the buyer's real decision criteria. Named failure family: irrelevant emotional appeals — pity, flattery, appeal to self-interest, guilt by association, playing to the gallery.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any story with a strong emotional beat, especially near a close.
**AVOID WHEN:** No exception — the operative rule is that if the audience feels unfairly manipulated, they have been. Note that this check, like WITNESS-TEST and GODIN-BUYER-TEST, is self-administered with no external check.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the author's rule, framed with a philosopher's fallacy taxonomy [Smith].

### [STORY:PALATE-CLEANSER] Insert a rational topic between the emotional climax and the close
**Move:** Do not move straight from an emotional story into asking for the decision; hold back one short logical topic, discuss it for a few minutes, then close.
**APPLY WHEN:** The story is the strongest emotional moment in the meeting and a decision is expected in that meeting.
**AVOID WHEN:** Very short calls where the filler topic would read as stalling.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** assertion — offered as the author's remedy without supporting cases [Smith].

### [STORY:INVITE-DIALOGUE] Ask the listener to discuss the story
**Move:** Explicitly invite questions, challenges and the listener's own reading — including questions about why you told it. The formulation borrowed by the canon: participation is what keeps it from being manipulation.
**APPLY WHEN:** Committee settings; discovery-stage conversations where you need the buyer talking anyway.
**AVOID WHEN:** You cannot afford the discussion to go where the buyer takes it.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — attributed to one trainer's seminar practice [Smith].

## Surprise

### [STORY:PARALLEL-LEAD] Open with the more unexpected of simultaneous events
**Move:** When two things happened at roughly the same time, open with the strange one, not the business one — the erupting volcano before the executive meeting.
**APPLY WHEN:** The story already contains two plot lines.
**AVOID WHEN:** Single plot line — the technique does not apply.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — the author's own volcano-and-conference story [Smith].

### [STORY:FLASHBACK] Lead with the most shocking scene, then backtrack
**Move:** Make the story's most surprising moment the opening scene, then return to context and run forward; you still deliver where and when, who the hero is and what he wanted — you just abandon chronological order.
**APPLY WHEN:** The story has one clearly outsized moment — a resignation on promotion day, a firing.
**AVOID WHEN:** You also want that same fact to be the surprise ending — the two techniques cancel each other.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one story as told in the book [Smith].

### [STORY:CONTEXT-OMIT] Withhold one context element and let the listener solve it
**Move:** Deliberately leave out one basic setup fact — what you sell, who the client is, what happened before the scene — and let it dawn mid-story, turning it into a small mystery.
**APPLY WHEN:** The withheld fact is one the listener can reliably infer; strongest when the reveal is emotionally loaded.
**AVOID WHEN:** The omitted fact is decision-relevant, or the listener may infer something wrong and disengage; the author flags that this deliberately breaks the standard structure.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=service/consulting/product · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — one detailed practitioner story plus the author's application to the running case [Smith].

### [STORY:FACT-TO-END] Move one key context fact to the closing line
**Move:** Relocate a single identifying fact — usually who the protagonist actually was — from the opening to the final sentence, telling the whole story about an unnamed party until the reveal.
**APPLY WHEN:** The teller is the person who made the mistake, and admitting it late buys both surprise and credibility.
**AVOID WHEN:** Combined with a flashback that already discloses the outcome; also when concealment would read as evasive rather than humble.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — two worked examples plus the author's recommendation for the running case [Smith].

## Dialogue, detail and length

### [STORY:OUTER-DIALOGUE] Quote what people actually said at the loaded moments
**Move:** Find the spots where something was said aloud, prioritize the most emotional or surprising ones, get the actual words — including exactly how a decision or a rebuke was phrased — and insert them as quotes.
**APPLY WHEN:** The turning point is a conversation; you have access to the person who was there.
**AVOID WHEN:** You would have to invent the wording of a quote attributed to a real, identifiable person.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the author's standard clinic step, illustrated on the running case [Smith].

### [STORY:INNER-DIALOGUE] Voice the unspoken thought
**Move:** Look for scenes where the real reaction never made it into speech — the prompt is where was I silent while wanting to scream — and render it as inner monologue; if the character is not you, call and ask. Powerful variant: what they wish they had said instead.
**APPLY WHEN:** Motivation, regret or fear is the point of the beat; also to deliver the lesson in the protagonist's own voice.
**AVOID WHEN:** The inner state is guessed rather than sourced.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — a routine clinic step across the author's story work [Smith].

### [STORY:DETAIL-MOTIVE] Add only detail that explains motivation or buildup
**Move:** Judge a detail by whether it explains why a character behaved as they did or shows buildup toward what happens next — the missed coffee, the tapping heel, the shrill voice before someone snaps. Length is not the test; relevance is.
**APPLY WHEN:** Listeners look confused, or the pivotal action seems unmotivated.
**AVOID WHEN:** The detail leads nowhere — the check is that the same buildup would be wasted if the scene ended uneventfully.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — a recurring correction with the author's storytelling clients [Smith].

### [STORY:SPECIFIC-WORDS] Swap general words for specific ones
**Move:** Replace vague descriptors where the fact matters: a height becomes 6-foot-4, an old car becomes an '82 Corolla with a missing headlight, "sales dropped big time" becomes a 30 percent drop over six months. Name people rather than roles.
**APPLY WHEN:** Always available; especially valuable in the resolution, where vague results undercut credibility.
**AVOID WHEN:** The specificity is invented — precision you cannot back is the embellishment risk.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the author's standard editing step [Smith].

### [STORY:DATE-ANCHOR] Anchor with time and place, skip atmospheric scene-setting
**Move:** Open with a plain specific anchor — month plus year, or "at the client I worked with last year" — instead of weather, foliage and scents; keep a sensory fact only if it is genuinely load-bearing.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any story told inside a buyer meeting, where it must read as conversation.
**AVOID WHEN:** No exception in sales; the flowery version is explicitly reserved for novels.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=transactional/consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the author reports seeing this failure mode repeatedly in new clients' first attempts [Smith].

### [STORY:ONE-VIVID-SCENE] Spend the sensory budget on a single meaningful scene
**Move:** Pick the one scene carrying the emotional climax or a metaphor for the protagonist's struggle and describe it in real visual detail; keep the rest lean.
**APPLY WHEN:** The story has an identifiable emotional climax and you have the time budget.
**AVOID WHEN:** The vividly described scene is not the important one — that is the atmospheric-opening failure at greater length.

### [STORY:METAPHOR] Compress an intangible into a metaphor
**Move:** Use a metaphor where you need detailed understanding in fewer words — a deadline as a tightening noose, a dark cloud, a truck bearing down, a lesson compressed into "don't steal the spotlight". Similes are the same tool with an explicit comparison.
**APPLY WHEN:** Intangible feelings and states — pressure, risk, dread — and replacing an abstract lesson sentence with something memorable.
**AVOID WHEN:** Technical or contractual precision is at stake; mixed or overreaching metaphors force the listener to decode; stacking so many that the language calls attention to itself.

### [STORY:LENGTH-TARGET] Target one to three minutes and carry several versions
**Move:** Aim at 1–3 minutes (roughly 150–450 words at speaking rate) and keep short, medium and long versions of your most-used stories — the second source's tiers are about 30 seconds, 3 minutes, and up to ~10 minutes with visuals, capping a first-call story at three. Switch on remaining meeting time, the buyer's attention and their reaction to the first 20 seconds, including switching mid-story: attention fading means land the point now.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any repeated story in your kit; especially when meeting length is unpredictable; first calls and time-boxed meetings.
**AVOID WHEN:** Not a hard cap — the longer version is genuinely better when the buyer wants to understand a process in depth; and do not improvise the compressed version, since improvised compression drops the point that made the story worth telling.

## Delivery

### [STORY:CONVERSATIONAL] Prepare the story, do not memorize the words
**Move:** Prepare structure and key quotes, not a script; let natural filler words, pauses and self-corrections stay in — a typical seller uses five or six fillers per minute in ordinary conversation — and expect each telling to differ.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any live buyer conversation.
**AVOID WHEN:** Not a licence to under-prepare — the point is preparing the right things.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the author's course experience plus a practitioner's formulation that a prepared presentation should not look prepared [Smith].

### [STORY:INVISIBLE] Keep the story inside the conversation
**Move:** Do not change vocal register when entering a story, do not stage it, and do nothing that would earn "what a great storyteller" as feedback in a sales meeting; blend it seamlessly into the dialogue.
**APPLY WHEN:** Every sales story.
**AVOID WHEN:** Stage or festival storytelling, where the opposite is correct.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the author's rule, contrasted with observed professional storytelling performance [Smith].

### [STORY:DRILL-FIRST] Drill until the mechanics disappear, then go live
**Move:** Rehearse a new story until you stop thinking about its structure — first with the cards, then on a non-client, and only then with a buyer. The first pass gets the sequence of events right; the second works the emotion words through delivery. If you are consciously tracking cards during a live call, you are not present with the buyer.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any new story entering your inventory; before a high-stakes first meeting.
**AVOID WHEN:** Over-rehearsal turns the story into a recitation — cards are prompts, and delivery that sounds memorized defeats the point; note also that this move's mirror step contradicts NO-MIRROR in the canon.

**Evidence:** assertion — the hand-off mechanism offered for it is unsourced and the practice-volume argument leans on a popular 10,000-hours claim the authors then deflate themselves [Bosworth].

### [STORY:WRITE-AS-SPEECH] Write stories the way you would like to speak
**Move:** For email, memo, deck or article versions keep the conversational register but strip fillers and false starts. Targets: 15–18 words per sentence, under 10 percent of words longer than two syllables, under 10 percent passive sentences, verb inside the first third, Flesch-Kincaid 7–8.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any written channel — follow-up email, proposal, case study, slide narrative.
**AVOID WHEN:** Contexts demanding formal register; the claim that corporate-memo prose is harder to follow is the author's judgment.

## The truth boundary

### [STORY:ACCURACY-NOT-PRECISION] Keep the specific detail even when unsure of the wording
**Move:** When you cannot recall exact words or figures, still tell the concrete version — a short exclamatory line of dialogue, a specific bid sequence — as long as it faithfully represents what happened; do not degrade it into "and then the price kept falling."
**APPLY WHEN:** Retelling a customer-success, objection or negotiation story where the drama lives in the specifics, and the event is yours but too old for verbatim recall.
**AVOID WHEN:** Testimony-grade settings — legal, regulatory, audit, formal RFP response — or where the buyer will cross-check numbers against contract data.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative/transactional
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — the author's own purchase story and one bid sequence recounted by an interviewee [Smith].

### [STORY:FACT-PARITY-LINE] Calibrate embellishment against your tolerance for rounding facts
**Move:** Before altering a story detail, ask whether you would make an alteration of the same magnitude to a hard number in a business review; if not, put the detail back. The canon's own line: 14.7% to 15% is acceptable, 14.7% to 25% is not.
**APPLY WHEN:** Preparing a story that will be repeated across many accounts; coaching a rep whose story has grown in retelling.
**AVOID WHEN:** The change touches a claim the buyer could rely on for a purchase decision — savings, timelines, outcomes — where no rounding at all is allowed.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** assertion — offered as the author's first-order approximation, no supporting data [Smith].

### [STORY:EXPECTATION-PREFACE] Signal the fidelity level before you start
**Move:** Open with a framing phrase telling the listener how literal to expect the account to be — "I saw this yesterday" for near-verbatim, "I've heard that" or "company legend has it" for second-hand, or an explicit admission that the tale may be apocryphal but still useful.
**APPLY WHEN:** Telling a story that is not yours, is old, or has passed through several people; rapport and company-legend stories especially.
**AVOID WHEN:** The buyer asked for a verifiable reference or case data — a hedged preface there reads as evasion.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — presented as standard practice from the author's storytelling training work [Smith].

### [STORY:WITNESS-TEST] The two-question acid test before you tell it
**Move:** Imagine someone who was actually present is in the room, then ask whether they would be offended and whether you would be embarrassed; a yes to either means you altered too much.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any story involving a named customer, colleague or competitor that has been retold more than a few times.
**AVOID WHEN:** No contraindication; the only limit is that it tests honesty, not effectiveness — and it is self-administered.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one training class where the real subject of the story turned out to be in the audience and confirmed it [Smith].

### [STORY:GODIN-BUYER-TEST] Buyer-side integrity check
**Move:** Ask two questions from the buyer's seat: knowing what you know, would they still buy; and after using the product, will they be glad they believed the story or feel cheated?
**APPLY WHEN:** Pre-close, when the story is doing persuasive heavy lifting on a claim the product must later deliver.
**AVOID WHEN:** Nothing contraindicated; note it tests the offer as much as the story.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** assertion — cited from another author's marketing book, no empirical backing [Smith].

### [STORY:HARD-SOFT-POINTS] Hard points versus soft points audit
**Move:** Classify each element before editing. Hard, leave alone: the event, the people, the obstacle, the approach used to overcome it, the resolution, the lesson. Soft, some leeway: time and place, names and physical descriptions, obvious puffery, resequencing, wording of dialogue. Anonymize by masking details or dropping names, never by changing what happened.
**APPLY WHEN:** Adapting a story for a new audience, shortening it, or scrubbing it for confidentiality.
**AVOID WHEN:** Uncertain about a specific edit — fall back to WITNESS-TEST rather than treating the list as complete.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — adapted from another storytelling author's method with the canon author's commentary [Smith].

## Sourcing the story supply

### [STORY:SELF-SOURCE] Mine your own past first
**Move:** Interview yourself using the chapter exercises; ask what you would say in a buyer's office with every statistic, chart, ranking and rating taken away; keep a running story journal in whatever capture tool is always to hand; read your own product reviews for raw material.
**APPLY WHEN:** Early in building a story set, or when your pitch leans entirely on data and rankings.
**AVOID WHEN:** You need third-party proof for a skeptical committee — self-sourced stories do not substitute for referenceable customers.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=product/saas/service/consulting · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the author's practice plus a financial-advisor storytelling book's challenge [Smith].

### [STORY:SOURCE-OTHERS] Harvest stories from colleagues and customers
**Move:** Set a cadence of one interview a week across sales, exec, customer service, marketing, product and insights until the wish list is exhausted; circulate the wish list widely so people self-match; run informal story hunts asking only whether they have any good stories; build story requests into post-sale communication.
**APPLY WHEN:** You have organizational access; especially strong for customer-success and after-the-sale stories.
**AVOID WHEN:** Story collection is mandated as compliance paperwork — that framing collides with the interview finding that pressure kills story recall.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — named programs at several organizations described by their own staff [Smith].

### [STORY:STORY-VENUES] Institutionalize story-sharing venues
**Move:** Create recurring places where stories surface: solicit customer stories on the website; run client-conference sharing sessions; make storytelling a standing rotating agenda item; hold weekly hour-long story circles; pair junior reps with senior buddies monthly; run a customer story contest.
**APPLY WHEN:** You own or influence a sales team and want story supply that outlives individual reps.
**AVOID WHEN:** Individual contributor with no organizational leverage — fall back to self-sourcing and one-on-one interviews.

### [STORY:INTERVIEW-ELICIT] Interview techniques that actually produce stories
**Move:** Talk as little as possible; use open-ended questions; anchor on a specific moment ("tell me about a time a customer called to say thank you"); use problem prompts; and only if those stall, tell a story of the kind you want, to trigger the "something like that happened to me" reflex.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any story-harvesting interview — colleague, exec or customer.
**AVOID WHEN:** Telling your story first when interview time is short; it consumes the minutes you need for theirs.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the author reports hundreds of one-on-one interviews conducted for the book [Smith].

### [STORY:SALESREP-QUESTIONS] Question set for interviewing salespeople
**Move:** With reps the word "story" is safe: ask for their most and least effective sales-call story, the most interesting story they heard from a buyer, and the legendary story in their company; then walk a checklist of situations from networking through price negotiation to service.
**APPLY WHEN:** Enablement, onboarding, or building a shared team story library.
**AVOID WHEN:** Interviewing non-sales people — the "best story" question reliably fails with executives and other functions.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the author's own interview protocol, with an explicit note that it works only with the sales population [Smith].

### [STORY:CUSTOMER-QUESTIONS] Question set for interviewing customers
**Move:** Never use the word "story". Ask for the best experience they ever had with your company; the worst with you, with competitors, and with anyone in the industry; a time they were completely surprised; and when they first knew for certain the relationship moved their bottom line — always closing with "tell me what happened".
**APPLY WHEN:** Post-sale reviews, QBRs, reference calls, win/loss debriefs.
**AVOID WHEN:** The relationship is fragile and the worst-experience questions would open a wound you cannot close in the same meeting.
**Deal-gate:** motion=expansion/any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise

### [STORY:INTERVIEW-LOGISTICS] Send questions ahead, record, get permission
**Move:** Send questions about a week ahead so people can think; disclose the recording in that message with an explicit statement of purpose and confidentiality; record with a phone app; afterwards let the source review how you captured it and approve its use — verbal internally, written for customers and suppliers.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any interview whose output will be reused publicly or across the team.
**AVOID WHEN:** The story reflects badly on a company or individual — expect approval to stall; anonymize characters, brands and companies instead.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any

### [STORY:PROCUREMENT-FEEDBACK] Use your own procurement department as a coach
**Move:** Find the buyers inside your own company, build a relationship with them, tell them your sales stories for critique, and ask for the best and worst sales stories they have ever been told.
**APPLY WHEN:** You have no access to buyer-side feedback and your win/loss data does not explain why pitches land or die.
**AVOID WHEN:** Treated as a substitute for testing on real prospects — internal procurement buys different categories than your buyers do.

## Practice, storage and rollout

### [STORY:NO-VERBATIM-SCRIPT] Never write the story out word for word
**Move:** Capture stories only as bullets and key phrases in the structure template so the wording is improvised at delivery; if memorizing helps confidence, memorize only the hook, the closing transition into the lesson, and possibly the first line of a section — and err toward less.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any story intended for live oral delivery in a call or meeting.
**AVOID WHEN:** The story must go out as written marketing copy, a case study, or a shared asset for other reps — those need full text.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — grounded in procurement interviews about what triggers the "I'm being sold to" reaction; scripted-sounding delivery was the top tell [Smith].

### [STORY:NO-MIRROR] Drop mirror practice
**Move:** Stop rehearsing in front of a mirror; practice conditions should match delivery conditions, and you are the one thing never in front of you when telling a buyer a story.
**APPLY WHEN:** Building a personal practice routine, especially if the default advice you were given was mirror work.
**AVOID WHEN:** It is already working for you — the author explicitly leaves that case alone. Note the second source prescribes exactly the mirror step this move rejects; the layer does not resolve the conflict.

**Move:** Find a private space, stand up, walk in a circle, imagine strolling with the buyer talking business, and tell the story out loud.
