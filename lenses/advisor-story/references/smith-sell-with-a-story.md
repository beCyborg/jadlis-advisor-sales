# Sell with a Story — digest

## What this book is for the lens

The lens's operating manual for narrative as a sales instrument: not "tell better stories" but a stage-by-stage inventory of which story does which job, plus a craft pipeline (select → structure → emotion → surprise → detail → length → delivery) and an explicit honesty boundary. It beats generic storytelling books because every move is indexed to a sales moment (intro, discovery, pitch, objection, close, post-sale) and because roughly half the source material comes from procurement professionals — the people who sit through more pitches than any seller — rather than from sellers describing their own wins. It beats sales-methodology books on one narrow axis only: what to say when facts are not moving the buyer. It is a skill layer, not a process; the author says so himself.

## Core model

- **Six markers define a story:** time, place, main character, obstacle, goal, events. Events decide it. Missing them means you hold a pitch, a testimonial, or a capability claim — all still useful, just not stories.
- **Claimed mechanisms:** pitch-defence drops; trust forms fast; decisions are emotional then rationalized; embedded facts recall better than free-standing ones; narrative lifts perceived value; a foreign context strips distraction; stories travel to absent decision makers; competitors cannot clone them.
- **Sequence:** the buyer's story first (it determines which of yours is worth telling), then rapport stories that *enact* traits instead of claiming them, then pitch stories, then objection and closing stories, then post-sale.
- **Selection beats delivery.** Four foundational elements: worthy lesson, relatable hero, relevant challenge, honest struggle — lesson ranked first. A 4-step selection procedure: fix the objective → search history for success / failure / moment of clarity → fabricate openly if empty → pick on hero relatability, not on humour.
- **Structure (7 steps, 4 of them story):** hook → context → challenge → conflict → resolution → lesson → recommended action. Context carries who/when/where/why and is most often skipped; conflict is longest and the only section that stands alone; exit is lesson, ask, silence, or a combination. Inverted pyramid and "tell them what you'll tell them" are rejected.
- **Craft layers:** emotion (tell → show → make me feel), surprise (beginning = attention, middle = processing, end = retention), dialogue (outer and inner), detail that explains motivation only, target 1–3 minutes.
- **Anchors:** Story Structure Template (Appendix C), Selling Story Roadmap (Appendix B), Story Clinic audit, Story Database, Appendix A's 25-story wish list, Ch. 24's 14 sourcing channels.
- **Truth boundary:** accuracy matters, precision does not; embellish a story no more than you would embellish a fact; hard points (event, people, obstacle, approach, resolution, lesson) are frozen, soft points (time, place, names, sequencing, wording) have leeway.

## Moves

**Foundations — what counts as a story**

### [STORY:SIXTEST] Six-marker story test before anything gets called a story
**Move:** Run any planned narrative past six checks — stated time, stated place, named main character, an obstacle acting as villain, a goal the audience would call worthy, and a sequence where something actually happens. Two or three markers means you hold a pitch; rebuild it or stop calling it a story.
**APPLY WHEN:** Prep for any call where someone asks "what's our story?"; auditing a deck, case study, or "customer story" asset before reuse.
**AVOID WHEN:** The asset is deliberately a spec sheet, price justification, or capability claim — those work and need no storifying.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** assertion — author's criteria, credited as inspired by Shawn Callahan's story test; no outcome data attached.

### [STORY:BUYERWANT] Build the five story types professional buyers say they want
**Move:** Keep ready a company founding story, a who-we-are/values story, a product origin story, a how-it-is-made story, and an integrity story — the recurring answers when procurement people were asked what they want to hear.
**APPLY WHEN:** First substantive meeting; vendor-qualification or supplier review; when the buyer says the category looks identical.
**AVOID WHEN:** Late-stage price/terms negotiation, or transactional reorders where the buyer already knows the company.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=product/saas/service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — qualitative interviews with sales and procurement people across 50 named organizations; frequencies not reported.

**Before the call — stories you tell yourself**

### [STORY:MOTIVESTORY] Build and rehearse one benefit-to-others motivation story
**Move:** Recall a specific time you made an outsized difference for a customer; reconstruct their problem, your reactions, the fix; pin the exact climax moment when you knew it mattered to them (their words, their voice, the note to your boss). Replay the run-up before calls you would otherwise skip.
**APPLY WHEN:** Slumps, end-of-week call avoidance, high-rejection outbound, any role with a defensible end-user benefit.
**AVOID WHEN:** The only story you can find is about what the job did for your own career — that variant showed no effect; also weak where the product has no credible beneficiary.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — cites Adam Grant's 2008 randomized call-center study plus interviewee self-reports; capped by lens ceiling, and the study measured fundraisers, not B2B sellers.

### [STORY:DETACHFRAME] Pre-call reframe that removes neediness from your voice
**Move:** Before the call, run an internal story in which you are financially independent and do not need this deal — or the softer version, in which you are the buyer's assistant helping them find what is best for them. Target is a tone that reads as consultant, not as audible quota.
**APPLY WHEN:** High-stakes single deals, thin pipeline where desperation would leak, founder-led selling.
**AVOID WHEN:** Detachment would license under-preparation or genuine indifference to the account's outcome.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=service/consulting/saas · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — borrowed from a fictional character in Lanigan & Goodbody's *Soft Tales and Hard Asses*, plus the author's own trial.

**Introduction and first contact**

### [STORY:SIMPLEWHAT] The "suppose you're in the chicken business" hypothetical
**Move:** Replace the jargon answer with an invented mini-case: a fictional protagonist in an industry you serve, a plausible chain of events, the vivid failure state you prevent, then one plain sentence about what you do about it. Accept ~30 seconds more airtime. Once fluent, ask their industry first and re-skin it live.
**APPLY WHEN:** Networking, conference intros, first email or cold call, any "what do you do?" moment.
**AVOID WHEN:** The listener is deep in evaluation and wants specifics, references, or numbers; audiences where an invented scenario reads as evasive.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/founder-led/inbound · acv=any · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** assertion — example lifted from Mark Satterfield's *Unique Sales Stories*; time estimate and effect claim stated without support.

### [STORY:HELPEDWHO] "Whom I've helped and how", stretched to the nearest adjacent case
**Move:** Tell one concrete engagement — situation, what you did, outcome framed as what it did for that customer's internal position, not just a cost number. When their need sits outside your track record, take the closest case you do have and tell it in the terms of their question. Goal is a follow-up meeting, not a decision.
**APPLY WHEN:** Speed-networking; a buyer whose remit only partly overlaps your experience; "have you done this before?"
**AVOID WHEN:** You would have to imply capability you lack — the adjacent case must be told honestly as a one-off.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/founder-led · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting/saas · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one conference speed-dating episode (Ben Koberna of EASI with an Abercrombie & Fitch procurement executive) that produced a demo.

**Discovery — getting the buyer to tell their story**

### [STORY:THREEASKS] Target three specific buyer stories, not "discovery" in general
**Move:** Enter discovery with three named extraction targets — a get-to-know-you personal story, a problem story told as a sequence of events, and a favourite-supplier story. Treat the third as the spec for winning behaviour in this account; nobody's favourite supplier is the one that discounts most.
**APPLY WHEN:** First and second meetings; account planning against an incumbent; relationships you intend to hold for years.
**AVOID WHEN:** Short transactional cycles with no supplier history worth narrating, or a hard 15-minute slot.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=product/saas/service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — synthesized from procurement interviews, illustrated by a stranded-travellers account a sourcing director reuses with suppliers outside travel.

### [STORY:STORYQ] Ask questions that can only be answered with a narrative
**Move:** Four question shapes instead of a checklist. (1) Open-ended over closed — "when did you know for sure you had a real problem?" beats "what's your number one problem area?" (2) Ask about a specific event in time, and never ask for "a story". (3) Problem prompts: name a concrete symptom you suspect ("does it slow down after dinner?") and let them recount the last occurrence. (4) Day-in-the-life, past or future.
**APPLY WHEN:** Discovery, needs qualification, vision-building before a proposal.
**AVOID WHEN:** You need a discrete fact (budget, headcount, renewal date) — closed questions are correct there and the book says keep using them.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the author's own interviewing practice over hundreds of executive interviews, plus a door-to-door telecom operator's prompt technique.

### [STORY:SILENCE] Leave the vacuum for them to fill
**Move:** Stop talking and let the pause run. If the filler is not your voice it will be theirs, and given enough room the filler becomes a story rather than a clipped statement.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any discovery call; right after an event-shaped question; when the buyer is only getting fragments in edgewise.
**AVOID WHEN:** Video/phone calls with lag where silence reads as a dropped connection; buyers who signalled a hard time limit.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** assertion — presented as obvious but underused; no data or named practitioner.

### [STORY:OFFICEOBJ] Point at the personal object and say "tell me about that"
**Move:** Scan desk and walls on arrival — family photos, race medals, diplomas, art. Scarce personal display space signals high personal value and a story the owner wants to tell; one invitation usually opens it.
**APPLY WHEN:** In-person meetings at the buyer's site, especially early relationship stages.
**AVOID WHEN:** Remote-only relationships or hot-desked/sterile offices; also fails when run as a transparent script rather than genuine curiosity.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/expansion · acv=any · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — described as the most commonly used tactic among interviewed sellers, echoed by a named account rep.

### [STORY:OFFSITE] Move the conversation out of their office
**Move:** Manufacture non-office contact time — market visits, store walks, plant tours, conferences, and the travel hours around them. In-office buyers wear their game face and mentally queue the next three vendors; outside it they have room to narrate.
**APPLY WHEN:** Accounts worth a multi-hour investment; relationship-building phases; incumbent displacement needing depth the meeting room denies.
**AVOID WHEN:** Entertainment-flavoured versions collide with conflict-of-interest policy (golf-style access is documented as curtailed); poor fit for low-value transactional accounts.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/expansion · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=product/service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one executive with roughly three decades in sales on why office-bound buyers stay closed.

### [STORY:GOFIRST] Prime reciprocity by telling the matching story yourself
**Move:** If a category of story is not coming, tell yours in that exact category first — your hometown story to get theirs, your IT annoyance to get theirs. Then stop: the moment they interrupt with their version, hand over the floor and stay quiet.
**APPLY WHEN:** Buyer is terse, guarded, or answering only in facts; early rapport stages.
**AVOID WHEN:** You are already the dominant talker — this is exactly the failure mode where storytelling turns the seller into a presenter and discovery dies.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** assertion — reciprocity argued by introspection, no study or named case.

**Rapport — stories about you and your company**

### [STORY:WHYIDO] "Why I do what I do"
**Move:** Tell the decision moment that put you in this job — what you gave up, what convinced you, what it says about what you value. Deliver early; the visible passion is the payload and the specifics carry it.
**APPLY WHEN:** First or second meeting; new territory or new rep on an inherited account.
**AVOID WHEN:** You cannot tell it truthfully, or the honest version is "the money" — the story then works against you.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one rep's career-change account plus the buyer recalling it years later as inspiring.

### [STORY:TRUSTCANDOR] The two candour stories buyers named as instant credibility
**Move:** Keep two ready: a time you told a customer your company was not the right answer for a specific need, and a time you disclosed your own mistake before they heard it elsewhere. Both told as past cases with another client, because the equivalent promise about the future is unverifiable. Live, the same posture reads as asking good questions and conceding a problem is hard.
**APPLY WHEN:** Early trust-building; competitive situations where a rival answers "yes, we can do that" to everything; recovery after a service failure.
**AVOID WHEN:** Manufacturing false humility or disqualifying yourself from needs you can serve; also weak with no such history — the instruction is to start behaving that way, not to fake it.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=product/saas/service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — named by procurement interviewees as the two things that immediately earn trust; illustrated by a bank IT sourcing decision awarded to the less confident bidder.

### [STORY:GOTOBAT] "I'll go to bat for you with my own company"
**Move:** Recount a supplier-customer dispute you assessed fairly, concluded your own side was wrong, and lobbied internally until your company gave ground. Include what it cost your side. Establishes that you are neither a pushover nor a contract enforcer.
**APPLY WHEN:** Contract-governed relationships; renewals; accounts expecting future disputes over terms or price.
**AVOID WHEN:** Buyers read it as a promise to concede on every request — a rep who always caves loses respect.
**Deal-gate:** motion=expansion/any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=product/saas/service · market=enterprise/consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — a single named account (a printing/copier vendor rescinding a volume-triggered price increase for a bank client).

### [STORY:NOTWHO] "I'm not who you think I am" — preempt the preconception sideways
**Move:** List the negative preconceptions prospects likely hold (ask your boss, peers, predecessor, trusted customers — they know), then tell a story whose details demonstrate the opposite without naming the rumour. Denying it directly sounds paranoid and plants it in listeners who had not heard it.
**APPLY WHEN:** New rep in a competitive market with active negative word-of-mouth; career-switcher; category with reputational baggage.
**AVOID WHEN:** The preconception is factually accurate — the story is then a lie waiting to be discovered.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/expansion · acv=low/mid · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=product/service · market=transactional/consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one yearbook sales rep countering competitor claims that he would quit and return to teaching.

### [STORY:PROXYBRAG] Convey your own traits through someone else's story
**Move:** When the buyer needs to know you are driven or disciplined, tell an admiring story about a person who embodies that trait — a parent, a mentor — and let them infer the resemblance. Saying it directly, or self-starring with the same moral, reads as arrogance.
**APPLY WHEN:** Credibility-building where self-praise is culturally penalized; senior buyer audiences.
**AVOID WHEN:** The buyer explicitly asked about your own track record — deflecting to a third party there is evasive.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one bank sourcing executive's habit of telling her mother's story instead of her own.

### [STORY:FEARBOND] Bond over a fear the audience shares
**Move:** Tell a story built on an embarrassing, widely-held fear you actually experienced, and let the audience recognize themselves. The bond forms on the shared fear, not on the achievement.
**APPLY WHEN:** Group settings, speaking-led selling, audiences whose situation you once occupied.
**AVOID WHEN:** The fear is not plausibly shared, or the disclosure exposes something a professional buyer would hold against you.
**Deal-gate:** motion=inbound/founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=info-product/consulting/service · market=consultative/stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — a speaker/trainer's account of audiences reporting the same secret side-business fear.

### [STORY:CLUEHUNT] Pre-call hunt for common ground, then swap in a matching story
**Move:** Harvest connection points before and during the meeting — objects in the office, the lower half of a LinkedIn profile (groups, followed thinkers, tracked companies), a calendar-scanning briefing app — then swap one of your prepared personal stories for the one that matches.
**APPLY WHEN:** First meetings with an unfamiliar buyer; a buyer who has been distant in earlier contact.
**AVOID WHEN:** The connection is manufactured, or the research is intrusive enough to read as surveillance. The named tool (Refresh) is 2016-era — treat as a category example, not a live recommendation.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one account rep's office-scanning habit plus a strategist's call that warmed after a dog story.

### [STORY:PACING] Calibrate depth and duration of personal disclosure
**Move:** Two dials. Depth: three phases — light background up front, more personal history once trust exists, genuinely vulnerable material only inside an established relationship. Duration: let the customer set how long rapport runs, and expect wide variance by geography and person.
**APPLY WHEN:** Every relationship, but especially cross-border selling and first meetings with senior buyers.
**AVOID WHEN:** Never skipped — but applying one culture's default length in another produces either an abrupt or an intrusive impression.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — an investment banker's three-phase model plus one sales manager's North America vs Latin America contrast.

### [STORY:FOUNDING] Founding story instead of company boilerplate
**Move:** Replace the credentials paragraph (rank, years, countries, volumes) with the founder's originating dissatisfaction and the moment the idea arrived. Deliberately do not explain what the company sells — this story conveys purpose and leaves them wanting the product conversation.
**APPLY WHEN:** Introductions, RFP-adjacent vendor overviews, any "so who are you?" moment.
**AVOID WHEN:** The buyer already knows the company well, or has explicitly asked for product/pricing specifics.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=product/saas/service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — founding stories were among the types procurement interviewees asked for; the two-version teaching comparison is author-constructed.

### [STORY:DIFFSTORY] Differentiation by observed detail, not by label
**Move:** Build differentiation out of small, specific, verifiable operating behaviour — what you found when you inspected the incumbent's work, what it revealed, what you changed. Withhold the category label or credential you could claim outright and let the details prove the standard.
**APPLY WHEN:** Crowded commodity-looking categories; competitive bids; buyers who cannot articulate a difference between shortlisted vendors.
**AVOID WHEN:** No operational access to observe anything, or RFPs where a formal designation must be stated explicitly for compliance.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=service/product · market=enterprise/consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — a real-estate services procurement lead recounting the cleaning contractor's walkthrough story that won her recommendation.

**Main pitch**

### [STORY:INVENT] Product invention/discovery story
**Move:** Open with a question about a person or institution the audience already knows, narrate how that person's accumulated experience produced a surprising pattern, and how the product was built around that pattern. The invention rationale, not the feature list, is the argument.
**APPLY WHEN:** Expert audiences with shared reference points; new or unfamiliar products; internal selling of a product idea up the chain and to your own sales force.
**AVOID WHEN:** The origin is unremarkable or purely commercial; audiences lacking the shared reference that makes the opening question land.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=product/saas/info-product · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one publishing manager's repeated pitch for a grammar handbook, with commercial success reported after the fact.

### [STORY:PROBLEMSTORY] Problem story ending in an unresolved question
**Move:** Tell a first-person incident where an organization's well-intended policy produced a personal mess for its own customer. Keep sensory detail and emotional tone; end on the exasperated unanswered question, not a moral. Then pivot with the diagnostic ask — do you have protocols that could do this to your customers? — and only then introduce the fix.
**APPLY WHEN:** Buyer does not believe the problem applies to them; early pitch stage; latent-need categories like security, compliance, process risk.
**AVOID WHEN:** The buyer already articulated the pain — replaying it as a story wastes time and can read as condescension.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=saas/service/product · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one security-technology sales director's repeatedly used Las Vegas ATM story.

### [STORY:SUCCESSSTORY] Customer success story engineered for "like me"
**Move:** Build a real customer narrative with events, not a quote. Prefer first-person capture — interview or record your best customers, since self-told versions carry more trust — while accepting third-person as the practical default. Select the case whose protagonist most closely resembles the prospect in role, size, industry, and situation.
**APPLY WHEN:** Mid-pitch proof, objection prevention, categories where the buyer is comparing near-identical vendors.
**AVOID WHEN:** All you have is a quote or satisfaction statement — that is a testimonial, not a story; also weak when the reference is an obviously dissimilar buyer.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=product/saas/service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the most widespread type across interviewed sellers; supporting trust ranking is a 2007 commercial survey, "like me" mechanism credited to Cialdini.

### [STORY:BORROWED-ICON] Borrowed success story from outside your own client roster
**Move:** Take the most recognized public case in the buyer's industry that resembles the problem you solve, retell it, then explicitly connect its mechanism to what your offer does — with no implication that you did the work. Use when your own cases are confidential, thin, or unknown to the buyer.
**APPLY WHEN:** Early pitch with no referenceable customer; new firm or new practice area; teaching-led selling where the point is the method, not the credit.
**AVOID WHEN:** Any phrasing lets the listener believe it was your engagement; also when the resemblance is a stretch, or the buyer wants peer-of-mine proof, where a real customer story wins.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led/inbound/any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting/saas · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one research-firm founder/CEO reports using the Febreze repositioning story instead of his own client cases.

### [STORY:TWO-ROADS] Paired without-us / with-us story
**Move:** Tell one short story of a protagonist two years out having chosen the cheaper or no-change option and hitting the predictable failure, then the same person and horizon with your option. Keep the facts identical to your feature list and change only the lived consequence.
**APPLY WHEN:** Upsell to a better tier; buyer already wants the category and is comparing spec sheets; features are true but not felt.
**AVOID WHEN:** Discovery is unfinished — you will narrate the wrong consequence; also weak if the downside story is implausible or reads as scare-selling.
**Deal-gate:** motion=inbound/outbound · acv=micro/low · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=product/saas · market=transactional/consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — one interviewee's inbound-call practice, reported as a sustained method shift after months of poor results; hypothetical (not true) stories are the norm here.

### [STORY:STORY-BOND] Value-adding story attached to a commodity item
**Move:** Find the human reality behind a generic item in your line and narrate the concrete person whose behaviour explains why this instance behaves better than its class; hand the buyer a version they can repeat to their own stakeholders.
**APPLY WHEN:** The offer is near-identical on paper; the buyer must justify the choice upward; repeat transactional relationships with otherwise nil differentiation.
**AVOID WHEN:** The underlying difference is not real — the story then manufactures a distinction that will not survive the buyer's retelling.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/expansion · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=product · market=transactional/investor
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one broker/dealer VP's "story bonds" practice selling mortgage-backed securities to bankers.

### [STORY:CYCLORAMA] Move the point into an unfamiliar context on purpose
**Move:** When teaching or persuading, set the illustrating case outside the listener's own industry. A familiar setting invites the audience to correct your details and solve the case instead of absorbing the method; a foreign backdrop keeps the point in focus.
**APPLY WHEN:** Explaining a method, a process change, or a way of thinking; expert audiences that nitpick; workshop or exec-education style selling.
**AVOID WHEN:** The objection is specifically "you don't understand our industry" — there the near-identical reference case is the asset.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=consulting/service/info-product · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one trainer's account (Mike Figliuolo) of what happens with client-native case studies.

**Telling stories with data**

### [STORY:HOW-WE-GOT-HERE] Walk the numbers forward in time, then ask the question
**Move:** Build the figures in front of the audience chronologically — starting balance, inflows, outflows, what remains — pause after the decisive comparison, then ask them what should be done instead of announcing your decision.
**APPLY WHEN:** Internal or client audiences facing a hard constraint you want them to own; time-series data with an intervention.
**AVOID WHEN:** You are not prepared to accept the answer they give, or the audience lacks the standing to answer.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led/expansion/any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise/investor
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one founder's account (bfinance, 2000) plus the author's generalization to trend charts.

### [STORY:DATA-DISCOVERY-JOURNEY] Replay the analysis and let the audience find the answer
**Move:** Invert recommendation → reasons → evidence. Present the setup and the anomaly, show the data that makes it strange, pause, invite hypotheses, confirm which ones you also chased and why they failed, mention a few dead ends, and steer until someone states the insight. Their conclusion may make your recommendation slide unnecessary.
**APPLY WHEN:** Senior audiences who resist being told; strategy or diagnosis work where the evidence is genuinely surprising.
**AVOID WHEN:** No time to let people struggle, or the data does not support one clean insight — you would be guiding them somewhere the evidence does not carry.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led/expansion · acv=high · stakeholders=committee · offer=consulting/service/saas · market=enterprise/consultative/investor
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — the author's own P&G diaper-strategy presentation, self-reported as his most effective in 20 years.

**Objections**

### [STORY:ANTI-STORY-SWAP] Replace the buyer's internal anti-story
**Move:** Probe until you can articulate the small, low-value scenario the buyer is privately running about your ROI, then tell the story of a comparable account that answered "what would you do with that capacity back?" with something tied to their primary revenue source — and stop talking. Facts do not defeat a story; a better story does.
**APPLY WHEN:** Objection is "the savings won't be worth it"; buyer quantifies the benefit against a trivial internal task; you have any account, won or lost, that reframed the same objection.
**AVOID WHEN:** The objection is budget or authority rather than value perception; also risky if it shames the buyer into an answer they cannot defend internally later.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/inbound · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=saas/service · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — one account executive's repeated use of the same story against a common objection, with a reported pattern across prospects.

### [STORY:PRICE-BENEFIT-STORY] Price story framed as buyer protection
**Move:** When defending price or an up-front payment model, drop cost-justification and tell a story where your pricing structure let you act in the customer's interest against your own revenue. The buyer's benefit, not your overheads, is the point.
**APPLY WHEN:** Final-stage price objection driven by a category-wide belief ("nobody should charge for this"); consumer or small-buyer contexts with an emotional decision.
**AVOID WHEN:** The price objection is really a differentiation gap — then the "how we differ" story is the right tool; and never if the pricing model does not in fact protect the buyer.
**Deal-gate:** motion=inbound · acv=low/mid · stakeholders=few · offer=service/consulting · market=transactional/consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one agency owner's three-step objection routine, where the story is the third and reportedly decisive step.

**Closing**

### [STORY:DELAY-REGRET] Cost-of-waiting story
**Move:** Tell the story of a buyer who deferred implementation for sensible calendar reasons, then got hit by an unplanned event that made the deferred work impossible and compounded the pain; close by noting they would have been better positioned had they started when first discussed.
**APPLY WHEN:** Buyer agrees on value but says "not now"; capacity-constrained teams; benefits already accepted so restating them is dead weight.
**AVOID WHEN:** The delay is externally fixed (budget cycle, contract end) and no story changes it; also when your own delivery would genuinely add load during their crunch.

### [STORY:SPONSOR-FEELING-STORY] Arm the champion with a feeling story

**Story selection (before craft)**

### [STORY:LESSON-FIRST] Objective before story
**Move:** State the single thing you want this buyer to think, feel or do, then select a story that delivers exactly that; refuse to shop for "something emotional" with no objective attached. Applies to rapport small talk too — framed as chitchat with a purpose.
**APPLY WHEN:** Every planned story.
**AVOID WHEN:** No contraindication — the failure mode is skipping it, not applying it.

**Evidence:** assertion — stated as the author's experience with seminar audiences, no data.

### [STORY:MIRROR-HERO] Pick the hero closest to the buyer
**Move:** Choose the protagonist your buyer can most easily see themselves in — a peer customer, supplier, boss, even competitor — and state the similarity explicitly early; keep several versions so you can match the person in front of you.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any story with candidate options; buyers who need proof that people in their exact position succeeded.
**AVOID WHEN:** The only available hero is aspirational and unreachable (celebrity CEOs, sports icons) — the "Superman problem"; also avoid repeatedly casting yourself as hero, which reads as self-promotion.

### [STORY:SURFACE-STRUGGLE] Make the struggle visible, including the internal one

**APPLY WHEN:** Stories that currently end too tidily; testimonials that read as luck; any story where the real conflict happened inside someone's head.
**AVOID WHEN:** The struggle would be invented — the standard is an honest struggle, and manufactured drama violates it.

**APPLY WHEN:** Building a story bank; preparing for a specific meeting with a defined objective.
**AVOID WHEN:** Selection criteria drift to entertainment value — humour and surprise are deferred to the craft stage.

### [STORY:FICTION-FLAG] Openly fabricated but plausible story, used last
**Move:** Search your own past and colleagues' experience first; fabrication is the last resort. **APPLY WHEN:** You know the general pattern but cannot access or disclose any particular case; sensitive or private situations; explaining a hypothetical the buyer has not yet experienced.
**AVOID WHEN:** Any framing that lets the buyer take it as a true case — the stated cost is total loss of credibility; also weak if the scenario is implausible.

**Structure and editing**

### [STORY:HOOK-ONE-LINE] One-sentence transition in, lesson-framed
**Move:** Enter with a single sentence tied to what was just said — a promise of the best example you have seen, the lesson you once learned, the way another client used the thing you just described — then start narrating immediately. Delete any opener that announces a story is coming.
**APPLY WHEN:** The story answers a question or objection just raised, or is the next logical piece of your pitch; any live buyer conversation where the story must feel like dialogue.
**AVOID WHEN:** Apologizing for the story, asking permission, saying "let me tell you a story", or previewing the ending — each is a distinct value-destroying opening. Exception: when deliberately flagging a fabricated story, announcing it is required.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise/stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — author's seminar experience plus a demonstration routine from another storytelling author's training classes.

### [STORY:CONTEXT-4W] Compressed context block
**Move:** Open with when and where it happened, who the protagonist is, what they wanted, and only the background needed for their later behaviour to make sense — a couple of sentences, not a section.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any told story; especially remarkable ones, where a stated time and place pre-empts "did this really happen?" and signals the story is true.
**AVOID WHEN:** Front material answers none of who/when/where/why — cut it; equally, leaving one unanswered creates a nagging question that blocks listening.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — pattern extracted from the author's interview examples; the credibility effect is asserted, not measured.

### [STORY:HERO-RECAST] Recast the protagonist
**Move:** Where the story allows, retell it from a different character's perspective to match the buyer, replace a company protagonist with a named person or job title, and move the product out of the hero role in favour of the customer.
**APPLY WHEN:** Your existing story library stars your product or your company; buyer demographics differ from the original protagonist.
**AVOID WHEN:** You do not know the other perspective well enough — the book expects research, not guesswork.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — an in-book story retold from a spouse's viewpoint plus a consumer-goods advertising shift.

### [STORY:ARRIVE-LATE] Arrive late, leave early in the conflict
**Move:** Start the conflict as close to its climax as the story allows and stop as soon as the point is made; drop intermediate attempts and aftermath that add no lesson.
**APPLY WHEN:** Conflict section running long; stories that lose the room in the middle.
**AVOID WHEN:** Trimming removes the visible struggle itself — conflict is meant to be the longest section, so cut detail, not difficulty.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any

### [STORY:FEELING-TEST] Resolution completeness test
**Move:** End the story only when the audience knows how to feel about what happened — state the outcome, what changed as a result, and if needed how the protagonist judged the decision in hindsight.
**APPLY WHEN:** Stories that stop at the factual outcome and land flat.
**AVOID WHEN:** No contraindication given; overrunning past the emotional close conflicts with leave-early.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any

### [STORY:LESSON-EXIT] State the lesson, then the ask
**Move:** Close with a first-person framing of what you took from the episode ("what I learned from that was…", "that's when I realized…"), which signals the story is over and steers interpretation without dictating it; add the recommended action only where the story naturally supports one.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any story whose meaning could be read several ways; stories delivered immediately before an ask.
**AVOID WHEN:** The story does not lead to an ask — an attached recommendation can read as crass; and a lesson stated as an instruction to the buyer loses storytelling's indirect advantage.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — transition phrases drawn from the interview corpus; the "align not entertain" rationale quoted from another author.

### [STORY:POST-STORY-SILENCE] Hand the floor back
**Move:** After the story, stop and let the buyer respond — they may state the lesson themselves or answer with a story of their own. After a genuinely strong emotional resolution, skip the lesson and recommendation entirely: following a climax with an ask raises the odds it reads as manipulation.
**APPLY WHEN:** Discovery and objection-handling, where the buyer's own conclusion binds harder than yours; any story chosen well enough that the intended lesson is obvious.
**AVOID WHEN:** The buyer's likely reading is wrong or damaging, or the story is merely illustrative — then state the lesson. The book concedes this is the hardest of the three exits to execute.

**AVOID WHEN:** Mid-call — this is prep work; also when the protagonist is unavailable to answer the gap questions.

### [STORY:LESSON-TO-END] Move the moral out of the opening
**Move:** If the first paragraph states the takeaway, cut it and place it in the lesson slot at the end; then check whether the stated lesson is too narrow (a literal rule about one artifact) or too broad (a platitude that does not follow from the events), and rewrite it to name the mechanism the events actually demonstrate.
**APPLY WHEN:** Reviewing a rep's story before a call; any story whose punchline is spoiled up front.
**AVOID WHEN:** Deliberately using flashback, where a fragment of the outcome is front-loaded on purpose.

### [STORY:RELEVANCE-CUT] Nine-question relevance test for every passage
**Move:** For each paragraph ask whether it answers any of: why am I telling you this, where/when, who is the hero and what does he want, what background explains motivation, what was the challenge, what did you do, how did it end, what did you learn, what should I do. Passages answering none get deleted — most often the teller's own résumé.
**APPLY WHEN:** Story runs long; you catch yourself explaining your own background.
**AVOID WHEN:** The background detail *is* the motivation — then it belongs in context.

### [STORY:CONFLICT-HUNT] Interview for the real turning point
**Move:** Ask the protagonist which moment was hardest for him, not which looks most dramatic. Expect an internal struggle or a private confrontation rather than the visible blow-up; write the story around that moment.
**APPLY WHEN:** The conflict section reads as one adjective ("uncomfortable"); you are building a story from someone else's experience.
**AVOID WHEN:** The protagonist is unreachable and you would have to invent the internal state — that crosses into fabrication.

**Emotion**

### [STORY:EMO-MAP] Map emotions on the template, then prioritize one or two
**Move:** Walk the filled template and annotate each row with the emotion the character or the listener should feel; pick the one or two beats with the most leverage on the outcome you want and invest the work there.
**APPLY WHEN:** Preparing a story you will reuse; coaching a rep's story before a committee meeting.
**AVOID WHEN:** Every beat gets amplified — spreading emotion evenly flattens the story and raises the manipulation risk.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the author's course exercise, used across client classes.

### [STORY:TELL-ME] Name the emotion outright (baseline tier)
**Move:** State the character's feeling in plain words ("she was livid over the price increase"), choosing a stronger and more specific word than the generic one.
**APPLY WHEN:** Low-stakes beats, or when you are short on time and the emotion still needs to register.
**AVOID WHEN:** The beat is the emotional climax — telling wastes it.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the "good" rung of a good/better/best set the author teaches.

### [STORY:SHOW-ME] Show behaviour, let the listener infer the feeling
**Move:** Replace the emotion word with observable behaviour: the red-faced tirade, the incumbent kicking over a chair, the woman composing herself behind the minivan, a man winding a phone cord around his finger.
**APPLY WHEN:** Mid-strength beats; any moment where naming the feeling would sound like editorializing.
**AVOID WHEN:** The behaviour is ambiguous enough that listeners could infer the wrong emotion.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — recurring across the book's collected practitioner stories.

### [STORY:AUDIENCE-POSITION] Choose the listener's position relative to the hero
**Move:** Decide deliberately whether the listener knows more than the hero (superior → tension, irony, comedy), less (inferior → curiosity), or exactly as much at the same moment (equal → the listener feels what the hero felt). For equal position, re-sequence so the listener learns each fact only when the hero did.
**APPLY WHEN:** You want a specific emotion, not just "more emotion"; strongest at the story's single most important beat.
**AVOID WHEN:** Re-sequencing would withhold something the buyer needs to evaluate your proposal.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — attributed to Richard Krevolin's screenwriting work (*The Hook*), applied by the author to sales stories.

### [STORY:CHAR-KNOW] Spend a few sentences making the character likeable first
**Move:** Before anything bad happens to a character, give two or three concrete human details about who they are and why you liked them — Krevolin's "Stormtrooper Effect": anonymous characters produce zero emotional response when hurt.
**APPLY WHEN:** The payload depends on the listener caring about an outcome (a firing, a loss, a rescue).
**AVOID WHEN:** Time-boxed one-minute versions where character build-up would crowd out the resolution.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — Krevolin's framing plus the author's own reworked examples.

### [STORY:INTIMACY-WATCH] Read the room's intimacy threshold and pull back on cue
**Move:** Treat the emotional intimacy a group will accept as a live variable. Watch for uncomfortable expressions, lost eye contact, strained voices, subject changes; when they appear, cut the emotional load rather than pushing harder.
**APPLY WHEN:** Cross-cultural or cross-border selling; unfamiliar committees; any first meeting.
**AVOID WHEN:** Never a reason to strip emotion entirely — the author's position is that the payoff is worth the calibrated risk.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — David Hutchens's seminar experience (a US airline motivational video rejected in Paris as too sappy) plus other trainers' observations.

### [STORY:FLAT-DELIVERY] Emotional content, neutral delivery
**Move:** Let the story carry the emotion; keep your own voice and face closer to neutral the more emotional the material is. Never perform the feeling — no tearing up over your own story.
**APPLY WHEN:** Every emotionally loaded story told to a buyer.
**AVOID WHEN:** Stage storytelling, where performance is the point — a different job.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — Ira Glass's stated approach plus the author's rule that a sales call is not an acting class.

### [STORY:MANIP-CHECK] Two-test screen for improper emotional manipulation
**Move:** Before using an emotional story, check (1) that the purpose serves the buyer's benefit, not only yours — selling something they demonstrably do not need or cannot afford makes any emotional content illegitimate; and (2) that the emotional payload does not pull attention from the buyer's real decision criteria. Named failure family: Damer's irrelevant emotional appeals — pity, flattery, appeal to self-interest, guilt by association, playing to the gallery.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any story with a strong emotional beat, especially near a close.
**AVOID WHEN:** No exception — the operative rule is that if the audience feels unfairly manipulated, they have been.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the author's rule, framed with T. Edward Damer's fallacy taxonomy (*Attacking Faulty Reasoning*).

### [STORY:PALATE-CLEANSER] Insert a rational topic between the emotional climax and the close
**Move:** Do not move straight from an emotional story into asking for the decision. Hold back one short logical topic, discuss it for a few minutes, then close.
**APPLY WHEN:** The story is the strongest emotional moment in the meeting and a decision is expected in that meeting.
**AVOID WHEN:** Very short calls where the filler topic would read as stalling.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** assertion — offered as the author's remedy without supporting cases.

### [STORY:INVITE-DIALOGUE] Ask the listener to discuss the story
**Move:** Explicitly invite questions, challenges, and the listener's own reading — including questions about why you told it. Hutchens's formulation: participation is what keeps it from being manipulation.
**APPLY WHEN:** Committee settings; discovery-stage conversations where you need the buyer talking anyway.
**AVOID WHEN:** You cannot afford the discussion to go where the buyer takes it.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — attributed to David Hutchens's seminar practice.

**Surprise**

### [STORY:PARALLEL-LEAD] Open with the more unexpected of simultaneous events
**Move:** When two things happened at roughly the same time, open with the strange one, not the business one — the erupting volcano before the executive meeting.
**APPLY WHEN:** The story already contains two plot lines.
**AVOID WHEN:** Single plot line — the technique does not apply.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — the author's Iceland-volcano/Microsoft story.

### [STORY:FLASHBACK] Lead with the most shocking scene, then backtrack
**Move:** Make the story's most surprising moment the opening scene, then return to context and run forward. You still deliver where/when, who the hero is and what he wanted — you just abandon chronological order.
**APPLY WHEN:** The story has one clearly outsized moment (a resignation on promotion day, a firing).
**AVOID WHEN:** You also want that same fact to be the surprise ending — the two techniques cancel each other.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — the Chris Powers / Ariba story as told in the book.

### [STORY:CONTEXT-OMIT] Withhold one context element and let the listener solve it
**Move:** Deliberately leave out one basic setup fact — what you sell, who the client is, what happened before the scene — and let it dawn mid-story, turning it into a small mystery the listener solves.
**APPLY WHEN:** The withheld fact is one the listener can reliably infer; strongest when the reveal is emotionally loaded.
**AVOID WHEN:** The omitted fact is decision-relevant, or the listener may infer something wrong and disengage. The author flags that this deliberately breaks the standard structure.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=service/consulting/product · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — one detailed practitioner story (Michael Davis's life-insurance story) plus the author's application to the running case.

### [STORY:FACT-TO-END] Move one key context fact to the closing line
**Move:** Relocate a single identifying fact — usually who the protagonist actually was — from the opening to the final sentence, telling the whole story about an unnamed party until the reveal.
**APPLY WHEN:** The teller is the person who made the mistake, and admitting it late buys both surprise and credibility.
**AVOID WHEN:** Combined with a flashback that already discloses the outcome; also when concealment would read as evasive rather than humble.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — two worked examples (including the James Watt kettle story) plus the author's recommendation for the running case.

**Craft — dialogue, detail, length**

### [STORY:OUTER-DIALOGUE] Quote what people actually said at the loaded moments
**Move:** Find the template spots where something was said aloud, prioritize the most emotional or surprising ones, get the actual words — including exactly how a decision or a rebuke was phrased — and insert them as quotes.
**APPLY WHEN:** The turning point is a conversation; you have access to the person who was there.
**AVOID WHEN:** You would have to invent the wording of a quote attributed to a real, identifiable person.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the author's standard clinic step, illustrated on the running case.

### [STORY:INNER-DIALOGUE] Voice the unspoken thought
**Move:** Look for scenes where the real reaction never made it into speech — the prompt is "where was I silent while wanting to scream or cry" — and render it as inner monologue. If the character is not you, call and ask. Powerful variant: what they wish they had said instead.
**APPLY WHEN:** Motivation, regret, or fear is the point of the beat; also to deliver the lesson in the protagonist's own voice.
**AVOID WHEN:** The inner state is guessed rather than sourced.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — a routine clinic step across the author's story work.

### [STORY:DETAIL-MOTIVE] Add only detail that explains motivation or buildup
**Move:** Judge a detail by whether it explains why a character behaved as they did or shows buildup toward what happens next (the missed coffee, the tapping heel, the shrill voice before someone snaps). Length is not the test; relevance is. Add clarifying detail where listeners have repeatedly missed what the protagonist did wrong.
**APPLY WHEN:** Listeners look confused, or the pivotal action seems unmotivated.
**AVOID WHEN:** The detail leads nowhere — the check is that the same buildup would be wasted if the scene ended uneventfully.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — a recurring correction with the author's storytelling clients.

### [STORY:SPECIFIC-WORDS] Swap general words for specific ones
**Move:** Replace vague descriptors where the fact matters: a height becomes 6-foot-4, an old car becomes an '82 Toyota Corolla with a missing headlight, "sales dropped big time" becomes a 30 percent drop over six months. Name people rather than roles — "Julie from accounting" beats "the accounts payable clerk."
**APPLY WHEN:** Always available; especially valuable in the resolution, where vague results undercut credibility.
**AVOID WHEN:** The specificity is invented — precision you cannot back is the embellishment risk.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the author's standard editing step.

### [STORY:DATE-ANCHOR] Anchor with time and place, skip atmospheric scene-setting
**Move:** Open with a plain specific anchor — month plus year, or "at the client I worked with last year" — instead of weather, foliage and scents. Keep a sensory fact only if it is genuinely load-bearing (an unseasonably warm day while testing winter coats).
**APPLY WHEN:** Any story told inside a buyer meeting, where it must read as conversation.
**AVOID WHEN:** No exception in sales; the flowery version is explicitly reserved for novels.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=transactional/consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the author reports seeing this failure mode repeatedly in new clients' first attempts.

### [STORY:ONE-VIVID-SCENE] Spend the sensory budget on a single meaningful scene
**Move:** Pick the one scene carrying the emotional climax or a metaphor for the protagonist's struggle and describe it in real visual detail; keep the rest lean.
**APPLY WHEN:** The story has an identifiable emotional climax and you have the time budget.
**AVOID WHEN:** The vividly described scene is not the important one — that is the "warm September morning" failure at greater length.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one training-class story (a woman packing her desk before exploratory surgery), with in-room reactions reported by the author.

### [STORY:METAPHOR] Compress detail into a metaphor
**Move:** Use a metaphor where you need detailed understanding in fewer words, not more — a deadline as a tightening noose, an executive stared at "like he was from Mars", a lesson compressed into "don't steal the spotlight".
**APPLY WHEN:** Intangible feelings and states; replacing an abstract lesson sentence with something memorable.
**AVOID WHEN:** Stacking so many that the language calls attention to itself.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the author's usage plus Bosworth & Zoldan (*What Great Salespeople Do*).

### [STORY:LENGTH-TARGET] Target one to three minutes, and carry three versions
**Move:** Aim at 1–3 minutes (roughly 150–450 words at normal speaking rate). Keep short, medium and long versions of your most-used stories and switch on remaining meeting time, the buyer's attention, and their reaction to the first 20 seconds — including switching mid-story.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any repeated story in your kit; especially when meeting length is unpredictable.
**AVOID WHEN:** Not a hard cap — the longer version is genuinely better when the buyer wants to understand a process in depth.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — derived from the author's own interview corpus of sales stories, compared against his earlier leadership-story corpus.

### [STORY:CUT-BY-TEMPLATE] Shorten through the template, not the prose
**Move:** To cut, go back to the filled template and remove material section by section — delete something in every section while leaving something in every section — rather than deleting sentences from the written text, which tends to wipe out a whole structural element. Build up from the template to the target length.
**APPLY WHEN:** Producing the two-minute and one-minute versions of a long story.
**AVOID WHEN:** Any cut that empties a whole section — that leaves the story structurally incomplete.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the Febreze worked example plus the author's stated preference over sentence-level trimming.

### [STORY:MINI-STORY] Compress hook, context and challenge into one sentence
**Move:** For very short stories, deliver hook, context (when, who, what they wanted) and challenge in the opening sentence, put the actions in the next two, and end with the result plus a one-line recommended action. Make the hero someone facing exactly the listener's situation — that relatability *is* the hook.
**APPLY WHEN:** Answering an objection or question mid-meeting when you have seconds, not minutes.
**AVOID WHEN:** The story needs the listener to care about the character (Stormtrooper Effect) before the payoff lands.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=micro/low · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=service/product · market=transactional/consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the 70-word Backroads story from a working practitioner, dissected against the structure.

**Delivery**

### [STORY:CONVERSATIONAL] Prepare the story, don't memorize the words
**Move:** Prepare structure and key quotes, not a script. Let natural filler words, pauses and self-corrections stay in — a typical seller uses five or six fillers per minute in ordinary conversation and the story should sound the same. Expect each telling to differ.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any live buyer conversation.
**AVOID WHEN:** Not a licence to under-prepare — the point is preparing the right things.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the author's course experience plus a practitioner's formulation that a prepared presentation should not look prepared.

### [STORY:INVISIBLE] Keep the story inside the conversation
**Move:** Do not change vocal register when entering a story, do not stage it, and do nothing that would earn "what a great storyteller" as feedback in a sales meeting. Blend it seamlessly into the dialogue.
**APPLY WHEN:** Every sales story.
**AVOID WHEN:** Stage or festival storytelling, where the opposite is correct.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the author's rule, contrasted with observed professional storytelling performance.

### [STORY:WRITE-AS-SPEECH] Write stories the way you would like to speak
**Move:** For email, memo, deck or article versions keep the conversational register but strip fillers and false starts. Targets: 15–18 words per sentence, under 10 percent of words longer than two syllables, under 10 percent passive sentences, verb inside the first third. Check with readability diagnostics; aim Flesch-Kincaid 7–8 for stories.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any written channel — follow-up email, proposal, case study, slide narrative.
**AVOID WHEN:** Contexts demanding formal register; the author's claim is that corporate-memo prose is harder to follow and sounds pretentious.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the author's own three-version Febreze comparison plus standard readability tooling.

**The truth boundary**

### [STORY:ACCURACY-NOT-PRECISION] Keep the specific detail even when unsure of the wording
**Move:** When you cannot recall exact words or figures, still tell the concrete version — a short exclamatory line of dialogue, a specific bid sequence — as long as it faithfully represents what happened; do not degrade it into "and then the price kept falling."
**APPLY WHEN:** Drafting or retelling a customer-success, objection or negotiation story where the drama lives in the specifics; the event is yours and too old for verbatim recall.
**AVOID WHEN:** Testimony-grade settings (legal, regulatory, audit, formal RFP response), or where the buyer will cross-check numbers against contract data.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative/transactional
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — the author's own Pig Island purchase and Ben Koberna's sludge-contract bid sequence.

### [STORY:FACT-PARITY-LINE] Calibrate embellishment against your own tolerance for rounding facts
**Move:** Before altering a story detail, ask whether you would make an alteration of the same magnitude to a hard number in a business review; if not, put the detail back.
**APPLY WHEN:** Preparing a story that will be repeated across many accounts; coaching a rep whose story has grown in retelling.
**AVOID WHEN:** The change touches a claim the buyer could rely on for a purchase decision (savings, timelines, outcomes) — then no rounding at all.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** assertion — offered as the author's "first-order approximation", no supporting data.

### [STORY:EXPECTATION-PREFACE] Signal the fidelity level before you start
**Move:** Open with a framing phrase telling the listener how literal to expect the account to be — "I saw this yesterday" for near-verbatim, "I've heard that…" or "company legend has it…" for second-hand, or an explicit admission that the tale may be apocryphal but still useful.
**APPLY WHEN:** Telling a story that is not yours, is old, or has passed through several people; rapport and company-legend stories especially.
**AVOID WHEN:** The buyer asked for a verifiable reference or case data — a hedged preface there reads as evasion.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — presented as standard practice from the author's storytelling training work.

### [STORY:WITNESS-TEST] The two-question acid test before you tell it
**Move:** Imagine someone who was actually present is in the room, then ask (1) would they be offended and (2) would you be embarrassed. A yes to either means you altered too much.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any story involving a named customer, colleague or competitor that has been retold more than a few times.
**AVOID WHEN:** No contraindication; the only limit is that it tests honesty, not effectiveness.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — Michael Miller's Primo Solutions class, where the real "Harry" from the story turned out to be in the audience and confirmed it.

### [STORY:GODIN-BUYER-TEST] Buyer-side integrity check
**Move:** Ask Seth Godin's two questions from the buyer's seat: knowing what you know, would they still buy; and after using the product, will they be glad they believed the story or feel cheated?
**APPLY WHEN:** Pre-close, when the story is doing persuasive heavy lifting on a claim the product must later deliver.
**AVOID WHEN:** Nothing contraindicated; note it tests the offer as much as the story.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** assertion — cited from Godin's *All Marketers Are Liars*, no empirical backing.

### [STORY:HARD-SOFT-POINTS] Hard points vs soft points audit
**Move:** Classify each element before editing. Hard (leave alone): the essence, the event, the people, the obstacle, the approach used to overcome it, the resolution, the lesson. Soft (leeway): time and place, names and physical descriptions, obvious puffery, resequencing, quotes and dialogue. Anonymize by masking details or dropping names, never by changing what happened.
**APPLY WHEN:** Adapting a story for a new audience, shortening it, or scrubbing it for confidentiality.
**AVOID WHEN:** Uncertain about a specific edit — fall back to the witness test rather than treating the list as complete.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — adapted from Doug Stevenson's *Story Theater Method* with the author's commentary.

**Sourcing the story supply**

### [STORY:WISH-LIST] Maintain a running story wish list
**Move:** Take the Appendix A list of 25 sales stories as the baseline, tick off what you already have, add situation-specific ones you need, prioritize, and keep the list live as gaps appear.
**APPLY WHEN:** Territory or role start, new product launch, or any rep review where the same one or two stories keep getting reused everywhere.
**AVOID WHEN:** It becomes a completion exercise — the list is a demand signal, not a quota to fill before selling.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the author's own framework, used across his training clients.

### [STORY:25-STORY-MAP] The wish-list taxonomy
**Move:** Audit inventory against seven buckets — introducing yourself (what you do; whom you've helped); stories told to yourself (motivation; stress reduction); rapport about you (why you do it; a mistake; a time you said you couldn't help; going to bat; "I'm not who you think I am"); rapport about your company (founding; how you differ); main pitch (invention; problem; customer success; two-roads; value-adding); objections (response; price negotiation; pre-emption); closing (urgency; arming the sponsor; coaching the breakup); after the sale (what worked before; loyalty; summarizing the call).
**APPLY WHEN:** Diagnosing why a rep stalls at a specific stage — the missing bucket usually maps to the stalled stage.
**AVOID WHEN:** Used as a mandatory 25-item completion target rather than a gap diagnostic.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the book's organizing taxonomy, derived from the author's interview corpus.

### [STORY:SELF-SOURCE] Mine your own past first
**Move:** Interview yourself using the Part I chapter exercises; take the West/Anthony challenge by asking what you would say in a buyer's office with every statistic, chart, ranking and rating taken away; keep a running story journal in whatever capture tool is always to hand; read your own product reviews for raw material.
**APPLY WHEN:** Early in building a story set, or when your pitch leans entirely on data and rankings.
**AVOID WHEN:** You need third-party proof for a skeptical committee — self-sourced stories do not substitute for referenceable customers.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=product/saas/service/consulting · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — author's practice plus West and Anthony's *Storyselling for Financial Advisors*.

### [STORY:SOURCE-OTHERS] Harvest stories from colleagues and customers
**Move:** Set a cadence of one interview a week across sales, exec, customer service, marketing, product and insights until the wish list is exhausted; circulate the wish list widely so people self-match; do Tom Peters-style story hunts asking only "got any good stories?"; build story requests into post-sale communication.
**APPLY WHEN:** You have organizational access; especially strong for customer-success and after-the-sale stories.
**AVOID WHEN:** Story collection is mandated as compliance paperwork — that framing collides with the interview finding that pressure kills story recall.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — Pam King Sams (Children's National Medical Center) hallway rounds; Jord watches asking customers to recall someone noticing the watch, via Waqas Shah.

### [STORY:STORY-VENUES] Institutionalize story-sharing venues
**Move:** Create recurring places where stories surface: solicit customer stories on the website; run client-conference sharing sessions; make storytelling a standing rotating agenda item in staff meetings; hold weekly hour-long story circles; pair junior reps with senior buddies monthly; run a customer story contest.
**APPLY WHEN:** You own or influence a sales team and want story supply that outlives individual reps.
**AVOID WHEN:** Individual contributor with no organizational leverage — fall back to self-sourcing and one-on-one interviews.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — named programs at BizLibrary (Becky Claggett), Pearson (Tia Finn), Backroads (Rich Snodsmith).

### [STORY:INTERVIEW-ELICIT] Interview techniques that actually produce stories
**Move:** Talk as little as possible; use open-ended questions; anchor on a specific moment ("tell me about a time when a customer called to say thank you"); use problem prompts ("have you ever noticed that…" plus a pain typical of their role); and only if those stall, tell a story of the kind you want, to trigger the "something like that happened to me" reflex.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any story-harvesting interview — colleague, exec or customer.
**AVOID WHEN:** Telling your story first when interview time is short; it consumes the minutes you need for theirs.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — author reports hundreds of one-on-one interviews conducted for this book.

### [STORY:SALESREP-QUESTIONS] Question set for interviewing salespeople
**Move:** With reps the word "story" is safe. Ask for their most and least effective sales-call story, the most interesting story they ever heard from a buyer, and the legendary story in their company or industry; then walk a checklist of situations — networking, rapport, who you are, what you do, whom you work for, what the product is, customer success, customer failure, demonstrating understanding, price negotiation, objections, closing, service.
**APPLY WHEN:** Enablement, onboarding, or building a shared team story library.
**AVOID WHEN:** Interviewing non-sales people — the "best story" question reliably fails with executives, other functions, parents and children.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the author's own interview protocol, with an explicit note that it works only with the sales population.

### [STORY:CUSTOMER-QUESTIONS] Question set for interviewing customers
**Move:** Never use the word "story". Ask for the best experience they ever had with your company; the worst with you, with competitors, and with anyone in the industry; a time they were completely surprised by something you or a competitor did; and when they first knew for certain the relationship moved their bottom line — always closing with "tell me what happened".
**APPLY WHEN:** Post-sale reviews, QBRs, reference calls, win/loss debriefs.
**AVOID WHEN:** The relationship is fragile and the "worst experience" questions would open a wound you cannot close in the same meeting.
**Deal-gate:** motion=expansion/any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the interview protocol the author used for the book's customer-side material.

### [STORY:INTERVIEW-LOGISTICS] Send questions ahead, record, get permission
**Move:** Send questions about a week before so people can think; disclose the recording in that same advance message with an explicit statement of purpose and confidentiality; record with a phone voice-memo app; afterwards let the source review how you captured it and approve its use — verbal internally, written for customers and suppliers.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any interview whose output will be reused publicly or across the team.
**AVOID WHEN:** The story reflects badly on a company or individual — expect approval to stall; anonymize characters, brands and companies instead.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — author reports learning the permission problem the hard way, with external-relations departments creating stress and risk for the interviewee.

### [STORY:PROCUREMENT-FEEDBACK] Use your own procurement department as a coach
**Move:** Find the buyers inside your own company, build a relationship with them, tell them your sales stories for critique, and ask for the best and worst sales stories they have ever been told.
**APPLY WHEN:** You have no access to buyer-side feedback and your win/loss data does not explain why pitches land or die.
**AVOID WHEN:** Treated as a substitute for testing on real prospects — internal procurement buys different categories than your buyers do.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise

**Practice, storage, rollout**

### [STORY:NO-VERBATIM-SCRIPT] Never write the story out word for word
**Move:** Capture stories only as bullets and key phrases in the structure template so the wording is improvised at delivery. If memorizing helps confidence, memorize only the hook, the closing transition into the lesson, and possibly the first line of challenge, conflict or resolution — and err toward less.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any story intended for live oral delivery in a call or meeting.
**AVOID WHEN:** The story must go out as written marketing copy, a case study, or a shared asset for other reps — those need full text.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any

### [STORY:NO-MIRROR] Drop mirror practice
**Move:** Stop rehearsing in front of a mirror; practice conditions should match delivery conditions, and you are the one thing never in front of you when telling a buyer a story.
**APPLY WHEN:** Building a personal practice routine, especially if the default advice you were given was mirror work.
**AVOID WHEN:** It is already working for you — the author explicitly leaves that case alone.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any

### [STORY:WALK-AND-TALK] Practice by walking and talking to an imaginary buyer
**Move:** Find a private space, stand up, walk in a circle, imagine strolling with the buyer talking business, and tell the story out loud. Home substitutes: shower, basement, backyard.
**APPLY WHEN:** You want delivery to land as conversational and have no live listener available.
**AVOID WHEN:** No contraindication given; the only named barrier is embarrassment.

## Where this book stops working

- **Discovery is the wrong place for most of this.** The strongest practitioner counter-signal against the book is that on discovery, questions beat stories: a story risks converting the seller into the talker exactly when listening is the job. Only STORYQ, SILENCE, THREEASKS, OFFICEOBJ, GOFIRST and POST-STORY-SILENCE survive that objection cleanly; GOFIRST is licensed only if immediately followed by shutting up.
- **Micro-ACV, high-velocity transactional motions.** Most of the craft chain (template audit, emotion mapping, three length versions, recorded iteration) costs prep hours that a sub-$1K, minutes-long cycle cannot amortize. MINI-STORY and TWO-ROADS are the only pieces gated to micro/low ACV.
- **Procurement-run, compliance-heavy processes.** Formal RFPs, reverse auctions, regulated bids and scored evaluations reward the exact artifacts the book deprioritizes — credentials, designations, cross-checkable numbers. DIFFSTORY, ACCURACY-NOT-PRECISION and FOUNDING carry explicit exclusions for that world.
- **Product-led / self-serve and channel motions.** Almost every move assumes a human in a conversation with airtime. There is no guidance for a page, a trial, or a partner selling on your behalf, beyond the thin WRITE-AS-SPEECH bridge.
- **Committee-first enterprise deals** get partial coverage only: SPONSOR-FEELING-STORY is the single move addressing rooms you cannot enter, and it depends entirely on an existing champion.
- **Latent, unbounded story budgets.** The wish list quietly assumes more stories is always better; it never asks what the marginal story displaces in a fixed-length call.
- **2016 tooling.** Named apps and platforms (Refresh, Evernote, Zahmoo, Imastory, SharperAx) are period examples, not live recommendations.

## What the book debunks

- **"Any meaningful set of words is a story."** Strategy docs, mission statements, logos and message tracks are excluded. Testimonials and capability statements are useful but non-stories; treating them as stories is the common category error.
- **"Passing the story test means it's good."** Bad stories pass too; and a well-built pitch can fail four of six markers and still work — the book does not claim pitches stop working.
- **"Any motivational story helps."** Directly contradicted: the control group and the self-benefit group showed no significant change; only benefit-to-others moved output.
- **"Brevity always wins."** Ten seconds of jargon loses to forty seconds of conversational narrative — the author's judgment, not a test.
- **"Ask them for a story."** Reliably fails: people encode experience as things that happened, not as stories, and the request triggers performance anxiety. The one exception is salespeople. "Tell me your best story" is also too ambiguous (funniest? most effective? favourite?).
- **"Ask questions and discovery takes care of itself."** Closed questions produce one-word answers and a follow-up chain that never reaches narrative.
- **"State the trait and it transfers."** "I'll always tell you when I'm not the best option" and advertised credentials are low-credibility substitutes for an enacted past case. Confidently answering yes to every hard question can *reduce* credibility — the buyer infers you did not understand.
- **"Rebut the competitor's smear directly."** Backfires twice: it sounds desperate and seeds the idea in listeners who had not heard it.
- **"Justify price by explaining your costs."** Rent, equipment and needing to be paid up front explain your need, not the buyer's gain, and reportedly do not move the objection.
- **"Restate the benefits when they say not now."** Dead weight; the leverage is the problem created by waiting.
- **"We're flexible, anything you want."** Inert. A single concrete past accommodation tells the customer what to ask for.
- **"Lead with the conclusion."** Inverted pyramid, memo structure and grade-school speech advice are named as wrong for stories. Announcing a story ("let me tell you a story", "it was a warm September morning") produces eye-rolls.
- **"Use all five senses, signal the season."** Fiction advice that damages sales stories — irrelevant scene-setting announces a performance.
- **"Delivery polish is the skill."** Ranked far below selection and construction; performance that upstages the pitch counts as failure even if enjoyed. Memorization is a failure mode, not preparation.
- **"Emotion clouds judgment."** Rejected via Damasio: reasoning with emotion stripped out is worse.
- **"Only exaggeration is dangerous."** Under-telling is a real failure: hedging every detail strips interest or buries the story in qualifiers.
- **"Never let the truth get in the way of a good story."** Flagged as convenient cover; the stated cost of drift is misleading the audience and permanently damaging the relationship.
- **Documented failure modes:** an unrehearsed live demonstration in front of a buyer that produced the opposite proof and killed the deal for over a year (lesson: never run a tactic whose outcome you cannot predict); the "if I could show you a way to save X, would you buy today" opener, reported as universally repellent to procurement; canned company-background slides; manufactured personal connection, which is detected; a mismatched feeling-story hero (wealthy suburban district told to a poor rural one); a predictable "surprise"; the sob-story sale that distracts from quality and price criteria the seller knows the product fails; heavy background music on video stories; performing the emotion; "everything got better after I arrived" stories, rated worse than Superman stories; memory drift, the telephone game where all the children are you.

## Numbers that matter

- **Author's corpus (self-reported):** 250+ in-depth one-on-one interviews over six years, 20 countries, 2,000+ documented personal stories; for this book, sales and procurement professionals from 50 organizations; plus 7 years on P&G global sales teams. Part I covers 24 sales stories while Appendix A is titled "25 Stories Salespeople Need" (unreconciled in the text).
- **Trust:** 1999 New York Times/CBS survey — people in general judged ~30% trustworthy, people you know ~70% (the survey does not measure storytelling; the bridge is the author's inference). Bridge Ratings 2007, 3,400 respondents, 1–10 scale: family and friends 8.6, journalists 6.1, advertising 2.2, telemarketers 1.8 (commercial survey, a decade older than the book).
- **Recall:** Chip Heath classroom exercise — average speech used 2.5 statistics, ~1 in 10 students told a story; 63% remembered story details, 5% remembered any individual statistic.
- **Value lift:** Walker & Glenn (July 2009) — 100 thrift objects averaging ~$1.29, total cost $128.74, resold on eBay with fictional stories for $3,612.51 within five months, ~2,800% increase. The author hedges that part of the gain may be listing effects.
- **Seller preparedness:** Forrester 2013, 319 executive-level buyers (North America and Europe) — sellers knowledgeable about their own company/products 62% of the time, about the buyer's industry 42%, carrying relevant examples or case studies only 21%, the lowest item in the survey.
- **Motivation:** Grant (2008) — three random groups, ten minutes of reading before a shift on two consecutive nights; the benefit-to-others group more than doubled both pledge count and money raised versus their own prior week; control and benefit-to-me groups showed no significant change. Fundraisers, not B2B sellers.
- **Length (author's own corpora, self-reported):** ~30 sales stories in this book — median ~280 words, one SD spanning 140–420, average just under 2 minutes, most 1–3. His earlier 112 leadership stories — median just over 600 words, most 450–750, ~4 minutes. Shortest story in the book: 70 words. Average adult speech ~150 words per minute (National Center for Voice and Speech). Worked example proportions: a ~300-word story with ~180 words of conflict (~60%).
- **Readability:** written Febreze version 20 words/sentence, 17% long words, Flesch-Kincaid 13; spoken version 12 words/sentence, 8%, FK 6; recommended hybrid 15 words/sentence, FK 8. Reference points: NYT/WSJ FK 8–10; Grisham and Clancy ~7–8; above 15 usually needs a second read; above 20 reads as a run-on. Business writing 8–10, stories 7–8. Final reworked case story ~480 words, ~3 minutes, FK 7. A salesperson normally uses ~5–6 filler words per minute.
- **Structures surveyed:** 3-part (Aristotle), 5-act (Freytag), 2-step conflict-resolution, 15-step screenwriting, 17-stage Hero's Journey, plus "more than a dozen" others; the adopted model is 7 steps, 4 story sections. Nine relevance questions. Three emotion rungs, three audience positions, three surprise placements, three exit options.
- **Sourcing and practice:** 14 named sourcing channels; goal of 1 interview per week; weekly one-hour story circles; monthly junior/senior buddy meetings; questions sent ~1 week ahead; Backroads picks 3 winning customer submissions a year with a $5,000 travel credit each. Practice ratios (one expert's estimate, self-reported): Olympic swimmer >1,000 hours per competition hour, professional footballer 200:1, professional musician 3–4:1, typical salesperson 0–5 minutes per sales-call hour. SharperAx (vendor-reported): usable version by the 5th–6th take, ~30 minutes to perfect a story, 30–90 minutes of practice per week.

In the Iceland illustration, 180 executives drifting to 360 or 48 sleepless hours to 24 does not damage the story because the fact is not load-bearing.

## Blind spots of this book

- **No trials, anywhere.** The strongest token available is practitioner-n-many. The only randomized study cited (Grant 2008) measured fundraisers reading someone else's story, not sellers telling one, and it supports a single pre-call move. Several numbers come from interested parties — SharperAx take counts and practice ratios are vendor-reported.
- **No counterfactual and no denominator.** Every success story is a story about a story that worked; the interview corpus cannot show how often the same move failed, and frequencies are never reported even for the "recurring answer" claims.
- **Selection bias by construction.** Stories that closed deals are what interviewees remember and volunteer. The author names the mirror problem for sellers and partly fixes it by interviewing procurement, but never separates "buyers recall this story" from "this story changed the award".
- **Silent on opportunity cost.** No move asks what a two-minute story displaces in a 30-minute call, and no guidance exists for how many stories one meeting can absorb. The wish list points one way only: more.
- **Discovery/monologue tension is never resolved.** The book's own structure chapters make the seller the talker for the story's duration, and the one move that returns the floor is rated the hardest to execute. The closing chapter does not address it at all.
- **Craft guidance is stage-blind.** Everything from Ch. 13 on assumes the story is already the right move at that moment; nothing tells you when not to tell one.
- **Practice is contested and left unresolved** — one practitioner rejects rehearsal outright as making stories dry, another works hard to sound natural, and the author reports both without adjudicating.
- **Almost entirely US/Anglophone B2B.** Cross-cultural material is two anecdotes (Latin America rapport length, a Paris audience rejecting an American video) presented as speculation about intimacy thresholds.
- **No pricing, no channel, no product-led motion, no digital-first buyer journey.** The buyer is always a person in a room or on a phone.
- **The manipulation limiters are self-administered.** MANIP-CHECK, WITNESS-TEST and GODIN-BUYER-TEST all ask the seller to judge their own honesty, with no external check and no consequence structure — and they sit in chapters most readers reach last.
