# What Great Salespeople Do — digest

## What this book is for the lens

The authors built the dominant question-based B2B methodologies (Solution Selling, CustomerCentric Selling) and then repudiated their own founding assumption: a scripted diagnostic sequence is not what moves a buyer. Selling is reframed as influencing a person to change, and the operative instrument is a short, true, first-person story containing a real mistake — told before any questioning, followed by silence that hands the floor back. Where other sources give the lens question frameworks, qualification rules and value math, this book supplies the missing layer beneath them: how a seller earns the right to be told the truth in the first place, and a concrete construction method (five cards, one point, one emotion word per beat) that turns "be authentic" into a repeatable build. It also supplies the honest counterweight — the same disclosure mechanics manufacture false intimacy just as easily, and the book never resolves that.

Evidence ceiling: practitioner-n-many. Nothing here is measured against a control; the largest artefact is a workshop practice run across many attendees. Every neuroscience explanation in the book (mirror neurons, left/right brain, limbic-first decision-making, the 7/38/55 split) is pop-science packaging — carry the behaviour, drop the mechanism.

## Core model

**The arc of change.** Story is defined (via McKee) as how and why life changes: balance → disruption → restoration. Buying requires the buyer to change, and people rationalize the status quo until pressure accumulates past a peak (the sinking-ship pattern: passengers invent reasons the ship may stop sinking because the lifeboat is unknown). A story mirrors that arc, so it can carry a buyer through it without demanding a decision.

**Five elements, built backward.** Point (the belief, per Sinek's Golden Circle) → Resolution (the after) → Setting (the before: people, stakes) → Complication (the struggle, ideally internal — the workshop term is the "dumbass moment") → Turning point (the decision that flipped the expected outcome). Five colored 3×5 cards: yellow, red, green, white, blue. Keywords only, handwritten, never a script. Then one circled emotion word per card except yellow — that second layer, not the plot, is what the listener actually receives.

**Three baseline stories, one ladder.** Who I Am, Who I Represent, Who I've Helped. Map the buyer's real buy cycle (Ch. 6), treat each gate as a rung, decide the single belief needed at that rung, stock one story per rung, and climb rung to rung rather than aiming at the close.

**Going first (Ch. 3).** Buyers must admit imperfection to buy, and will not admit it to a seller who performs perfection. So the seller discloses first — a real mistake, a real limit — which licenses reciprocal disclosure. The book's own instrumental example (the LAPD negotiator manufacturing similarity) is the ethical fault line it never closes.

**Story tending (Ch. 8).** The replacement for pain diagnosis. Awareness (register nonverbal signal), Encouragement (short prompts, silence, leaning in), Reflection (play the story back with its emotions and ask whether you got the person). Challenges arrive as complications inside the buyer's own arc, never as answers to a pain question.

**Shifting the herd (Ch. 9).** Explicit retraction of the authors' own "call high, stay high" rule. Enterprises change bottom-up, tribal leader to tribal leader, until the C level ratifies what already happened. The transport mechanism for a story the buyer cannot yet retell is a recap email written in the five elements and sent back for correction — a forwardable script plus a free qualification signal.

**Organization (Ch. 10–11).** The founder story is the company's DNA; enablement should model the selling motion rather than transmit slides; the weekly pipeline interrogation is replaced by a story round. Adoption comes from one supervised live trial, not from argument, and discomfort is pre-framed as the learning zone.

## Moves

### Stage 0 — Posture before any call

### [STORY:REDEFINE-SELL] Restate the call objective as a change decision
**Move:** In call planning, replace "what problem do I solve and how do I prove it" with "what change is this person deciding whether to make, and what would make that change feel safe." Plan the emotional prerequisite — trust, similarity — as its own step rather than as warm-up.
**APPLY WHEN:** buyer is nominally interested but keeps deferring; the status quo, not a competitor, is the real opponent.
**AVOID WHEN:** transactional reorder or renewal where the change decision is already made and the work is logistics.

**Evidence:** assertion — the book's redefinition, synthesized from outside disciplines, no sales-outcome data.

### [STORY:DROP-PERFECT] Stop performing the all-answers expert
**Move:** Keep the domain expertise but drop the posture of having every answer; say plainly when you do not know, and answer from having been there rather than from authority.
**APPLY WHEN:** the buyer is generally skeptical of vendors; you catch yourself rehearsing to appear flawless before a high-stakes meeting.
**AVOID WHEN:** it becomes a substitute for preparation — the book insists the seller still knows product, market, pricing and process cold; "I don't know" about basics reads as incompetence.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — the therapist Alana and her designer-handbag failure with a shelter client, framed as a personal turning point.

### [STORY:NO-MEMORY-NO-DESIRE] Enter the call without a predetermined outcome
**Move:** Park both the old agenda (what you already believe about this account) and the new one (the deal you want). Commit to what emerges from real contact, let yourself be changed by what the buyer says, and when a question you are about to ask serves your agenda rather than your understanding, do not ask it.
**APPLY WHEN:** discovery and early consultative calls; renewals and expansions where you carry heavy priors; relationship repair after a bad interaction; any call you enter with a forecast number in mind.
**AVOID WHEN:** stages with hard forcing functions — pricing negotiation, mutual close plan, contract deadlines — where dropping the objective simply loses the deal; also note it is a mindset instruction with no verification mechanism, so treat compliance as unmeasurable.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting/saas · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** assertion — imported from a therapy-dialogue model and from maxims attributed to Bion and Shneidman, not research findings.

### [STORY:MISTAKE-LIST] Build a mistake inventory as pre-work
**Move:** Write down three mistakes you made, three your company made, three your clients made, and commit to being willing to tell them to family and friends. Then name one mistake you could never tell anyone, ask why, and ask how you would react if a friend confessed the same thing.
**APPLY WHEN:** preparing a seller for vulnerability-based calls; onboarding a rep who defaults to feature-pitching.
**AVOID WHEN:** the list is treated as a talk track to recite; client mistakes must never be tellable in a form that identifies or shames a real customer.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — a standing workshop exercise.

### [STORY:SLOW-REVEAL] Practise disclosure on a safe listener first
**Move:** Tell someone you care about something you are not proud of, reveal it slowly instead of dumping it, and when they answer with curiosity, deliberately notice the connection that forms.
**APPLY WHEN:** the rep accepts vulnerability intellectually but freezes attempting it with a buyer; the rep lacks vocabulary for describing their own emotional states.
**AVOID WHEN:** the person carries an active shame issue they are not resourced to handle — the book gives no safety guidance and the exercise is not therapy.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — end-of-chapter workshop exercise.

### [STORY:TYPE-A-CURB] Rein in your own tempo
**Move:** Slow down deliberately: no multitasking, no finishing the buyer's sentences, no racing toward the finish line. Match the buyer's pace, especially with a reserved buyer.
**APPLY WHEN:** you are the fast, competitive, answer-for-everything type and the buyer is quiet or deliberate.
**AVOID WHEN:** the buyer is themselves high-tempo and reads slowness as low competence — the book concedes a fast seller can work well with a fast buyer.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** assertion — personality-type characterisation offered without measurement.

### [STORY:DRILL-FIRST] Drill until the mechanics disappear, then go live
**Move:** Rehearse a story until you stop thinking about its structure: first with cards in front of a mirror, then on a non-client, and only then with a buyer. First pass gets the sequence of events right; second pass works the circled emotion words through nonverbal delivery. Do not attempt it from memory without cards until it feels second nature — if you are consciously tracking cards or torch-passing during a live call, you are not present with the buyer.
**APPLY WHEN:** any new story entering your inventory; before a high-stakes first meeting.
**AVOID WHEN:** over-rehearsal turns the story into a recitation — cards are prompts, and delivery that sounds memorized defeats the point.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** assertion — the left/right-brain hand-off mechanism is unsourced; the practice-volume argument leans on Gladwell's 10,000 hours secondhand, with the book itself conceding storytelling needs far less.

### [STORY:LENGTH-FIT] Keep short and long variants of every story
**Move:** Hold a repertoire spanning roughly 30 seconds, 3 minutes, and up to ~10 minutes (the longest only with visuals), and rehearse both a compressed and a developed variant of each story. Cap a first-call story at about three minutes, and read the listener mid-story — attention fading means land the point now, leaning in means develop it.
**APPLY WHEN:** any storytelling moment; especially first calls and time-boxed meetings.
**AVOID WHEN:** you have not actually practised the compressed version — improvised compression tends to drop the point that made the story worth telling.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the authors' delivery experience; the cited illustration is Mark Hurd's ~2-minute story about his daughter.

### Stage 0b — Building the story inventory

### [STORY:POINT-FIRST] Fix the point before building anything
**Move:** Write the single belief you want the listener to hold — a moral-sized statement, e.g. that there is a better way — and discard any story that does not deliver exactly that.
**APPLY WHEN:** building any reusable story; auditing an existing story that rambles.
**AVOID WHEN:** the "point" is a product claim in disguise — a point is a belief, not a feature or a differentiator.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — workshop participants report the pre-set point is what improved their storytelling; the Golden Circle rationale is borrowed framing, not evidence.

### [STORY:CARD-BUILD] Build on five cards in point → resolution → setting → complication → turning-point order
**Move:** Never build chronologically. Yellow = the point. Red = the ending/resolution. Green = working backward from those, where the journey began. White = the struggle, the bumps, the moments you were plainly wrong. Blue = the turning point. Then on every card except yellow write and circle at least one word naming the emotion of that beat. The result follows the arc of change.
**APPLY WHEN:** constructing any story in the inventory; rebuilding a story that rambles or has no payload; team story-building sessions.
**AVOID WHEN:** you cannot name the point first — a story without a defined point should not be told; also skip when the buyer asked directly for data; and do not let the cards become a script to memorize, or the resolution be invented rather than recalled (the method assumes a true event).
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the workshop's core construction drill run across many attendees; the colour-aids-memory claim is the authors' observation.

### [STORY:HANDWRITE] Draft by hand, in keywords
**Move:** Write the cards by hand in bullets and keywords, ignoring grammar and complete sentences, so the editing and spell-checking impulse does not interfere with recall of sensory and emotional detail.
**APPLY WHEN:** first draft of any personal story; reps who freeze trying to phrase things correctly.
**AVOID WHEN:** distributed teams needing shared artifacts — the offered rationale is the hemisphere metaphor, so treat the mechanism as unproven and the practice as an anti-perfectionism device.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** assertion — rests entirely on the book's brain-hemisphere framing.

### [STORY:SETTING-CONTEXT] Set the scene with people and stakes, then cut
**Move:** Open with enough context that the listener knows who these people are and what they stand to lose, rather than starting at the incident; then get to the action fast and keep the setting proportional to total length.
**APPLY WHEN:** any story told live to a prospect; rewriting a story that gets a flat reaction.
**AVOID WHEN:** the setting starts covering everything that happened before — over-context loses the listener before the point arrives, and novel-scale proportions do not transfer to a live conversation.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — taught with paired before/after examples (a service-call opener with and without characters).

### [STORY:WHITE-CARD] Name the obstacle, preferably an internal one
**Move:** On the white card, name the specific obstacle standing between the opening and the climax. Prefer an obstacle inside yourself — temptation, insecurity, a wrong belief — over an external one; if the story is about your firm, name the mistake the firm actually made rather than a heroic effort.
**APPLY WHEN:** building any first-meeting or trust-stage story; the draft reads flat or self-congratulatory.
**AVOID WHEN:** the admitted flaw is the exact risk the buyer is buying insurance against on this deal; regulated or litigated contexts where admitting fault carries legal weight.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the standard card exercise repeated across the authors' workshops.

### [STORY:DUMBASS-BEAT] Keep the beat where you were plainly wrong
**Move:** Locate the moment you were wrong, seduced, or resistant to change — real shame, internal conflict included — and keep it in the told version rather than a struggle that flatters you. The workshop label is the "dumbass moment"; a story without one reads as a brag.
**APPLY WHEN:** early call with a defensive or arms-crossed buyer; building a Who I Am story meant to earn trust; when you need the buyer to admit something embarrassing first.
**AVOID WHEN:** the flaw undermines your competence on the exact thing being bought; a committee scoring you on risk in late-stage procurement; the disclosure exceeds what the business relationship can hold or exposes a third party — the book asserts the payoff without bounding the downside.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — Ben's own crashed sales call and Linda's workshop rewrite; linked by the authors to Goleman's emotional-intelligence framing, which is invoked rather than evidenced.

### [STORY:BLUE-CARD] Test the turning point against the resolution
**Move:** Hunt for the key decision, event or action that flipped the expected outcome — not the moment the problem was fixed. Reaction and resolution belong on the red card.
**APPLY WHEN:** a story reads like a report; listeners nod but nothing lands.
**AVOID WHEN:** no such moment exists — some stories legitimately have none (see NO-RESOLUTION).
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the recurring diagnostic in workshop story-building.

### [STORY:BOARD] Lay the cards out as a physical plot
**Move:** Once all five cards exist, arrange them chronologically as a visible storyboard. With repetition the layout becomes mental — practised tellers assemble the sequence in real time on a call.
**APPLY WHEN:** preparing a repertoire before a cycle starts; coaching a rep whose stories ramble.
**AVOID WHEN:** mid-call improvisation by someone who has never done it on paper.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — workshop participants reportedly internalize the cards after a few stories.

### [STORY:EMO-WORD] One emotion word per card
**Move:** After the cards are ordered, ask of each one how you felt at that point, name one or two emotion words, and write them at the bottom of every card except the yellow point card.
**APPLY WHEN:** any story you intend to tell aloud more than once.
**AVOID WHEN:** the words are manufactured rather than recalled — invented emotion produces performed, not felt, delivery.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the standard second pass of the card exercise.

### [STORY:EMO-SCALE] Encode intensity visually on the card
**Move:** Write strong emotions large or in capitals and weak ones small, circling each — the card is a visual prompt, so scale the emotion visually rather than describing it in prose.
**APPLY WHEN:** rehearsing delivery; stories that read evenly and land flat.
**AVOID WHEN:** written collateral or email adaptations, where card mechanics do not transfer.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** assertion — offered as a memory aid with no support beyond the authors' preference.

### [STORY:NO-RESOLUTION] Keep one story that ends in failure
**Move:** Retain a story with no fix at the end — the absence of resolution is the point, and it maximizes displayed vulnerability.
**APPLY WHEN:** the belief the buyer must reach is that going it alone fails; an arms-crossed audience braced for a chest-beating pitch.
**AVOID WHEN:** the rung requires proof of capability or de-risking — late-stage, committee, procurement scoring.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — Ben's coaching call with Adam and the meeting that followed.

### [STORY:INTERROGATE-DRAFT] Interrogate a weak draft card by card
**Move:** Coach a flat story by asking in order: what is the point, what is the resolution, what is the beginning — then push on the missing middle, asking whether there was a time the teller was not yet the person they claim to be. Keep asking for more until a genuine failure period surfaces.
**APPLY WHEN:** coaching or self-editing; the draft describes traits ("customer advocate") instead of change.
**AVOID WHEN:** public coaching with the teller's boss present and genuinely damaging material surfacing — Linda's account notes she performed partly to avoid looking weak in front of her VP.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — a single reconstructed workshop dialogue.

### [STORY:THREE-BASE] Carry a minimum repertoire of three
**Move:** Keep Who I Am (your journey and why you do this), Who I Represent (your company's journey), and Who I've Helped (a buyer's change after buying from you) available at all times; they cover the curiosity, connection and trust rungs common to both long enterprise cycles and one-call cycles.
**APPLY WHEN:** any first contact, any new territory, any rep ramp.
**AVOID WHEN:** the buyer already knows you and your firm — then a bespoke story built for the specific belief is the right tool.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the workshop's standing baseline across clients.

### [STORY:WHO-I-AM] Build the Who I Am story from your professional journey
**Move:** You are the main character. Point = why you do what you do. Resolution = your current role. Setting = how the journey began (it usually surfaces on its own once point and resolution are fixed). Complication = your struggles and your own mistakes. Turning point = when you decided to become who you are now and what changed your direction. A usable version necessarily admits there was a time you were less capable or less committed than today.
**APPLY WHEN:** opening a first meeting; introducing yourself to a new stakeholder mid-deal.
**AVOID WHEN:** the vulnerable beat is disproportionate to the relationship — a stranger, a hostile committee, a formal panel — or would raise doubt on the exact axis the buyer is evaluating.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=any · market=consultative/stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — Exercise 2, the standard workshop build.

### [STORY:WHO-I-REPRESENT] Company story as why, not timeline
**Move:** Refuse the About Us timeline. Interview senior executives to extract one focused story carrying the company's guiding belief and answering why it exists — for instance an underdog challenging an entrenched incumbent, including the period the company lost its way and had to depend on the very incumbent it set out to beat.
**APPLY WHEN:** enabling a sales force; reps who never bought into corporate messaging; brand-new territory where the firm is unknown.
**AVOID WHEN:** you budget an hour for it — the documented case took nearly a full day with five executives and open conflict.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=product/saas/service · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one 2011 client engagement plus Laura's self-reported reaction.

### [STORY:NARROW-REP] Narrow the company story to one unit and one point
**Move:** Do not attempt a comprehensive corporate history. Pick the business unit or product group you actually sell, pick the one chapter of its history that makes the single point you need, and build only that: point = why the company does what it does; resolution = what it does today; setting = how the journey began; complications = the missteps, told through the founder's or an executive's point of view to keep a human face on it; turning point = the moment it changed direction.
**APPLY WHEN:** you represent a large multi-product vendor; the buyer has no idea which part of your company matters to them.
**AVOID WHEN:** solo operator or single-product company — there Who I Am and Who I Represent collapse into one story.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=product/saas · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the standard workshop exercise, illustrated by the Oracle case.

### [STORY:CO-BUILD-HELPED] Co-build the Who I've Helped story with the client
**Move:** Keep at least one, preferably several. The main character is an individual customer and their personal journey, not their company's: point = the client's belief, possibly one you influenced; resolution = what they can do today; setting = how their journey started, with enough detail that they read as a real person; complication = their struggles the old way; turning point = the moment they switched, with you possibly entering there. Where possible build it with the client — ask them for one emotion word per beat, circle those on the cards, and rehearse by telling it back so they can correct and fill gaps.
**APPLY WHEN:** the buyer needs proof that someone like them changed; reference-hungry mid-funnel stages.
**AVOID WHEN:** you lack the client's permission to narrate their story, or details are identifiable and sensitive; when the buyer wants quantified outcomes rather than narrative.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=low/mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=product/saas/service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — Exercise 4; the co-build step is the authors' standing recommendation.

### [STORY:RIGHT-TOOL] Build the bespoke story the point needs, and go first with it
**Move:** When neither Who I Am nor Who I Represent fits, name the belief the buyer must reach (for example that doing this on the cheap is false economy), then search your own history for a time you made that same mistake and tell that first. Your confession is what earns their version.
**APPLY WHEN:** the buyer has visibly failed at something and is defensive about it; you need them to name the real cause without being told.
**AVOID WHEN:** you have no comparable failure — fabricating one is the obvious misuse mode; also when the buyer's failure is a live internal blame fight.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=low/mid/high · stakeholders=few · offer=service/consulting/saas · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — Adam Luff's single cafeteria meeting that expanded into three opportunities.

### Stage 0c — Delivery craft and post-call diagnosis

### [STORY:WHY-BEFORE-WHAT] Deliver the why as narrative before the what as data
**Move:** Replace a feature or fact block with a short narrative about a specific person in a specific situation, ending on the meaning you drew from it; hand over the supporting figures only afterwards. The bulleted version is what you are replacing, not supplementing.
**APPLY WHEN:** early discovery or a first substantive meeting; the buyer is new to the category; deck-driven meetings where attention visibly drifts.
**AVOID WHEN:** the buyer has explicitly asked for specifications, pricing or a comparison table; late-stage procurement and technical validation, where narrative reads as evasion.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — a classroom analogy (a dates-and-facts slide versus a narrative about Paul Revere) plus the authors' training experience; no measured comparison.

### [STORY:CHECK-IN] Check in with the emotion before and during the telling
**Move:** Recall the emotion word for the card you are on. Naming the state to yourself is claimed to be enough to move tone, rhythm, face and posture; the words themselves are never spoken aloud.
**APPLY WHEN:** any live telling, especially by reps whose delivery is monotone.
**AVOID WHEN:** the recalled emotion is one you cannot regulate in front of this buyer.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the authors' standing workshop instruction; the "research indicates" backing is unnamed, so the mechanism claim is an assertion.

### [STORY:ACT-OUT] Relive the vivid beats instead of narrating them
**Move:** Physically act out the story's vivid moments rather than reporting them; the claim is that the listener follows you in.
**APPLY WHEN:** small-room, in-person tellings; stories whose turning point is a physical or emotional moment.
**AVOID WHEN:** video calls, formal boardrooms, or cross-cultural settings where the performance reads as theatrical or manipulative.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** assertion — stated as technique with no case attached.

### [STORY:EMO-DRILL] Two congruence drills
**Move:** Drill 1 — say that you are fine while sending the opposite signal through tone, face and posture, and observe which channel the partner believes. Drill 2 — act out one card's emotion word and have a partner guess it and say why; then tell a whole built story without using any emotion words and have them name the words.
**APPLY WHEN:** rep enablement, pre-call rehearsal, coaching delivery rather than content.
**AVOID WHEN:** Drill 1 is run on an unwitting colleague — that is mild deception; keep it inside a training pair.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — printed as standard workshop exercises.

### [STORY:METAPHOR] Use metaphor for intangibles
**Move:** Replace abstract states with a concrete image — a deadline as a tightening noose, a dark cloud, a truck bearing down. Express the unfamiliar through the familiar; similes are the same tool with an explicit comparison.
**APPLY WHEN:** describing pressure, risk or feeling — the parts of a story that otherwise become adjectives.
**AVOID WHEN:** technical or contractual precision is at stake; mixed or overreaching metaphors force the listener to decode and defeat the purpose.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — a standard toolbox item, illustrated rather than tested.

### [STORY:TEND-DRILL] Rehearse story-tending off-target before using it on a buyer
**Move:** Tell a friend a story about your day, then pass the torch by asking about theirs. Then: give undivided attention; encourage nonverbally and with two-word prompts; ask for clarification when something is unclear; watch nonverbal cues for what is communicated but not said and follow those crumbs; probe the emotion under the words with a tentative label and a question about where it comes from. Close by reflecting the whole story back in your own words, asking whether you got them, and stating your own emotional reaction.
**APPLY WHEN:** building the skill; also the live pattern for the listening half of any discovery call.
**AVOID WHEN:** emotion-probing on a buyer who has signalled they want a transactional exchange — it reads as intrusive; and using the "do I get you" check to fake alignment on facts you have not understood is the misuse mode.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — Exercise 5, the workshop's empathic-listening drill.

### [STORY:STAGE-CHECK] Diagnose which stage a told story reached
**Move:** After the call, locate where the story stalled against Sturm's seven stages — did you stay outside it and never get absorbed, did the buyer never begin reliving it, did you fail to land an ending? Rebuild the weak element instead of adding more stories.
**APPLY WHEN:** deal reviews and call coaching; a rep reports that they told the story and nothing happened.
**AVOID WHEN:** used as a scoring rubric — the stages are descriptive and no measurement method is offered.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative
**Evidence:** assertion — a named academic's descriptive model, presented without validation data.

### Stage 1 — Prospecting and the first meeting

### [STORY:LEAD-WHY] Open with the belief, not the outcome
**Move:** In the first seconds of contact, state the belief that is your story's point rather than the result you produced. The contrast the book draws: claiming you helped a named client lift revenue by 30 percent sounds like every other seller, while naming the belief that drove him — wanting real transformation across his organization — earns the next two minutes.
**APPLY WHEN:** cold or warm outreach, trade shows, any first contact where you have seconds.
**AVOID WHEN:** the buyer explicitly asked for numbers or is already deep in evaluation — a belief-first opener there reads as evasive.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/inbound · acv=any · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=any · market=transactional/consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the authors' repeated prospecting practice; the claim that very few people turn them down is unquantified.

### [STORY:STORY-OFFER-COLD] Offer a story to a stranger at first contact
**Move:** Introduce yourself, acknowledge that you have not met, and ask whether you may share a story about someone comparable to them. If they accept, tell a two-minute version.
**APPLY WHEN:** cold approach at events; hostile or bored openings where a pitch would be refused outright.
**AVOID WHEN:** time-boxed formal agenda slots where the ask itself reads as stalling; a buyer already annoyed by an earlier failed pitch.

### [STORY:DETHREAT-ROLE] Remove the seller signal from your role framing

### [STORY:ASK-PERMISSION] Ask permission to tell a story

### [STORY:CALL-SEQ] Run the four-beat sequence on one call

### [STORY:MISTAKE-STORY] Open with a story in which you were the one who erred

### [STORY:PASS-TORCH] Pass the torch after every story

### Stage 2 — Discovery, listening and story tending

### [STORY:ABORT-INTERROGATION] Kill the question sequence at the first irritation signal
**Move:** Watch for the shutdown cluster — answers shrinking to a word, arms crossed, attention on a device, no eye contact, or the buyer starting to question you. On that signal stop the diagnostic sequence outright instead of pushing the remaining questions, and switch to a short first-person story.
**APPLY WHEN:** first or second meeting; a senior buyer who has sat through many vendor pitches; you are visibly in an interview dynamic.
**AVOID WHEN:** the buyer is engaged and expanding on answers; regulated or technical qualification where the questions are compliance requirements rather than persuasion.

### [STORY:WHOLE-STORY-INVITE] Answer "how can you help?" by asking for their whole story
**Move:** When the buyer finally hands you the floor, decline the opening to present. Say you think you could help but would understand their position far better if they told you the whole story — then let them run, prompting only with short questions about what you just heard.
**APPLY WHEN:** first substantive call; the buyer has lectured or stated a strong position and you have not heard how they arrived at it.
**AVOID WHEN:** late-stage calls where the story has already been told and specifics are wanted; short transactional calls with no time budget; buyers who read open-ended invitations as dodging a direct question.

### [STORY:ARC-NOT-PAIN] Collect challenges as story complications, not as elicited pains
**Move:** Never ask a buyer to enumerate pains or challenges. Get the full arc — past, present, desired future — through storytelling plus torch-passing, and take the challenges from the complications inside it. Then use your stories to help them build the one part of the arc they cannot supply: the resolution, with you in it.
**APPLY WHEN:** discovery in any consultative deal, especially where competing vendors have already run diagnostics on this buyer.
**AVOID WHEN:** structured qualification is contractually required; the buyer is a repeat or expansion customer who prefers a direct checklist.

### [STORY:GO-FIRST-DISCLOSE] Go first with your own failure to unlock theirs
**Move:** Before asking a buyer to admit a problem, name something imperfect on your side — your mistake, your company's, or a limit of your knowledge — and let it sit without pivoting to a pitch. When the deal is stuck because the buyer will not admit a problem exists, tell the story of the same mistake and what it cost you, then stop and pass the torch. **APPLY WHEN:** early-stage call where the buyer must concede the status quo is broken; discovery producing polite non-answers; the offering is new to them; you need acknowledgment rather than information.

### [STORY:QUEST-FROM-HEAR] Build every question from what you just heard

### [STORY:EYE-LISTEN] Listen with your eyes, not only your ears

### [STORY:WHOLE-BRAIN-LISTEN] Synthesize what is said, how it is said, and what is not said

### [STORY:CUE-READ] Read posture and hand-to-face cues as prompts

**APPLY WHEN:** in-person meetings, demos, committee rooms where verbal agreement may be polite cover.
**AVOID WHEN:** phone or async channels; and whenever you would act on a cue as fact — this vocabulary is folk body-language and its supporting rationale is unsourced.

### [STORY:NONVERB-Q] Encourage nonverbally: attention, lean, pause
**Move:** Use three wordless prompts — attentive nodding with steady eye contact, leaning toward the speaker, and deliberate silence after they stop. People fill pauses, and a pause held with relaxed body language invites the next thread instead of closing one.
**APPLY WHEN:** the buyer has started opening up and a verbal question would break the flow; after an emotionally loaded disclosure.
**AVOID WHEN:** the pause would read as a pressure tactic — for instance after a price statement in a negotiation, where silence is a recognised squeeze.

### [STORY:SHORT-Q] Keep verbal prompts extremely short
**Move:** Prefer two-to-four-word prompts — then what, why, tell me more about that — because short questions feel spontaneous while long ones feel rehearsed. For genuine confusion, ask plainly for help understanding or ask them to go back to a point.
**APPLY WHEN:** the buyer's narrative is moving and you only need to keep it moving.
**AVOID WHEN:** a bare "why" would sound challenging given tone or hierarchy — then use the longer clarification form.

### [STORY:BREADCRUMB] Follow the crumb, not the stated topic
**Move:** Treat every anomaly — an object thrown, a shift in tone, crumpled papers, a change in eye contact — as a crumb and ask about the crumb instead of the stated subject. Name what you observed neutrally, offer a tentative emotion label, and let the person correct or escalate it until the real story surfaces.
**APPLY WHEN:** the objection is disproportionate to its stated cause; a champion suddenly goes cold; a meeting turns hostile over a minor issue.
**AVOID WHEN:** you have no relationship yet and the observation would feel invasive; or when you would use the surfaced emotion as leverage rather than to understand.

### [STORY:EMO-LADDER] Three-step emotion sequence, phrased as observations
**Move:** In order: offer a tentative label and invite correction; ask how much of that feeling there is, to get degree; then ask where it comes from, framed as a request for permission to understand. Use observations that invite an answer rather than direct questions, to reduce the interrogation feel.
**APPLY WHEN:** the buyer has surfaced something emotionally loaded but is speaking in flat business language; business contexts where a direct feelings question would jar.
**AVOID WHEN:** the buyer has shown no signal of wanting to go there — having one's story tended is unsettling for people unused to it or lacking emotional vocabulary; also avoid running all three steps mechanically, which recreates the scripted-sequence failure the chapter condemns.

**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the authors' taught sequence; no outcome data.

### [STORY:REFLECT-BACK] Reflect the story back with its emotions and check whether you got them
**Move:** Synthesize verbal and nonverbal input and paraphrase it back, including the emotions rather than only the events, so the buyer's information becomes narrative-shaped. Close the reflection with a direct check that you understood them, and add an honest personal connection line where one exists.
**APPLY WHEN:** the end of any substantial discovery block; before moving to the next story or stage; whenever you need the whole story rather than a fragment.
**AVOID WHEN:** your paraphrase would be a covert reframe toward your product — the buyer hears the tilt and the move converts into the persuasion attempt it was meant to replace; also skip the personal-connection line if the feeling is manufactured.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=solo/few/committee · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise

### [STORY:NO-LABEL] Suspend the instant label on a hostile buyer
**Move:** When a buyer behaves hostilely — refuses a handshake, calls salespeople liars — consciously withhold the write-off label and keep gathering the story, assuming an off-screen cause exists: a parent in hospital, three sleepless nights, two prior vendors who over-promised.

### [STORY:NO-PREMATURE] Withhold the perfect solution

Earn the right to prescribe by first being validated as having understood.
**APPLY WHEN:** you are the domain expert; the buyer's first sentences already map to a known pattern; you feel excitement about the fit or the commission.
**AVOID WHEN:** the buyer is late-cycle and explicitly asking for a recommendation — withholding then reads as stalling.

### [STORY:TEND-STORY] Tend the buyer's story instead of questioning it
**Move:** As listener, slow the exchange down, respond with sincere curiosity that names the emotion you heard and asks for more, and stay in that role instead of redirecting to your agenda. The drill runs in threes: one narrates below normal pace, one tends, one observes.
**APPLY WHEN:** the buyer begins telling you something personal or a difficult account of their situation.
**AVOID WHEN:** performed as a listening technique rather than genuine attention — the observed effect depended on sincerity; it can also surface far more emotion than a business setting can hold (workshop participants wept).

### [STORY:BUYER-AUTHORITY] Treat "you don't understand" as fact
**Move:** When the buyer says you have missed the point, accept it literally instead of re-explaining; treat their criticism as a possibly accurate read of your blind spot and say so.
**APPLY WHEN:** the buyer pushes back, corrects you, or goes flat after your framing; a champion tells you the message is not landing.
**AVOID WHEN:** used as blanket capitulation on factual matters — pricing, security, technical limits — where deferring misleads them.

### [STORY:INFO-TO-STORY] Use what you learn to pick the next story, not to pitch
**Move:** Treat everything the buyer discloses as input for selecting your next story rather than ammunition for a persuasion attempt. Keep the call alternating between stories until the buyer has told you their full situation.
**APPLY WHEN:** discovery is going well and the temptation to show them how you solve it appears.
**AVOID WHEN:** the buyer has moved into evaluation or procurement and is explicitly requesting specifics — trading stories then reads as evasion.

### [STORY:COOLDOWN-CUE] Keep a visible cue that interrupts your own defensiveness
**Move:** Keep a written reminder of the behaviour you intend to run in your line of sight. When you notice yourself getting defensive or wanting to rush to the finish, look at it and switch back to listening instead of arguing.
**APPLY WHEN:** the buyer's opening feels confrontational; you catch yourself thinking they do not get it; anxiety is pushing you to compress the call.
**AVOID WHEN:** the cue substitutes for preparation, or the resistance is substantive rather than emotional — a real objection needs an answer, not just calmer listening.

### [STORY:DECK-BREAK] Interrupt your own deck with a short personal story
**Move:** Mid-presentation, stop the slide sequence and tell a brief first-person story of thirty to sixty seconds from an everyday setting that carries the same point, then return to the material.
**APPLY WHEN:** you can see the room disengaging — phones out, side conversations; executive audiences receiving a market or positioning monologue.
**AVOID WHEN:** the story does not carry the argument and is decoration; audiences who explicitly asked for data and will read an anecdote as evasion.

### [STORY:ELICIT] Ask one specific personal question that invites their story
**Move:** When the counterpart is stiff and reading from prepared material, interrupt with a concrete, personal, non-business question about something you already know a little about, and let them narrate.
**APPLY WHEN:** formal first meetings, scripted executive briefings, high-status counterparts who default to talking points.
**AVOID WHEN:** the personal detail is sensitive or sourced from surveillance-grade research; the book's own example (Bush's memoir account of Putin and the cross) carries the caution that the resulting warmth may have been the other side's tactic rather than genuine connection.

### [STORY:HONEST-UNKNOWN] Answer a capability question with the truth plus a partnership commitment
**Move:** When asked whether you can do something you have not done, say plainly that you do not know and have never done it, then immediately commit to being in it jointly with the buyer. Do not hedge into a qualified yes.
**APPLY WHEN:** the buyer raises the capability question themselves late in a connected conversation and you have standing to commit — founder, executive, owner of delivery.

### Stage 3 — Building belief across the cycle and across the account

### [STORY:MAP-BUY-CYCLE] Map the buyer's actual gates, then check your process against them
**Move:** Write the real sequence of gates the buyer passes before signing — in the book's enterprise example: curious about a new approach, expresses needs, willing to brainstorm with someone trusted, sees the new way, gets a committee to consider change, sees value against the cost of the status quo, sees an implementation path, commits. Condense into stages, compare with your current sales process, and fix the mismatch. Complexity, deal size and number of decision-makers change the number of gates, not the three underlying phases.
**APPLY WHEN:** building a repeatable motion; onboarding reps; diagnosing where deals stall or die at a particular stage.
**AVOID WHEN:** treating the sequence as universal boilerplate — the book supplies two very different real cycles (eight rungs versus four); and when the cycle is genuinely unknown (new category, first ten customers), build it from interviews rather than inventing rungs.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=saas/product/service · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — cycles built with two real firms and used as Exercise 1 in every workshop, framed on Rackham's buyer-behaviour research.

### [STORY:LADDER-RUNG-STORY] One point, one story, per rung
**Move:** For each rung ask what point must be made there, and build a story whose point delivers exactly that belief. A needs-rung story is designed to make the buyer volunteer needs; a solved-problem story is designed to make them discuss the right solution. Fly rung to rung rather than aiming straight at the close.
**APPLY WHEN:** multi-step cycles where reps skip to the product pitch and lose the thread.
**AVOID WHEN:** one-call transactional sales collapse the ladder — do not manufacture eight rungs where four exist.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=low/mid/high · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — client-built ladders in both B2B and B2C engagements.

### [STORY:WHO-IVE-HELPED] Bridge with a third-party arc instead of a value pitch
**Move:** Once the problem is acknowledged, bridge by saying their situation reminds you of another buyer in the same role, and tell that arc: an experienced person tried to handle it alone, hit a concrete failure event, brought you in narrowly, and the narrow win expanded. Keep their initial skepticism in rather than smoothing it out; the point is why that buyer chose you, and the resolution is how their complication got resolved. Hand back with a low-pressure closer.
**APPLY WHEN:** the needs and evaluate-options rungs; the buyer has admitted a problem but doubts it is solvable; the buyer wants proof but resists case-study decks; whenever you would otherwise reach for the deck or demo.
**AVOID WHEN:** the reference is not genuinely role-matched or comparable, or the outcome numbers cannot survive scrutiny; the buyer explicitly asked for product specifics and is in evaluation mode.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=saas/service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — the Adam narrative and the Clear Technologies account, self-reported by the client; no comparative win-rate data.

### [STORY:RECAP-EMAIL] Write the prospect's story back and ask for corrections
**Move:** Close the call by saying you will send a summary so you can confirm you understood who they are. Then write their story, not your pitch, using the five elements: point (the belief or goal the tribe forms around), setting (how the journey started, characters, emotions), complication (challenges, impact, emotions), turning point (why act now), resolution (the future state where your offering addresses the challenges). End by asking whether you understood correctly, before proposing the next meeting. The artifact is a forwardable script your contact can send upward safely.
**APPLY WHEN:** after a call where the buyer opened up; multi-silo deals where your contact must persuade others; you want a low-friction next step that also qualifies.
**AVOID WHEN:** you did not actually get the story — a generic or fabricated recap exposes that you were not listening; the disclosures were confidential or politically sensitive and would damage her if forwarded; heavily regulated or written-record-averse accounts.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/inbound · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=saas/service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — standard Story Leaders practice with a full worked example letter and six recurring benefits observed in enterprise cycles.

### [STORY:REPLY-TEMPERATURE] Qualify on the emotional register of the reply
**Move:** Read the answer to the recap email for register, not just content. A short, formal, we-will-review-when-time-permits reply signals no connection; a reply that volunteers the buyer's own beliefs, agrees with your framing and asks what is next signals a real one. Use the difference to decide where company resources go.
**APPLY WHEN:** pipeline triage; deciding which accounts get presales or executive time.
**AVOID WHEN:** cultural or seniority norms make terse email normal; the contact is simply a low-writing-volume buyer; treating one cold reply as a verdict on an otherwise engaged account.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — two contrasting sample replies to the same letter, presented illustratively.

### [STORY:SILO-CRUMBS] Write the other silos' complications into the reflected story
**Move:** In a committee sale touching engineering, operations, finance, order processing, marketing and so on, treat each silo as a separate tribe. Ask your tribal leader who else must get on board, then write those silos' complications and benefits into the story you send back so the document travels with the broadest appeal — leaning on the insider's knowledge of internal politics and of who the other tribal leaders are.
**APPLY WHEN:** complex B2B purchase with cross-functional impact; the decision process is opaque from outside.
**AVOID WHEN:** single-silo purchase; or when broadening the story so far that it stops being your contact's own story and loses its emotional grip.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/expansion · acv=high · stakeholders=committee · offer=saas/service/consulting · market=enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — standing guidance for complex sales in the authors' workshops.

### [STORY:TRIBAL-LEADER-HUNT] Qualify on the ability to move an idea, not on budget authority
**Move:** Screen each contact on three tests — will this person hear your story, do they think the current way is worth changing, and will they carry that belief to other groups and ask others to follow. Instead of asking whether you have access to power, ask who inside the account knows how to advance a new idea. Invest in whoever passes, even several levels below an executive you could reach directly.
**APPLY WHEN:** enterprise or multi-silo deal; disruptive offering; you have an entry point but no access to power; you already burned a top-down attempt and got routed to a dead end.
**AVOID WHEN:** procurement-driven or formally gated purchases where an unsponsored bottom-up motion is disqualified; small solo-buyer deals with no herd to shift; a sales cycle shorter than the time a grassroots movement needs; a low-level contact with no peer network; deal size or disruption that genuinely requires an executive signature early.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/channel/expansion · acv=high · stakeholders=committee · offer=saas/service/consulting · market=enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — repeated in the authors' workshops as revised advice, illustrated by one contrast case (a cold call to IBM's North American president went nowhere while an affiliate's midlevel sales director grew into a multimillion-dollar license).

### [STORY:NO-STEPPING-STONE] Do not ask your champion for access to the decision maker
**Move:** Avoid openly using a lower-level contact as a route to the signer; the stated risk is that they conclude they are only a stepping stone and stop advocating. Ask instead whether they are in a tribe and whether they lead one, and work on making them the owner of the story.
**APPLY WHEN:** you have engaged a midlevel champion who is beginning to connect you to peers.
**AVOID WHEN:** the buying process formally requires you to identify and meet the approver; the champion has explicitly offered the introduction; deadline pressure makes an indirect route unaffordable.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/expansion · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=enterprise
**Evidence:** assertion — stated as a mechanism with an imagined internal monologue, no case or count.

### [STORY:ENTRY-ALTITUDE] Set entry altitude from three variables
**Move:** Decide how high you need to connect from the size of the potential transaction, how disruptive the offering is to the organization, and how many people it affects — rather than defaulting to the C suite. Read late executive interest as a warning: if the executive is already moving, tribes below have probably been talking to competitors first.
**APPLY WHEN:** planning account entry; deciding whether to escalate.
**AVOID WHEN:** you need it to decide a borderline case — the calibration is offered without thresholds, so it does not resolve close calls.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=enterprise
**Evidence:** assertion — presented as the authors' judgment, no data attached.

### [STORY:SHIP-CHECK] Read the buyer's status-quo rationalizations against the arc
**Move:** Listen for the reasoning that the ship is still above water — the invented scenarios in which nothing needs to change — and diagnose how much pressure has actually accumulated instead of pushing for a decision before the peak.
**APPLY WHEN:** stalled deals where the buyer agrees with the analysis but does nothing; forecast hygiene.
**AVOID WHEN:** it becomes a licence to wait indefinitely; the sinking-ship generalization is presented without a citable source.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** assertion — "researchers who study change" cited with no named source.

### Stage 4 — Stalls, skepticism and credibility repair

### [STORY:UNSTALL] Build a belief-targeted story for a stalled opportunity
**Move:** Take a stalled deal, brainstorm why you believe it is stalled, then convert that into one question: what would this buyer have to believe in order to move (a common answer being that the cost of doing nothing exceeds the cost of acting). Build a story carrying exactly that belief, drawing on an episode where your own failure to act cost you, and use it to move the buyer one rung — not to close.
**APPLY WHEN:** an opportunity has gone quiet or is parked at one stage; status-quo bias rather than a competitor is the obstacle.
**AVOID WHEN:** the stall has a mechanical cause — budget freeze, reorg, a missing legal step — since a belief story does not move a procurement calendar; also when your diagnosis of the stall is an untested guess.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=product/saas/service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — Exercise 7; the underlying going-it-alone case is Adam's story from earlier in the book.

### [STORY:FAVOR-TRIAL] Trade a favor for one bounded live trial
**Move:** When a prospect or internal skeptic says they understand the method but it is culturally impossible, stop arguing the model and ask for one bounded experiment: name an unrelated favor you already did, then ask them to run the full sequence on their very next call and report back. Scope it to one call, never a rollout — the buyer's reaction in a live call, not you, is what breaks the skepticism.
**APPLY WHEN:** the buyer or champion says they get it but are not ready, or that it feels strange; late-stage evaluation where the objection is cultural fit rather than capability; the person has already invested time in a workshop, pilot or long meeting.
**AVOID WHEN:** the objection is substantive — price, security, a capability gap — since a trial does not answer it; when no real favor exists and you would be manufacturing obligation; when a failed solo attempt would burn a real account. The authors themselves call the guilt lever uncomfortable and used it only for lack of another path: treat as ethically borderline and state the ask plainly.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led/outbound · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting/saas · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one named executive (John Burke, Oracle), self-reported outcome, no control.

### [STORY:DIRTY-LAUNDRY] Keep the company's own failure in the story over internal objection
**Move:** Build the Who I Represent story around a decision your company got wrong, what customers told you, and what you changed — and keep the admission in when a colleague objects that it airs dirty laundry. The book's worked case: Oracle announced it would fuse two acquired application lines into a single successor, sales of both slowed because customers with large sunk investments did not want the disruption, and the admitted error was assuming the vendor knew best instead of listening; within five months the strategy was replaced with a commitment to keep building new capabilities on each existing product for five years and support them for their lifetime. Customers stayed skeptical and bought more only after delivery proved the commitment.
**APPLY WHEN:** you carry a credibility deficit from a merger, pivot, outage or reputation problem the buyer already knows about; the buyer expects vendor spin.
**AVOID WHEN:** the admission touches an unresolved liability, a live legal matter, or a failure you have not actually fixed — the pattern works only when the turning point and the delivered outcome are real; regulated or procurement-scored contexts where the words enter the record.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=product/saas/service · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — Burke's own retelling of the Oracle episode.

### Stage 5 — Enablement, management and the organization

### [STORY:FOUNDER-DNA] Teach the founding story to every employee at every level
**Move:** Construct the company origin story with the same elements used for buyers — setting (the market conditions that made the idea necessary), complication (who fought you), turning point (what let you launch anyway), resolution (what the company achieved) — and make sure everyone from executives to frontline staff learns why the company exists, not just what it sells.
**APPLY WHEN:** onboarding; culture-building; sales enablement where reps need something retellable.
**AVOID WHEN:** the story is retrofitted or sanitized — the examples rely on real conflict and real failures, so a scrubbed version has nothing to transmit.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led/any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative/stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — Herb Kelleher and Southwest as the worked case, plus a publisher's quote calling CEOs the chief storytellers; no counterfactual and no control for the business model itself.

### [STORY:KEYNOTE-CONFESSION] Spend the executive keynote on the company story, failures included
**Move:** With the internal stage, use the whole slot to narrate why you founded the company, what you struggled with, what you learned, how the company reinvented itself, and your own worst decisions — instead of a performance update.
**APPLY WHEN:** all-hands, anniversary, kickoff; audiences of employees or partners who need to believe before they sell.
**AVOID WHEN:** investor or regulated disclosure settings; a leader with no credibility to trade on, where self-deprecation reads as weakness rather than candour.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — Larry Ellison's 45-minute keynote at Oracle's thirtieth anniversary plus workshop-participant reactions to a video of it.

### [STORY:PRODUCT-ORIGIN-ARC] Brief the field on the product's birth story before its specs
**Move:** Build the launch narrative by answering whose idea it was, what is interesting about that person, whose need it was designed for, what the embarrassing mistakes in development were, what was learned, and how one specific real human is helped by it. Present that first, and describe what a person does with the product rather than its specifications.
**APPLY WHEN:** product launch to the sales force; onboarding new hires onto the line; channel or reseller enablement.
**AVOID WHEN:** technical audiences who need spec detail to qualify deals — the instruction is to use bullets, lists and statistics sparingly, not to eliminate them; also when the origin story is dull or the release is a routine iteration.
**Deal-gate:** motion=channel/any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=product/saas · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — a composite senior product marketer reporting before-and-after on one launch, with engagement judged by looking at the room.

### [STORY:INTERNAL-CUSTOMER] Sell to the field exactly the way you want them to sell
**Move:** Run the enablement session as a demonstration of the selling motion: tell stories, drop the all-knowing expert posture, get the reps to open up, and let tribes form, so people leave with something retellable rather than a deck to regurgitate.
**APPLY WHEN:** any internal launch or training, especially where reps currently arrive at customer meetings and replay the launch deck.
**AVOID WHEN:** compliance or certification training with mandated content; sessions whose required outcome is verifiable knowledge transfer rather than belief.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=product/saas/service · market=consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the authors' recurring workshop position, illustrated by two client-side complaints about slide-driven sessions.

### [STORY:VISCERAL-SLIDE] Replace bulleted arguments with one image and speak the text
**Move:** For each point, show a single image carrying the idea — their example is a train wreck while you describe IT departments in disarray — instead of a bulleted list, and put the wording the presenter needs into speaker notes so a rep reusing the deck still knows what to say.
**APPLY WHEN:** field messaging decks meant to be reused by sellers; customer presentations; any point meant to be felt rather than proven.
**AVOID WHEN:** the audience needs numbers on screen to evaluate a claim; procurement or technical reviews; the visual metaphor is so oblique it needs its own explanation.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative/stage
**Evidence:** assertion — supported by a personal anecdote about a photographer asking admen what a photograph makes them feel, plus a popular-learning book's notion of visceralization; the brain mechanism is not from a named study.

### [STORY:DECK-STORYBOARD] Storyboard the presentation as an arc
**Move:** Before building slides, lay the presentation out as setting → complication → turning point → resolution, and seed individual stories throughout the talk. Treat the deck as a narrative rather than a body of information.
**APPLY WHEN:** building launch material, field messaging, or a customer-facing pitch reps will reuse.
**AVOID WHEN:** reference or documentation decks whose job is lookup, not persuasion.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative/stage
**Evidence:** assertion — offered as method, with the tagline-versus-deck contradiction (a simplicity promise delivered via 250-plus slides) as the only supporting illustration.

### [STORY:KNOW-YOUR-HORSES] Manage by individual knowledge, not by uniform pressure
**Move:** Invest time in learning where each rep comes from, what drives them and why they do the work, and adapt per person rather than applying one corrective method to everyone labelled lazy.
**APPLY WHEN:** a rep with visible capability is underperforming; mixed-tenure teams; you inherited the team.
**AVOID WHEN:** the gap is a skill or territory problem with a concrete fix; individualized treatment shades into inconsistent standards across the team.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** assertion — argued through the Seabiscuit trainer swap and Phil Jackson's coaching record, both external retrospective narratives with obvious confounds.

### [STORY:MGR-GO-FIRST] Unlock a closed rep by disclosing your own struggle first
**Move:** When a rep will not open up under normal questioning, stop asking and disclose something you are personally struggling with that has the same emotional shape as their situation. Do not solve their problem; demonstrate that you understand it.
**APPLY WHEN:** morale or engagement decline in a rep whose pipeline is otherwise sound; direct questioning has already failed; you suspect the real cause but they will not name it.
**AVOID WHEN:** the issue is performance management with formal consequences; disclosure would be inappropriate to the reporting relationship or the rep's boundaries; used as a ritual rather than actual disclosure.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one sales VP's account of connecting with a rep by telling the story of his son's school year; the rep's subsequent record year is credited to that conversation without controls.

### [STORY:MONDAY-CAMPFIRE] Replace the pipeline meeting with a story round
**Move:** Drop the forecast-and-commit interrogation format for the weekly team meeting; instead go around the team and have each person tell a story about a recent selling experience, explicitly including their own mistakes, so old and new reps learn from each other.
**APPLY WHEN:** weekly meetings have become a watchdog exercise everyone resents; team knowledge is concentrated in a few top performers; you want the selling behaviour modelled internally.
**AVOID WHEN:** forecast discipline is genuinely absent and has no other venue; the culture punishes admitted failure, which makes the round performative or unsafe.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one client running a weekly story round, with self-reported improvements in attendance, morale and engagement; no measurement given.

### [STORY:PREFRAME-DISCOMFORT] Pre-frame the first attempts as deliberately uncomfortable
**Move:** Before asking anyone to attempt the new behaviour, make the wrongness of a new habit physical — clasp your hands, note which thumb sits on top, then switch it — and draw the three zones (comfort, learning, panic), having people recall their own first attempt at a skill they now have. Position the requested change as a step into the learning zone and tell them to expect a struggle and to make mistakes rather than expecting fluency.
**APPLY WHEN:** rolling out a new selling method; the first calls after training, where reversion is likeliest; coaching a champion through a behaviour change; a buyer facing an internal change-management objection.
**AVOID WHEN:** the resistance is capacity or authority rather than confidence — reframing discomfort creates neither budget nor mandate; and when it is used to excuse a method that keeps failing, since "expect to struggle" also suppresses legitimate feedback that the approach does not fit the market.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=service/consulting/info-product · market=consultative/stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — standard workshop devices; the uniform "very uncomfortable" answer is the authors' own tally and the preparation-over-talent claim is borrowed from a popular book.

### [STORY:RATIO-AUDIT] Run your own revenue-concentration numbers before believing your method works
**Move:** Compute what share of revenue comes from what share of your sellers or partners, and check whether it is the same people every year. Then look at what the top group actually does on calls rather than what your methodology says they should do.
**APPLY WHEN:** sales-enablement or leadership review; a training rollout showing adoption drop-off.
**AVOID WHEN:** teams too small for the ratio to mean anything; territory or account-quality differences unaccounted for — the book does not control for these.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — Mike's own affiliate network: roughly 40 affiliates, five producing about 90% of revenue, the same five each year.

## Where this book stops working

- **Transactional, high-velocity and self-serve motions.** The core loop — disclose, trade stories, tend, reflect back — assumes a meeting with a real time budget. The observed flagship call ran about 45 minutes before the requirement surfaced. Sub-$10k ACV, SDR-length calls and one-touch closes cannot carry it; the book's own B2C example collapses eight rungs to four and its prospecting advice shrinks to a 10–20-second opener.
- **Procurement-gated and regulated deals.** Almost every vulnerability move inverts once the words enter a scored record: admitted mistakes become risk findings, "I have never done this" becomes a capability gap, a champion-led bottom-up motion gets disqualified for lacking a sponsor. The book acknowledges this only in passing.
- **Late-stage evaluation and technical validation.** Narrative substituted for specs reads as evasion. Every move whose AVOID WHEN clause names evaluation mode is a case of this; the book has essentially nothing to say about competitive displacement, pricing negotiation or close mechanics.
- **Committee sales where you are the unknown vendor.** A first-meeting confession to a scoring committee spends credibility you have not yet earned; the book's own successful confession cases were told by a CEO, an owner, or someone with prior relationship standing. The authority variable is uncontrolled throughout — the counterargument that the CEO could sell that way *because* he was CEO is answered only rhetorically.
- **Short cycles under quota pressure.** Entering a call with no predetermined outcome and no agenda is written for a consultant or founder, not for a rep with a monthly number and no pipeline slack; the book itself notes quota structures are rigged and then prescribes behaviour that assumes they are not.
- **Cultures and buyers who treat workplace disclosure as intrusive.** Personal-story trading and emotion-probing sequences are presented as near-universal on the strength of the authors' own workshops in one industry and one country.
- **The headline claim itself.** "20% of sellers make 80% of revenue and storytelling is what separates them" is an unaudited self-assertion; the 87/13 variant comes from a vendor index quoted secondhand from a keynote, and Burke's 90/10 is one executive's impression. None of it establishes that story training moves the ratio — the book never reports a before/after on any client's distribution.

## What the book debunks

- **That top performers succeed because they use the methodology.** The authors' own affiliate numbers (five of ~40 producing ~90%, the same five yearly) collapse the trainer's self-serving inference, and they concede bottom performers abandon a taught methodology within about ten days.
- **That relationship skill is an unteachable gift.** The "he has the intangibles" explanation is attacked as a dodge that stopped anyone from teaching the behaviour; the counter (neuroplasticity, everyone is wired for story) is asserted from popular-science books, not demonstrated.
- **That the buyer's decision is a logical vendor evaluation.** Claimed to be emotional first, logically justified afterwards — sourced to "research now tells us" with no named study.
- **That questioning builds trust.** Three decades of question-based consultative models are judged not to have changed industry outcomes, because teaching a sequence trains sellers to think about the next question instead of listening. The documented backfire: an agenda-plus-diagnostic opening pushes a senior buyer into interrogation mode and forces the seller into premature product talk.
- **The doctor/patient metaphor for selling.** Buyers do not treat sellers as diagnosticians and recognise pain-elicitation questions from every prior vendor, so the move signals exactly what the seller hoped to hide.
- **That expertise helps on the call.** Premature elaboration — recognising the fit and saying so — loses deals already effectively won; the recalled Xerox curve has new-hire performance peaking around eighteen months, then declining as sellers become product experts.
- **That vulnerability equals weakness.** Asserted, not measured; a workshop room split on the objection and it was answered rhetorically.
- **That more slides and more detail persuade.** The failure named is overload, not underload; analytic appetite is unbounded, so product detail turns a call into endless product discussion. The tool is explicitly not blamed — the usage is. A second failure mode is confusion rather than boredom.
- **That selling and managing need different skills.** Both are framed as influencing people to change; new managers fail by swapping connection for control, and a manager who reproduces the interrogation style he hated as a rep gets evasive answers.
- **That polish matters.** A flawless-hero story is the documented failure mode; a superhuman-effort service story lands weaker than an admitted-mistake-then-made-good one; a story with no struggle is not a story; an all-facts story cannot move anyone and an all-emotion story stops making sense.
- **Named anti-patterns:** "Why I'm So Great" masquerading as Who I Am; the About Us timeline masquerading as a company story; treating the resolution as the turning point; telling a story only as an opener and abandoning it for the rest of the cycle; telling a story and then not yielding the floor; sending a recap of a story the buyer never told.
- **Self-undercutting claims to carry as such:** the book's own hedge that a story may have been used successfully *on* the teller (the Bush/Putin case) — these techniques are outcome-neutral; the closing promise that struggle and early failure are expected, which makes the method unfalsifiable if a rep never gets results; and the terminal "everyone can do this" claim sitting directly against the 80/20 framing the same chapter opens with.

## Numbers that matter

- **87/13** — top 13% of B2B salespeople producing 87% of revenue, attributed to Greg Alexander of Sales Benchmark Index from an index of 1,100 B2B sales organizations; vendor research quoted secondhand from a keynote, not peer-reviewed. Predecessor: Xerox's 80/20. Burke's variant at Oracle: closer to 90/10, self-reported.
- **Mike's affiliate network** — ~40 affiliates, 5 producing ~90% of revenue, the same 5 each year (self-reported).
- **SPIN project** — 1,500 sales calls recorded among Xerox's top 20% of sellers, 1979, with Neil Rackham. Xerox new-hire performance peaked at ~18 months, then declined (recalled, not reproduced).
- **Quota** — fewer than 50% of B2B IT salespeople hit quota even in the 1990s; ~17% judged likely to make quota from the authors' work with client sales managers. Both self-reported, unsourced.
- **Methodology decay** — bottom performers stop using a taught methodology within ~10 days of the workshop (managers' self-report).
- **Call anatomy of the flagship anecdote** — a ~3-minute mistake story; the real requirement surfaced ~45 minutes in; zero questions asked by the seller.
- **Story sizing** — repertoire tiers of 30 seconds / 3 minutes / ~10 minutes; first-call story capped at ~3 minutes; cold-approach window of 10–20 seconds; a 2-minute compressed version; Hurd's illustrative story ~2 minutes; the 45-minute call as too short to teach a prospect to tell her own story.
- **Card system** — 5 colors on 3×5 cards (yellow point, red resolution, green setting, white complication, blue turning point); build order point → resolution → setting → complication → turning point; ≥1 circled emotion word per card except yellow; 8 workshop exercises; Story Leaders workshops run 2.5 days with vulnerability taught on day 2.
- **Proportion** — Ben's Scanlon story ~850 words with ~100 words (~12%) of setting; contrast case of 10–15 pages of setting acceptable in a 300-page novel.
- **Enterprise example** — 6-month average cycle, $250,000 average sale, committee buyer, 8-step cycle; B2C investment-services example, one-meeting close, 4-step cycle.
- **Herd and diffusion analogies** — 51% of a herd must move before the rest follow (animal observation, source unnamed); Moore's segments as presented: innovators 4%, early adopters 16%, early majority 33%, late majority 33%, laggards 14%; Microsoft Office cited above 90% enterprise share, attributed to grassroots spread.
- **IBM account** — one workshop became roughly half a dozen within three months; IBM later licensed and rebranded Solution Selling and trained more than 15,000 salespeople; a multimillion-dollar sale, self-reported by the author.
- **Client outcome claims (self-reported)** — Clear Technologies 43% revenue growth in 2010 into Q1 2011, biggest year in company history; Laura's firm expanded reach by 300% and cut dependence on the incumbent carrier to zero; Juliet's dating matches doubled after disclosure (n=1, outside sales).
- **Deck caricatures (self-reported by narrators inside the book)** — 300-slide decks at ~30 bullets per slide; slide 149 reached by 10 a.m. on day one of a two-day partner session; ~100 slides in one afternoon; 250+ slides used to launch a product whose tagline promised simplicity.
- **Oracle case** — PeopleSoft acquired 2005, strategy reversed within 5 months, new capabilities guaranteed for 5 years plus lifetime support; the thirtieth-anniversary keynote ran 45 minutes.
- **Southwest** — idea 1967, launched 1971 with four planes and three Texas destinations; 24 consecutive profitable years; stock up 300% since 1990 (as stated in a 2012 book, unverified, and with no control for the business model).
- **Folklore-grade figures to flag rather than repeat** — 7% words / 38% tone / 55% nonverbal, and the 93% non-word remainder, sourced here to Rogers and Farson; the underlying studies concerned judging feelings from mismatched single-word cues, not general communication. Also: ~6,000 emotion words in English (unsourced); storytelling as the primary means of conveying ideas for ~95,000 years (an anthropologist friend's claim); 10,000 hours (Gladwell, cited secondhand and then deflated by the authors themselves); "better than 95% of salespeople" if you become a good listener (self-reported).

## Blind spots of this book

- **No measurement of its own core claim.** Not one before/after, control group, win-rate or cycle-time comparison appears. Every outcome is self-reported by an author, a workshop participant, or a client of the authors' training company — including the testimonials used to validate the method.
- **Neuroscience as decoration.** Mirror neurons, left/right hemisphere division of labour, the triune brain, limbic-first decision-making and MNRD carry the entire explanatory load and are sourced to trade books and a conference talk rather than primary studies. Several are contested or outdated. The behavioural advice survives without them; asserting the mechanism to a buyer is a liability.
- **Authority and selection confounds untouched.** The best cases are told by CEOs, founders and executives with standing to be candid; the sports and Seabiscuit analogies are retrospective narratives with no counterfactual; Linda's account of overriding her own team is survivorship-biased.
- **The manipulation problem is raised and abandoned.** The book's own opening exemplar has a negotiator deliberately setting aside his real feelings to manufacture similarity, and its own mirror-neuron framing says the effect depends on genuine intent. It never resolves the contradiction, never gives a test for whether disclosure is honest, and offers no guidance on what a buyer is owed once the technique is recognised.
- **No consent or safety layer.** Client mistakes, a mentor's child's addiction, workshop participants weeping, an exercise pushing people toward their own shame — all are used as material with no discussion of consent, confidentiality or the limits of a salesperson's competence to handle what surfaces.
- **Commercial layers missing entirely.** Pricing, negotiation, competitive displacement, close mechanics, multithreading against a rival's champion, renewal and expansion economics, and any form of forecasting beyond email tone are absent. The lens must import all of them from elsewhere.
- **The method is unfalsifiable as taught.** Discomfort is pre-framed as evidence of learning, early failure is promised as part of the arc, and no failure case of the method itself is offered anywhere in the book — a rep who tries it and loses has no way, inside this framework, to conclude that it did not fit.
- **Digital, asynchronous and remote selling.** Written in 2012 for in-person meetings: video calls appear only as a caveat, and the sole asynchronous artefact is the recap email. Silence, mirroring, body cues and acting-out all degrade or invert in the channels most B2B deals now run through.
