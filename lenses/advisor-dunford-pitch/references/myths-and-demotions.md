# advisor-dunford-pitch — Myths, demotions and failure modes

What this tradition **refutes**, what it **demotes**, and what inside it must **never be quoted as fact**. Source: *Sales Pitch* (Dunford, 2023). Read this before citing any number or any confident claim from this lens.

---

## 1. Myths the book actively debunks

| Myth | The book's counter | Status |
|---|---|---|
| "Buyers have enough information now, they don't need vendor guidance." | Volume is the problem, not the cure — buyers are information-rich and sense-making-poor. | REFUTED (argued) |
| "No decision means they preferred their current tool." | More often a vote against changing under uncertainty; log it as a loss to a named alternative approach. [PITCH:NODECISION-RENAME] | DEMOTED (reasoning, no data) |
| "Buyers resent vendors having opinions about competitors." | The book claims the reverse — teaching is what buyers rate highest, as long as the framing stays factual. Mapping the market ≠ bad-mouthing. | CONTESTED (assertion; the "rate highest" claim carries no cited study) |
| "New or emerging-category products have no competition." | There is always an alternative: Excel, an intern, a manual process. [PITCH:SQFIRST] | REFUTED |
| "Value = what your product does." | Value is the answer to "so what?", and only *differentiated* value is a reason to buy. [PITCH:SOWHAT] | REFUTED |
| "Required to buy = differentiating." | Table stakes (SOC 2, ERP compatibility) belong in objection handling, not the value story. [PITCH:TABLESTAKES-DEFER] | REFUTED |
| "An inspiring vision deck also sells product." | It invites "come back next year" and adds delivery risk to the buyer's mental ledger; in B2B, "new" can read as untested or insecure. [PITCH:PICKNARR] | REFUTED |
| "Frame all competition as the old way." | Fails as soon as several credible new approaches exist that cannot be sorted old vs new. [PITCH:APPROACHGRP] | REFUTED |
| "Proof must be numeric." | Experiential value is better proved by demo plus customers narrating their own experience. The governing rule: the *vendor* asserting something is not proof. [PITCH:THIRDPARTY-VOICE] | REFUTED |
| "A standard script produces robots." | Standardisation buys consistent positioning, fast rep onboarding and measurability; improvisation is expected on top. [PITCH:STANDARD-DECK] | CONTESTED (assertion) |
| "Reps will like the better pitch." | Dislike of a new pitch is normal and is not a quality signal — hence single-rep validation rather than a popularity vote. [PITCH:PILOT-REP], [PITCH:PASSFAIL] | DEMOTED (self-serving: it also immunises the method against rep pushback) |

**Note on the last row.** "Reps disliking it isn't evidence against it" is a genuinely useful correction *and* an unfalsifiable shield. When this lens invokes it, it must also name what evidence *would* count against the pitch — otherwise it is arguing in a way that cannot lose.

## 2. Anti-recommendations (things this lens tells you NOT to do, with the why)

1. **Do not open with a generic trend.** "More data than ever", "AI adoption is accelerating", "digital transformation" set up nothing, because any competitor could open the same way. An insight that does not reverse-engineer from *your* differentiated value is decoration. [PITCH:INSIGHT-RE]
2. **Do not run the Alternatives step as a monologue.** Reps talking at the prospect instead of confirming they recognise themselves in the market picture is the book's most-repeated pitch failure. [PITCH:ALTDISCOVERY]
3. **Do not run interrogation-style discovery.** Questions before you have taught anything read as an intake form. Named explicitly as a failure mode.
4. **Do not customize the first demo.** Discovery is too shallow after call one to tailor credibly; the customized demo is the *next step*, not this one. [PITCH:CUSTOMLATER] — but note this rule is the author's opinion, unsupported by data, and it inverts on short transactional cycles where a second meeting kills momentum.
5. **Do not introduce competitors the buyer has not named.** You hand them research to do and a vendor to go find. [PITCH:NAMEHOLD]
6. **Do not raise an objection you answer badly.** Surfacing it unprompted converts a maybe-latent risk into a live one. Treat its existence as a company-priority problem instead. [PITCH:NOANSWER]
7. **Do not gerrymander the criteria list.** If the Perfect World list is only reachable by ignoring a real strength of an alternative, the buyer feels steered; a coerced yes buys nothing later. [PITCH:RIGHTGATE]
8. **Do not restructure the pitch mid-test.** Substantial rewrites invalidate the test you are running. [PITCH:FREEZECORE]
9. **Do not deliver the sales pitch from a conference stage.** The author treats it as inappropriate; only the insight travels. [PITCH:INSIGHT-CONTENT]
10. **Do not treat positioning as set-and-forget**, and do not treat a named owner as a substitute for cross-functional creation. [PITCH:OWNER], [PITCH:XFUNC]

## 3. Documented failure modes (the mistake catalogue = the acceptance test for a finished pitch)

The book's seven named mistakes:
1. Insight that is not unique — a generic trend sets up nothing.
2. No discovery in the Alternatives step.
3. Overlong setup — and, symmetrically, skipping the setup altogether.
4. Weak or undifferentiated value themes (a positioning defect surfacing as a pitch defect).
5. Proof that does not match the prospect's industry, use case, or prior status quo.
6. An ask so complicated the buyer second-guesses accepting it; or ending with "call us if you have questions".
7. Launch-and-forget — untrained new hires, unchecked drift, no update when positioning shifts.

Further failure modes named across the book: the interrogation-style discovery call; the fully customized first demo; inherited decks nobody has questioned structurally (about-us → logo NASCAR → generic problem → walkthrough → pricing); reading the alternatives table aloud; value-theme slides degrading into feature bullets; reverting to a walkthrough inside the value step; a platform diagram that makes optional components look mandatory; a bespoke example implementation the prospect assumes is included in the base price; introducing horizon competitors or a future vision that gives the buyer more research to do; marketing's positioning dying at the handoff to sales; multi-product companies pitching the whole portfolio by default; rewriting mid-test.

## 4. Where the method itself stops working (demotions of its own scope)

- **Undifferentiated products.** The structure is a delivery vehicle for value only you have. With no defensible capability gap, the setup builds criteria you cannot then satisfy and the Perfect World step exposes the hole rather than covering it. The book's only remedy is "go fix positioning first" — it offers **no pitch for parity products**. This is the single largest scope hole.
- **Transactional and unconsidered purchases.** Engineered against considered-purchase risk: 5–8 stakeholders, a champion staking credibility, an hour-long call. Low-ACV self-serve and high-velocity SMB motions cannot carry a 5–10-minute market-education setup; [PITCH:CUSTOMLATER] kills momentum outright there. Only [PITCH:ASK-STANDARDIZE] and [PITCH:DISQUALIFY-FAST] are tuned lower down.
- **Expert repeat buyers with fixed criteria.** [PITCH:BUYRUBRIC], [PITCH:APPROACHGRP] and [PITCH:RIGHTGATE] all assume a buyer who does not yet know how to choose. Against a third-time purchaser, teaching the market reads as condescension — [PITCH:PICKNARR] itself concedes a walkthrough can be adequate.
- **Not a 101 method.** Qualification, discovery technique, objection handling and negotiation are declared out of scope and assumed known.
- **Narrow-landscape and unfamiliar-category deals need surgery.** [PITCH:COMPRESS-ALTS] and [PITCH:CATEGORY-DEFINE] exist because the default misfires.
- **Non-software and services offers are thinly covered.** All worked examples are B2B tech; services, consulting and hardware appear only at the edges.
- **Regulated/committee deals shift weight to the objections step.** A pitch tuned only to the champion still stalls in security review if [PITCH:TABLESTAKES-DEFER], [PITCH:CHANGE-FEAR] and [PITCH:POC-LOGISTICS] are skipped.
- **No treatment of AI-assisted or asynchronous buying.** The unit of analysis is a live human first call.

## 5. Numbers that must NOT be quoted as fact

**Quotable with attribution (one item only):**
- 40–60% of B2B purchase processes end in no decision — attributed to Dixon & McKenna, *The JOLT Effect* (2022). Borrowed, not generated by this book. It supports "the status quo is the competitor", **not** "this pitch structure wins more deals".

**Structural counts — descriptions of the framework, not findings:** 5 positioning components; 8 pitch steps (3 setup + 5 follow-through); ≤3 value themes; ~3 approach groups; 8 proof formats; 3 Introduction diagram archetypes; 4 visual-asset types; 7 common mistakes. The 3-theme cap has an asserted memory-limit rationale with no cited cognitive research — do not present it as a psychological finding.

**Timing figures — observed by the author, not measured:** first call 30–60 minutes; setup ~5–10 minutes (reported range 90 seconds to 10 minutes); insight ~1 slide / 1–2 minutes.

**Author's base, all self-reported, no audit:** ~30 years in positioning; 25 years as an executive across 7 venture-backed start-ups; ~10 years consulting; 200+ tech companies positioned; 200+ pitches built over ~15 years; ~12 in-house pitches with earlier versions of the structure.

**The one outcome story is not evidence.** A start-up reported as on a path to doubling revenue the year after the pitch rebuild, later acquired by IBM — single case, no counterfactual, no control. Never cite as the method's result.

**Objection-script figures are illustrative examples, not measured outcomes:** ~2 weeks typical deployment; as little as 1 week to go live on a small initial deployment; ~1 day to migrate data for a POC; pricing shown across 3 customer types. Quoting these as your own promise is exactly the [PITCH:CHANGE-FEAR] failure mode — an overstated number becomes a broken promise in implementation.

**Illustrative value claims used as examples only:** 30% faster; 10 hours/week saved on admin; 99% uptime; one day vs one week to feel fully trained. These are placeholders in the book's examples, not findings about any product.

**Company self-reported figures:** Gearset — 2,000+ paid deployments, >4× the nearest competitor. Postman (2022) — 20 million users, half a million companies, largest API hub. Vendor-supplied, unaudited.

**Consumer anecdote:** the toilet store — 3 weeks of research → 0 purchases; 3 criteria → 15 minutes. Retail illustration of the rubric mechanism; carries zero B2B weight.

## 6. Blind spots of the tradition (state these when this lens speaks)

- **No outcome measurement.** No win-rate data, no control group, no A/B against the deck being replaced. The book's own prescribed validation is one rep's judgement (2 criteria, 1 person) and is explicitly non-statistical — so the core claim is untested by the book's own standard.
- **Positioning quality is assumed solvable.** "Fix your positioning first" answers most failure modes, which pushes the hardest problem out of scope (into *Obviously Awesome*) and leaves parity products with nothing.
- **Only the first call.** Multi-threading, the middle of the deal, procurement, pricing negotiation, renewals and expansion get essentially nothing; [PITCH:POC-LOGISTICS] is as far downstream as it goes.
- **Champion-only optics.** Little to say about the rooms the seller is not in, beyond "hand them material".
- **Ethics handled by assertion.** The line between guiding a buyer and gerrymandering criteria rests entirely on the seller's own honesty — no external check, no test for when a criteria list has become self-serving.
- **Buyer heterogeneity flattened.** Culture, geography, procurement regimes and non-tech industries are absent; every worked example is a Western B2B software company.
- **Rep skill and manager incentives.** The structure assumes a rep able to hold a market conversation and leadership willing to gate certification; it says little about low-skill, high-churn sales floors where the script degrades on contact.
- **Common bias of the genre.** The author monetises the methodology through consulting and training; the book's "evidence" is her own client roster. Agreement between this book and *Obviously Awesome* is the same author and must never be presented as independent confirmation.
