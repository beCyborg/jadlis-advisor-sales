# Let's Get Real or Let's Not Play — digest

## What this book is for the lens

The lens's engine for complex, multi-stakeholder B2B deals where a diagnostic conversation is actually possible. Its mechanism is not persuasion but *mutual exploration*: the seller's job is to help the client reach a good decision — including "no" — and the payoff argument is that a red light found early is cheaper for both sides than a won-and-failed deal. It beats other sources on three specific things: (1) converting vague client language into client-stated numbers before any solution is discussed; (2) qualifying the *decision process* and stakeholder access rather than only the need; (3) a disciplined yellow-light protocol that drags unspoken doubts into the room while you are still in it. Where other lenses supply closing pressure, this one supplies the structured refusal to present, propose, or discount before the preconditions exist.

## Core model

**ORDER** — Opportunity (should they do it?), Resources (can they?), Decisions (how and by whom?), Exact solution / Enable decisions (will they do it with us?), Results (will they do more with us?). ORD before ER; drawn linear, applied iteratively across many conversations.

Five key beliefs underneath: consultant and client share an interest in an exact fit; intent counts more than technique (Trust = Intent + Expertise; pushing triggers reactance); solutions have no inherent value, only problems people care about or results they highly value; method fuses IQ (business thinking) + EQ (safety so information flows) + XQ (repeatable process); world-class inquiry precedes world-class advocacy — the three default postures being Tell, Accept, Guess.

Qualification metric: **the free flow of meaningful client-stated information**. Two disciplines: **No Guessing** (about Opportunity, Resources, Decisions) and **Slow Down for Yellow Lights**. The Opportunity Checklist is Issues / Evidence / Impact / Context / Constraints, run as: move off the solution → get all issues → prioritize → evidence and impact → context and constraints, closing with the **nine-inquiry** gate and the **20-question** quick reference. Decisions are worked through the **grid** (steps · decision at each step · dates · people), with the rule that the *how* is learned directly from the *who*. Winning has two levers: do not present until ready, and present to enable a decision — via the seven-element **Meeting Plan** (End in Mind, Key Beliefs, Proof/Action, Questions, Yellow Lights, Next Steps, Agenda). Yellow lights resolve in a strict three beats: **acknowledge → understand → resolve**. Price is a function of scope × timing × division of labor, negotiated only when price is provably the last issue. Initiating runs the **five P's** — Prioritize, Prepare, Personalize, Practice, Pre-position — on a 95/5 prospect list, with referrals replacing cold calls.

## Moves

### Stance, intent, and personal preparation

### [REAL:INTENT-STMT] Write, test, and rehearse an explicit Statement of Intent
**Move:** Put your intent toward the client in one written line that is explicitly mutually beneficial and aligns rather than hides your self-interest — e.g. to find out whether there is a real fit between what they value and what you do well, and to say so first if there is not. Test the wording on colleagues or friends for reaction, then practise acting congruently with it under pressure; the test is whether it survives moments when your own quota is at stake. Where a conversation breaks down, stating the intent aloud can re-establish common ground.
**APPLY WHEN:** Before any first meeting; in quarters where revenue pressure is high enough to distort behaviour; whenever you struggle to hold a non-pushy stance.
**AVOID WHEN:** Recited at the client as a script — stated intent contradicted by observed behaviour deepens distrust rather than repairing it; intent is conveyed by consistent action, not claims.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=low/mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — repeated coaching exercise, shipped as an appendix; sample statements borrowed from Wilson and Johnson.

### [REAL:CLIENT-NUMBERS] Invert focus under quota pressure
**Move:** When the pull to close is strongest, deliberately drop your own metrics from the conversation and redirect all attention to the client's — what they measure, where it stands, what they want it to be. Treat "I must make this sale" as the signal to invert, not to push.
**APPLY WHEN:** End of period, thin pipeline, deals you cannot afford to lose.
**AVOID WHEN:** Genuinely transactional purchases where the buyer wants speed and price, not a diagnosis of their business.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — the authors' contrast between selling to survive, selling from ego, and helping clients succeed.

### [REAL:ASSUMPTION-AUDIT] Surface the assumptions hidden in your solution statement
**Move:** Before proposing anything, enumerate what you are assuming — that the problem exists, is singular, is prioritized, is measurable, that the solution's cost sits meaningfully below the problem's, that it will not create worse problems, that someone will fund it. Run the same audit after client meetings: list every assumption that went unchallenged, mark the mission-critical ones, repeat over several meetings to find your pattern.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any time you catch yourself ready to say "what you need is…"; as a post-call discipline in pipeline review.
**AVOID WHEN:** Turned into an interrogation of the client — the audit is yours, and only the critical unknowns become questions.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=low/mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — taught as a repeated workshop exercise across many audiences.

### [REAL:LISTEN-EXACT] Listen for the client's exact words, not your inner commentary
**Move:** During the call, consciously choose whose voice you attend to and turn the internal one down. Capture and reuse the client's repeated key words, metaphors and analogies instead of translating them into your vocabulary; match pace, emphasis and inflection; register mismatches between words, tone and body language and treat the incongruence as information. Build the skill outside sales calls.
**APPLY WHEN:** Every qualifying dialogue; especially before advocacy, since your advocacy should be phrased in their metaphors.
**AVOID WHEN:** Mirroring is mechanical — copying words and cadence without understanding reads as mimicry and damages trust.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=low/mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the authors' stated conclusion from field work with thousands of consultants.

### [REAL:QUESTHYG] Questioning and listening hygiene
**Move:** Ask permission before questioning; one question at a time, wait for the answer; reward the response before the next question, using the client's own words as the reward; be wary of leading questions built to extract agreement rather than understanding; prefer "how" or "what" over "why" where it works; summarize and ask whether you got it right and left anything out. Treat listening as choice plus concentration — focus on the answer rather than your next question, lower internal dialogue, and check the gap between what was said and what was meant.
**APPLY WHEN:** Every discovery conversation.
**AVOID WHEN:** Nothing structurally — but rewards and summaries go mechanical when run as a checklist rather than attention.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the book's consolidated questioning discipline; the optional meditation exercises are practice, not evidence.

### [REAL:FOURVS] Congruence check on the four Vs
**Move:** Audit your communication for alignment across verbal (what the words mean), vocal (how you say them), visual (how you look saying them), and visceral (whether others sense you mean it, driven by the other three). Congruence is claimed to produce belief in your intent even among people who disagree with you.
**APPLY WHEN:** Rehearsing a script or a difficult message; when a message that reads well on paper lands badly.
**AVOID WHEN:** Substituted for substance — congruence buys belief in intent, not credibility of expertise, which must be demonstrated separately.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** assertion — framework stated without support.

### [REAL:ANCHORSTATE] Build a resource state and fire it before client contact
**Move:** Four steps from NLP sources (Laborde, Robbins). Introspect: recall or imagine a moment of full congruence with your intent and catalogue in detail what you look, sound (words, timbre, tone, tempo) and feel like; repeat across instances. Attach a trigger — physical action, phrase, image, or combination — and fire it until the state comes with it. Calibrate what knocks you out of the state and what changes first. Apply: fire the trigger immediately before every call, and keep a card listing intent, looks/sounds/feels like, and trigger.
**APPLY WHEN:** Before high-stakes client interactions; for sellers who lose composure under challenge.
**AVOID WHEN:** Expected to substitute for preparation; the NLP framing carries no independent support here.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** assertion — explicitly derived from NLP authors, presented without evidence of effect.

### [REAL:COMMITDRILL] Build self-trust and relationship trust by kept commitments
**Move:** Self-trust drill: pick a run of consecutive days (30-40 suggested for habit formation, or start with 3-5), make at least one spoken personal commitment daily, keep it, record the experience, restart at day one on any miss. Relationship trust: be deliberate about which commitments you make to clients, keep every one regardless of size, and mark the commitment and its completion in a low-key way ("as promised…") without self-congratulation.
**APPLY WHEN:** Building credibility with a new account; personal discipline work.
**AVOID WHEN:** Marking is overdramatized or self-serving — flagged as low-EQ and counterproductive.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** assertion — derived from Stephen M. R. Covey's The Speed of Trust; the neural-pathway framing is unsupported.

### Initiating new opportunities (the five P's)

### [REAL:RULE955] Apply the 95/5 rule to prospecting
**Move:** Choose your top five prospects and put 95% of prospecting energy into them; when one leaves the list, add one; review periodically. Prioritize on explicit criteria — likelihood of a referral to the CEO or CFO, prestige of the name as a future reference, fit of the solution to their situation, potential economic return.
**APPLY WHEN:** A long undifferentiated prospect list; complex, high-ticket engagements; founder-led or small teams with fixed attention.
**AVOID WHEN:** Genuinely transactional, high-volume, low-ACV motions where throughput does drive results — the authors concede that if volume works, keep it.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/founder-led · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — the We-Do-It engagement (100 potentials → 5 → 3, business won with all 3), self-reported.

### [REAL:BCHYPO1] Build a researched business case hypothesis (data available)
**Move:** Where data exists — existing clients, public companies, companies in the news, private ones you know through third parties — research the same five slots you would qualify on: context (industry issues, trends, key strategies and initiatives), issues your solution addresses, evidence that something must change, probable financial and intangible impact, and constraints that have stopped or could stop them. Pick an entry-point person, filter the research through what matters to that role, and size impact as a range with a working midpoint.
**APPLY WHEN:** Top-five prospects where the research effort is justified; executive entry points.
**AVOID WHEN:** Presented as fact — the hypothesis exists to be tested with the client, not asserted.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/founder-led · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — the NewLogo worked example, including three named constraints (disbelief anyone could solve it; scarce time and better-return alternatives; internal defenders of the status quo who would look bad).

### [REAL:BCHYPO2] Build a presumed business case by interviewing yourself
**Move:** Where prospect data is unavailable, model the closest comparable companies and self-interview in six steps: move off your own solution and brainstorm every problem it solves or result it creates for similar clients; group into themes and likely priorities; select the highest-impact themes; identify what evidence would prove or disprove each and compute order-of-magnitude impact for a company that size; reason about why such companies fail to address it or would not consider new options; map context and which people and functions are affected — possibly roles beyond your usual contact. Exit condition: you can describe your offering as problems solved and results produced with evidence and impact, not features and benefits.
**APPLY WHEN:** Private, opaque or small prospects; entering a new segment; building a repeatable outbound narrative.
**AVOID WHEN:** Treated as verified — only inquiry with the actual prospect confirms it.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/founder-led · acv=low/mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting/saas · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — repeatable exercise, worked through on a generic CRM example.

### [REAL:SHOULDTALK] Set the initial End in Mind to "should we be talking?"
**Move:** Do not try to sell anything on first contact; make the meeting's purpose a mutual decision about whether a conversation is worthwhile. Prepare against three beliefs the prospect must hold: there is something worth talking about, the meeting will be enjoyable or at least not painful, and their time will be used well. Back them with the referral source, the business case hypothesis, careful language, the Meeting Plan, and readiness to let the client do most of the talking.
**APPLY WHEN:** First call or first meeting with a prospect, referred or otherwise.
**AVOID WHEN:** Inbound buyers arriving with an active, specified need who want to move straight into qualifying.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/founder-led/inbound · acv=low/mid/high · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=service/consulting/saas · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the book's standard initiating frame; the We-Do-It variant used deal-specific beliefs.

### [REAL:THREEHEAR] "Three things I keep hearing" opener
**Move:** Introduce the presumed business case as other people's issues rather than your claim: when you talk to peers in the same role you keep hearing three specific problems — name them concretely — then ask whether any are issues for them too. If yes, go deeper into evidence, impact, context and constraints. If no, accept it cheerfully and ask what issues they *do* face in that area.
**APPLY WHEN:** Prospects you could not research specifically; role-based outbound.
**AVOID WHEN:** The three issues are generic enough to sound scripted; or the person has already named their own issue — then use theirs.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/founder-led · acv=low/mid/high · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=saas/service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — prepared phrasing for the generic CRM example.

### [REAL:FEWQ] Ask three to five questions, chosen for importance not completeness
**Move:** Prepare the small number of questions that, answered thoroughly, would matter most — and separately prepare for the questions you expect *from* the client, since most are predictable. Open by asking permission and framing relevance ("if we could only cover one to three key issues that would make this a good use of your time, what should we cover?") or by inviting a high-level view of their top three-to-five business challenges over the next one to two years.
**APPLY WHEN:** First meetings with limited time; executive access.
**AVOID WHEN:** Deep qualification sessions where broader coverage is agreed — this is a first-call constraint, not a universal cap.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=low/mid/high · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=service/consulting/saas · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — Randy as a buyer handed 53 questions for a one-hour meeting.

### [REAL:INITYL] Prepared responses to first-contact yellow lights
**Move:** Answer the three standard brush-offs with an easy exit built in. "I'm busy, call me later" → offer a quick overview now so they can decide whether a callback is even wanted, explicitly not wanting to waste their time. "We're happy with our current partner" → good to hear; in the interest of continuous improvement, is there anything the partner does not do, or not do well, worth exploring — and no is an okay answer. "Not interested" → thank them for being direct, then ask out of curiosity whether it is because they are not experiencing these issues, the timing is bad, or it is already covered.
**APPLY WHEN:** Cold-ish first contact, referral calls, early screening.
**AVOID WHEN:** The prospect has explicitly asked not to be contacted — persistence there becomes the behaviour the chapter opens by condemning.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/founder-led · acv=low/mid/high · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=service/consulting/saas · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — three scripted responses plus a deal-specific set.

### [REAL:CYCLESHORT] Compress the sales cycle deliberately
**Move:** Ask what steps could be condensed and what, done before the first meeting, would shorten the cycle for both sides: give the client homework, request information in advance, interview attendees beforehand about what would make the meeting worth their time, agree the End in Mind and key beliefs before the meeting. Propose next steps that are jointly taken, low risk and low investment for both parties, continuing only while everything keeps making sense.
**APPLY WHEN:** Long-cycle consultative sales; whenever both sides are motivated to reach a decision fast.
**AVOID WHEN:** Compression removes the buyer's genuine internal steps — the point is removing waste, not skipping decisions.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — We-Do-It condensed four planned steps into one meeting because both sides did homework in advance.

### [REAL:NOCOLD] Get a referral instead of calling cold
**Move:** Never contact a top prospect without a referral — either someone calls on your behalf or lets you use their name. Research the individual as well as the company and trace the web of past and present connections; be willing to wait months for a referral rather than spend a low-probability cold call. Sort sources into three tiers: people who know you well (best but few), people you know (accessible and willing but often politely inert), and people you know of (many, harder to reach, less motivated).
**APPLY WHEN:** Executive-level targets; a short prioritized prospect list where waiting is affordable.
**AVOID WHEN:** Time-boxed campaigns or markets with no connection path at all — though the authors claim they always find one.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/founder-led · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — authors claim a referral every time, even in industries where they start knowing nobody; appointment-rate percentages come from a named white paper.

### [REAL:EASYNO] Ask for the referral with an easy no
**Move:** Make the End in Mind of the referral call whether the person would feel *comfortable* referring you — and make no an easy answer, on the claim that this paradoxically raises the yes rate. After catching up, name it as a possible favour, name the target person, ask whether the name rings a bell, then explicitly disclaim any assumption that they should refer you and offer a quick overview so they can decide.
**APPLY WHEN:** Asking tier-two and tier-three sources; anyone whose reputation is on the line.
**AVOID WHEN:** The easy-out is performative — a source who senses pressure behind it agrees politely and does nothing.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/founder-led · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — scripted approach from the authors' own prospecting.

### [REAL:TRIALRUN] Rehearse your reasoning on the referral source
**Move:** Try out with the source the exact reasoning you intend to give the prospect. Four claimed payoffs: a low-risk trial run, an informed decision by the person lending their name, refinement of your thinking, and additional names you had not considered. Treat the referral call with at least as much care as the client call.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any warm-intro path, especially where the source is senior or protective of the relationship.
**AVOID WHEN:** The source has no context or interest — the rehearsal then just consumes goodwill.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/founder-led · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — standard practice with four enumerated benefits.

### [REAL:REFSCRIPT] Hand the referrer a suggested script
**Move:** If the source will call or write on your behalf, supply a suggested structure (not to be read verbatim) covering the same three beliefs as the first meeting: there is something worthwhile to discuss, it will be pleasant rather than painful, their time will be respected. The model text names why the referrer would not pass on the name without reason, what the person does and for what kind of company, that measurable results exist elsewhere though applicability is unknown, a character vouch, and an explicit promise to keep the call short if there is no reason to talk — closing with a yes/no request.
**APPLY WHEN:** The source has agreed to make the introduction themselves.
**AVOID WHEN:** The source is senior enough to be insulted by a script — walk it through verbally instead.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/founder-led · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — a reusable template the authors provide.

### [REAL:SOURCEASCLIENT] Treat the referral source like a client
**Move:** When a source offers a quick introduction that smells like a polite do-nothing, thank them and propose a working meeting first — buy them lunch, let them meet you, hear the problems you solve and results you produce, then decide whether the introduction still makes sense. Claimed payoff: a source who can represent and position you accurately, and who often volunteers further introductions.
**APPLY WHEN:** The source is well connected but knows little about your business; the target is a CEO-level contact.
**AVOID WHEN:** The source already knows your work well — the extra step wastes their time.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/founder-led · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — Randy's chain: friend in sales → head of investments → two-hour meeting instead of lunch → CEO appointment → completed engagement, plus two further CEO introductions offered.

### [REAL:PRECALL] Pre-call the prospect before the scheduled meeting
**Move:** Even with a meeting booked, ask the source to arrange a short call first. On it: acknowledge their time, say you do not want them to invest a meeting only to discover relevance and then need a second one, ask to share the hypothesis briefly, and let them decide whether the meeting is the right use of time. Present the researched impact number with an explicit caveat that outside data is incomplete; when they reject the number, ask what they think a realistic one is and use their counter-number as the qualifying threshold. Then ask who needs to participate and propose developing the agenda together.
**APPLY WHEN:** Executive meetings with travel or high time cost; where a hypothesis can be tested cheaply beforehand.
**AVOID WHEN:** The source resists — they may not see why a call is needed before an agreed meeting — and the relationship cannot absorb the friction.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/founder-led · acv=high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — the transcribed NewLogo CFO call; the $36M estimate was rejected outright and the CFO volunteered that $3M would still be worth recovering.

### [REAL:HIGHRISK5] Script and rehearse the High Risk Five
**Move:** Treat the first five minutes of a call or meeting as the highest-leverage time and script it, then practise until it sounds natural rather than read. Four steps: begin early (it takes longer than expected); write it (a written meeting plan costs less than pursuing low-probability opportunities and helps exit them early, shorten the cycle and raise win rate); say it out loud, ideally with another person; visualize the successful call in detail beforehand and re-create that state right before the real one.
**APPLY WHEN:** First contact with a prioritized prospect; team-sold meetings.
**AVOID WHEN:** Rehearsal is last-minute (a named standard error), or the script is delivered as a script rather than internalized.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/founder-led · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the authors' preparation regimen; the visualization component is asserted.

### [REAL:OPENSTMT] Four-part opening statement
**Move:** Build the opening to connect personally, state the purpose plainly, create curiosity or interest quickly, and transition into inquiry — then move to key beliefs, proof or proposed action, yellow lights, and the End in Mind. Match wording to the executive's style. The worked example inverts expectations deliberately: no slide deck, no company monologue, an explicit statement that the agenda leads to a yes-or-no and that a no does no harm, a check that this matches what they want to accomplish, and a request to start with questions.
**APPLY WHEN:** Opening a first substantive meeting, especially with a blunt, time-pressured executive.
**AVOID WHEN:** Monologue creep, excessive detail, or an all-about-us opening — the four named common errors.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/founder-led · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — the NewLogo opening statement is the single worked case.

### [REAL:REFNETWORK] Build a referral network so people call you
**Move:** Six actions: find the people who take pride in being a go-to connector; become one yourself by knowing and recommending the best providers in your market; identify people who would both refer you and have someone to refer, ask permission to be in your network and keep them updated; meet each source in person or by phone aiming for one strong referral per meeting, thank every referral immediately even when nothing sells (handwritten note, visit, or call with a small token) and report outcomes back; keep adding sources, including clients who came via referral; make yourself known through associations, speaking, volunteering, writing, columns, blogs, webinars or podcasts — joining only where you will contribute without expecting return. Be specific about the problem you solve rather than the product you sell.
**APPLY WHEN:** Building a durable personal pipeline; the network is framed as your responsibility and portable across employers.
**AVOID WHEN:** Joining communities transactionally — intent counts more than technique here too.
**Deal-gate:** motion=inbound/founder-led · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — things the authors have done themselves or heard from others; all cadence figures self-reported.

### Qualifying — entry discipline

### [REAL:INFO-FLOW-QUAL] Qualify on the flow of meaningful information
**Move:** Score each open deal by how much specific, client-stated information you actually hold across Opportunity, Resources and Decisions — refusing to count your own inferences as data. Ask explicitly whether the client uttered confirming words about the issue's importance, evidence and impact, then reallocate time from information-poor deals to information-rich ones.
**APPLY WHEN:** Pipeline review, forecast calls, any moment of deciding where to spend pursuit resources.
**AVOID WHEN:** Deals where low disclosure is structurally normal (regulated or security-constrained procurement) — the metric misreads confidentiality as disinterest.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — primary qualification tool in a multi-billion-dollar scrub of ~400 deals.

### [REAL:RFP-HUMAN-RULE] No RFP response without access to a human
**Move:** Institute a bidding rule: nobody responds to an RFP unless permitted to speak with at least one person intimately familiar with the business issues the RFP is meant to address. Treat refusal of contact as the qualification answer.
**APPLY WHEN:** Inbound, unsolicited or no-contact RFPs, especially where response cost is material.
**AVOID WHEN:** Response cost is trivial, or a rule-break is a deliberate, priced strategic exception — the authors note the rule was sometimes broken and still served the company.
**Deal-gate:** motion=inbound · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=service/consulting · market=enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — one client's own 18-month audit plus the policy that followed.

### [REAL:KEYWORD-MEANING] Ask what the client's key word means to them
**Move:** When the client uses a loaded term (ERP, data warehousing, supply-chain optimization, leadership), do not launch into capability. Normalize the question first — note that the term means different things to different organizations — then ask what it means to them in the context of what they are trying to achieve.
**APPLY WHEN:** First substantive conversation; any time an abstraction is doing heavy lifting in the client's request.
**AVOID WHEN:** Overused within one call — asking for definitions of every term stalls the conversation and looks like evasion of a direct question.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=low/mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=saas/service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — workshop word-overlap exercise repeated over years with tens of thousands of consultants.

### [REAL:UNASKED-Q] Ask the question you are avoiding
**Move:** Track the questions that form in your mind and die there — the ones that decide whether there is a deal at all. Two steps: awareness (which questions am I not asking?) and choice (how can I ask this so both of us feel fine about it?). When you notice yourself about to substitute a safe factual question (headcount, software version), ask the hard one instead in soft, non-accusatory wording, and treat your own discomfort rather than the client's as the source of the flinch. Practise on low-risk conversations and notice which questions you still refuse to ask.
**APPLY WHEN:** An obvious, awkward, business-critical unknown persists past the second meeting; discovery is one or two levels from a measurable; rapport feels "not yet earned."
**AVOID WHEN:** Asked bluntly with emotional charge — the technique is hard question, soft delivery; also when you have no diagnostic intent and are just pressing for leverage, or the question would breach an explicit regulated/legal boundary.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=low/mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — repeated field challenge framed with Argyris on making the undiscussable discussable, plus win/loss interviews where buyers said they would have welcomed the question.

### [REAL:YELLOW-SLOW] Slow down for yellow lights
**Move:** Treat doubts, stalls, concerns, objections, incongruent tone and your own gut unease as signals to decelerate, not accelerate. The rule: if you see it, hear it or feel it, find a tactful way to say it. Convert the yellow to an explicit green or red rather than leaving it live.
**APPLY WHEN:** Early in a long, expensive pursuit — the cheapest moment to hit a red light is the first one available.
**AVOID WHEN:** Ego is running the meeting (bringing a VIP guest, protecting appearances) — the authors' own failure case shows this is exactly what gets abandoned then.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — win/loss analysis of a billion-dollar outsourcing pursuit plus repeated coaching cases.

### [REAL:STATE-OBVIOUS] Three-part statement of the obvious
**Move:** Name the yellow light in three beats without emotional charge: flag it (I have a concern / I'm confused / we may have a problem); state the concern concretely, including the case where even a perfect solution would not be adopted; ask what they think should happen next. Read their answer as green or red. Works on hard yellows (missing feature, no budget, incumbent love) and soft ones (watch-glancing, disengagement).
**APPLY WHEN:** A deal-relevant doubt exists and you have enough rapport that naming it will be heard as candour.
**AVOID WHEN:** Used to manufacture urgency or corner the buyer; mismatched to buyers who read direct naming of doubt as weakness or pressure.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=low/mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — pattern taught across the coursework with worked examples.

### [REAL:TWENTYQ] The 20-question qualifying checklist
**Move:** Carry the fixed set as a pre-call reference. Opportunity (1-9): all issues the solution addresses; what lets them know it is a problem; what it costs the organization; how success will be recognised; the payoff of success; who or what else is affected; the organizational big picture; what stopped resolution before and what might stop it in future; then summarize and ask what was missed. Resources (10-12): when they hope to start; how effort divides between their people and yours (a rough ratio such as 70/30); whether a budget is established. Decisions (13-20): all steps to a good decision with yes or no both acceptable; what decision each step makes; when each occurs; who is involved; how the decision makers will decide and on what criteria — learned directly from those people, not about them; how they will choose among alternatives; who gains or loses if adopted; how personally committed your contact is.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any consultative qualification; deal reviews; coaching.
**AVOID WHEN:** Run as an interrogation list in one sitting — the book's own 53-questions anecdote is the warning.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=low/mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the consolidated FranklinCovey method taught across the practice.
### Qualifying — Opportunity (issues, evidence, impact, context, constraints)

### [REAL:LSM] Listen–Soften–Move off the solution
**Move:** Three beats when the client opens with a requested solution. Listen to exactly how they phrase it. Soften — because answering a question with a question breaks the expected pattern, open with a bridge (glad to; to keep this relevant to your situation; I could talk for hours and that may not be what you want). Move — ask what problems the missing solution is causing, or ask them to stand a year in the future with the solution working and describe what the business can then do that it cannot do today.
**APPLY WHEN:** The client names a solution, asks for a capability pitch, or hands you an RFP-shaped request.
**AVOID WHEN:** The buyer has genuinely completed their own diagnosis and is buying a commodity on price and speed; also when repeated deflection starts to look like you cannot answer the question.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=low/mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=saas/service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the book's signature move, taught with worked ERP and supply-chain dialogues across the global coursework.

### [REAL:PAIN-GAIN-READ] Read pain versus gain language
**Move:** Classify the client's own vocabulary. Pain-motivated buyers list frustrations in physical terms — bleeding, killing us, headache, nightmare, like pulling teeth. Gain-motivated buyers speak in vision terms — what we'd like to see, what's possible, our vision, what we're excited about. Match your subsequent questions and framing to whichever register they used.
**APPLY WHEN:** Immediately after the move-off-solution question, while collecting issues.
**AVOID WHEN:** Treated as a fixed buyer type — the same person shifts register across issues and across stakeholders.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=low/mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — repeated field observation, no measurement offered.

### [REAL:ALL-ISSUES] Get the whole list before going deep on any one
**Move:** Resist depth on the first issue mentioned; collect the complete list first, acknowledging each item verbally and non-verbally so it does not feel like robotic intake. Periodically summarize back in their words. Add candidate issues non-leadingly ("other clients facing this have also been wrestling with X — has that been an issue for you?"). Test completeness: if you addressed all of these and nothing else, would that be a solution that exactly met your needs?
**APPLY WHEN:** The client has moved off the solution and started naming issues.
**AVOID WHEN:** The client is unwilling or short on time — the discipline holds only as long as they keep listing.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=low/mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the stated risks (never getting the full list, depth on the wrong issue, wasted time) are argued, not measured.

### [REAL:PRIORITIZE] Make the client rank leverage
**Move:** Acknowledge that everything on the list is important and interrelated, then ask which one carries the most leverage or impact. If they cannot choose, agree all are important, promise to cover all, and ask which to discuss first. The top one or two priorities is enough to start depth work.
**APPLY WHEN:** Right after the issue list is complete, before evidence gathering.
**AVOID WHEN:** With a single stakeholder standing in for a committee — their ranking may not survive contact with the other decision influencers.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=low/mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — paired with an unsourced 80/20 rule of thumb.

### [REAL:FIVE-GOLDEN] The Five Golden Questions on a hard issue
**Move:** For any issue with numbers behind it, walk five questions: how do you measure it; what is it now; what would you like it to be; what is the value of the difference; what is that worth over a reasonable management horizon (2-3 years). Question five is your confirming arithmetic, not homework for them. Optionally insert the requested solution between two and three ("with the software in place and working as you hope, what output would you expect?"). Skipping question one and opening with "what is it now?" is fine when the measure is obvious.
**APPLY WHEN:** The prioritized issue is hard — sales, costs, inventory turns, reject rates, productivity — or your measurable-alert fires on a number the client dropped in passing.
**AVOID WHEN:** The issue is genuinely soft; forcing monetization too early skips the peeling work and produces numbers the client does not believe.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the core taught questioning sequence, illustrated with margin and quality dialogues.

### [REAL:ENVELOPE-MATH] Back-of-the-envelope math, chunked down or rolled up
**Move:** Aim for order of magnitude, not accounting precision, using ballpark/rough/best-guess language, and convert every percentage and ratio into money. Two routes: chunk it down — get unit cost and multiply through volume step by step out loud; or roll it up — ask for the annual total in ranges, bracket it ("closer to two or three, or closer to eight or nine?"), then apply the improvement fraction. Finish by extending to the 2-3 year horizon.
**APPLY WHEN:** The client cannot do the arithmetic in their head, which is most of the time once you ask for the value of the difference.
**AVOID WHEN:** The estimate is fragile enough that a wrong order of magnitude would discredit you; and never present it as precise.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — two worked dialogues plus general field practice.

### [REAL:CLIENT-OWNS-MATH] Make the number theirs, and be the conservative party
**Move:** Have the client run the numbers with you or for you, or narrate your own arithmetic step by step and ask "does that sound right?". Never punch a calculator silently and announce a finding. When the total comes out large, do not gloat — say it seems like a large number and ask whether it is realistic, which lets them either revise it down to something they believe or confirm it is understated.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any monetization step during evidence and impact.
**AVOID WHEN:** Never — but note the failure mode it prevents: a number you own is your problem, not theirs.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — repeated coaching correction of a common consultant error.

### [REAL:SOFT-EVIDENCE-QS] How/What question bank for soft issues
**Move:** For unmeasurable issues, build evidence with how- and what-framed questions. Problems: how does it show up as a problem; how did the company become convinced it is one; if we walked the floor, what would convince us; how would you prove it to the CFO; when you can't do X, then what happens. Results: how specifically would you measure success; how would you and others know we succeeded; have you thought about how you'd measure return; what indicators move; what's your punch list for success; how should we prove to stakeholders this was a good idea.
**APPLY WHEN:** The priority issue is morale, communication, competitive position, teamwork, reputation, leadership.
**AVOID WHEN:** Fired as a checklist — the bank is a menu, and reading it out sequentially turns dialogue into interrogation.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=low/mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — question sets drawn from the taught curriculum.

### [REAL:PEEL-ONION] Peel for pain / peel for gain until a hard measure appears
**Move:** Take a soft issue down layer by layer to its business or emotional driver. Peel for pain: when that happens, then what happens; what does that affect; what are the consequences; how does that hit the bottom line; worst case if it never changes. Peel for gain: if you had that, what would it let you do; what does that get you; where do the benefits show up; best case, what is the ultimate gain. When two consequences surface, ask which concerns them more and follow that branch. Stop at a hard measure (then switch to the Five Golden Questions), a genuine emotional driver, or both — and return later for the benefits you deferred.
**APPLY WHEN:** The stated issue is an abstraction ("holistic view of the customer", "improved communication") that cannot be priced as stated.
**AVOID WHEN:** Peeling becomes an interrogation chain — narrate understanding between layers rather than stacking bare questions; stop if layers produce defensiveness rather than clarity.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — two full worked dialogues (customer view → dollars per initial sale; communication → forecast accuracy → inventory, markdowns, stock-outs).

### [REAL:SCALE10] Qualify on a 1-10 scale when you cannot quantify
**Move:** Ask the client to place the issue on a 1-10 scale with both anchors defined out loud — 10 meaning mission-critical and unavoidable, 1 meaning annoying but survivable. Read 8-10 as motivating, 7 as "keep talking, not a no-brainer," 6 or below as a yellow light.
**APPLY WHEN:** The issue is soft or intangible, or the client lacks the patience, data, discipline or trust to quantify in currency.
**AVOID WHEN:** A hard currency number is obtainable — the scale is a fallback, not a substitute; and a self-reported 9 is not validated evidence.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=service/consulting/saas · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the authors' standing rule across client engagements.

### [REAL:EVIDPATH] Route the missing evidence
**Move:** When the client has no evidence, run the fork explicitly: does someone else have it (then ask who and when you can talk to them), or does nobody? If nobody, ask whether the evidence matters for building the business case; if it matters, ask whether they can get it themselves or need help. That last question is where a paid diagnostic sells itself.
**APPLY WHEN:** Issues named but unmeasured; the buyer will need internal funding from people who do not live with the problem daily.
**AVOID WHEN:** The evidence question is a pretext to sell a study you already decided to sell; or the buyer already has adequate data and the exercise only delays.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — supported only by the authors' own 80% rule of thumb, not an outside dataset.

### [REAL:TAKEAWAY] Take the solution away when impact looks small
**Move:** Say plainly that the problem may be cheaper than the cure, and hand them the do-nothing option: keep the incumbent, do it in-house, watch it for two years, eliminate risk by not running the project. Then stop and watch which way they move — release or fight. A client who fights to keep it has sold themselves; one who lets it go was never going to buy.
**APPLY WHEN:** Quantified impact is small relative to the likely fee; the issue rates around 5 on the 1-10 scale; you would genuinely be willing to walk.
**AVOID WHEN:** You are not actually prepared to lose the deal — it then reads as a manipulation gambit and dies on incongruence; also poor fit for transactional buying where no dialogue is expected.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — sample phrasings and the authors' observations, no measured win-rate data.

### [REAL:IMPACTGAP] Close a disputed-impact gap with questions plus data
**Move:** When you read the impact as larger than the client does, say so, ask further questions, and supply the missing computation (e.g. direct and opportunity cost of 38% turnover against a 20% industry benchmark) with an explicitly open mind about being wrong. Accept three outcomes: you agree it is small and exit gracefully, they agree it is big and you continue, or you agree to disagree and hold at yellow.
**APPLY WHEN:** The client has the raw statistic but attributes no cost to it.
**AVOID WHEN:** The aim has shifted to winning the argument — a problem is not a problem until the client says it is.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — a single turnover example.

### [REAL:CONTEXT2] Run both context axes
**Move:** Ask how the initiative connects to the big picture (mission, values, current strategies and initiatives, outside and inside pressures, politics), then ask who or what else is affected — this department only or others, this process only or others. Use the answers to find extra opportunities, additional stakeholders whose names had not surfaced, and partners to refer when you cannot help.
**APPLY WHEN:** Impact is established and the solution touches shared processes or crosses departments.
**AVOID WHEN:** Single-owner, contained purchase with no cross-functional footprint — the widening reads as padding.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** assertion — the price-sensitivity and alignment claims are stated without support.

### [REAL:CONSTRAINTQ] The constraints question
**Move:** For a long-running situation ask what has stopped the organization from resolving this before now; for a brand-new initiative ask what, if anything, might prevent successful implementation. Sort answers into good constraints (barriers no longer applying; capacity, expertise, focus the client lacks) and bad constraints (unfunded, no exec buy-in, a group with an interest in killing it, politics, too complicated, outranked by other priorities).
**APPLY WHEN:** You and the client have jointly concluded the impact is substantial.
**AVOID WHEN:** Never skip it out of fear the client will realize they can self-serve — if that is their best choice they reach it anyway, later and more expensively for you.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — a standard diagnostic step in the method.

### [REAL:WHATSDIFF] "What is different this time?"
**Move:** On a bad constraint, ask what has changed since the last failure. If nothing is different, name the concern out loud — that even a perfect solution will not be adopted because the same force will kill it — and ask what they think should be done. Slow down instead of speeding up; separate adoption risk from execution risk. Frequently the client responds by asking you to help remove the constraint, which is billable work.
**APPLY WHEN:** The constraint that blocked past attempts is still structurally in place.
**AVOID WHEN:** Used to manufacture scope; or the constraint is genuinely dissolved and re-litigating it stalls a green deal.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — repeated coaching pattern; the "more business from removing constraints than from pushing" claim is itself an assertion.

### [REAL:NOMOVE] Diagnose a client who will not move off the solution
**Move:** Treat refusal to discuss underlying problems as a yellow light and test five causes before pushing: wrong people (they do not know the problems), not a real opportunity (they do not intend to hire you), your own communication failed (weak IQ case, insufficient EQ/safety, clumsy wording), the purchase is legally or regulatorily mandated (structure around compliance criteria instead), or the solution is already chosen (structure around decision criteria instead).
**APPLY WHEN:** Two or more invitations to move off the stated solution are declined.
**AVOID WHEN:** You have not yet made one clean, well-worded attempt — the default assumption should include your own execution.
**Deal-gate:** motion=inbound/outbound/channel · acv=low/mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting/product · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — enumerated failure taxonomy from field coaching.

### [REAL:CRITERIA2] Two-criteria conversation for a predetermined solution
**Move:** When the product choice is locked, run the same structured conversation over two lists instead: criteria for an ideal implementation, and criteria for an ideal solution provider ("what will be most important to you about the people you work with?"). Complete the list, then take each criterion through evidence, impact, context and constraints — including intangibles like "better service": past evidence of bad service, its impact, how future success will be measured, whether those measurements exist, whether they want help setting them up.
**APPLY WHEN:** Client insists on a named product or solution; RFP-driven or compliance-driven purchase.
**AVOID WHEN:** Accepting the predetermined solution carries risk you have not priced — the authors flag this explicitly.
**Deal-gate:** motion=inbound/channel · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=product/saas/service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the standard fallback structure.

### [REAL:NINEQ] The nine-inquiry checklist
**Move:** Before leaving Opportunity, confirm answers to all nine: all issues the solution must resolve; what tells them it is a problem (problem evidence); business impact of the problem; how success would be measured (result evidence); payoff if success is achieved (result impact); who or what else is affected (operational context); the big picture (organizational context); what has stopped resolution in the past (constraints); and a summary check — did I get it right, did I leave anything out.
**APPLY WHEN:** As a pre-Resources gate on any complex opportunity.
**AVOID WHEN:** Short transactional cycles where the buyer has no tolerance for a nine-part diagnosis.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the book's canonical structure, taught as a method.

### Qualifying — Resources (time, people, money)

### [REAL:TIMEQ] Ask the timetable question and test the extremes
**Move:** Ask by what date they hope to have results in place, or when they hoped to start, or what time frame you are working within. Then test extremes. If the date is far off relative to a quantified loss, say you are confused, put the arithmetic on the table (a $10M/yr problem deferred three years is $30M lost) and ask what makes that acceptable. If the date is sooner than you can deliver, find out what is driving it — political, financial or organizational reasons may be satisfiable another way, or the work may be phaseable — and if genuinely impossible, name the concern and ask whether it is worth continuing on a longer timeline.
**APPLY WHEN:** After impact is quantified; you have enough rapport and enough of the client's own numbers to challenge with.
**AVOID WHEN:** Opportunity was skipped or shallow — without their financial assumptions the challenge has nothing to stand on and reads as pressure.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — standard qualifying step with illustrative scripts.

### [REAL:PEOPLESPLIT] Test the division of labor at both extremes
**Move:** Ask at a high level how effort and responsibility split between their company and yours, and which of their people are critical and will actually be available. If they will not commit critical participation (e.g. top management), state the concern and ask what would have to happen to secure it. If they want to own critical parts you doubt they can execute, use the responsibility framing: are you managing the project and accountable for completion with their people effectively subcontracted to you, or are they accountable and subcontracting you? If the former, ask for the same authority to remove and replace underperformers on their side that they would expect on yours.
**APPLY WHEN:** Delivery outcomes depend on client-side execution; the client wants to self-perform a load-bearing piece.
**AVOID WHEN:** Handled without care — it implicitly says their people may not do a good job; and it is wrong for deals with no delivery dependency.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — repeated qualifying pattern with scripted wording.

### [REAL:MONEY3] The Three-Part Response on investment level
**Move:** Open money with "have you thought about what level of investment is appropriate for the results we discussed?" or "have you established a budget?" Then, against any predictable answer (no budget / you tell me / money's no problem / I can't disclose / I don't know), give the same three-part structure: you do not know what it will cost, every situation is unique; other companies in similar situations who successfully achieved these results tend to invest between X and Y — phrased as what *others invested*, whether with you, a competitor or on their own, not as what you charge; can they see themselves falling somewhere in that range. Keep Y no more than 25-50% above X. With no realistic range yet, get permission to run rough numbers with your team and call back before anyone builds a proposal. On "I don't know," ask who would know and try the same range on that person.
**APPLY WHEN:** After impact is quantified and before any Exact Solution or proposal work, with the person appropriate to the decision.
**AVOID WHEN:** Used as a price quote — this is value justification, not pricing, and confusing the two invites premature negotiation. A range like $100K-$10M is not a range.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — reported to work in most cases; counterexamples are consultants who spent more on a proposal than the client's whole budget.

### [REAL:HOWNUMBER] "How did you come up with that number?"
**Move:** When the client's figure is below yours, ask how they arrived at it and sort the answer into logistics or value. Logistics ("that's all that's left this year," "that's all I'm authorized to spend," "that's all we have to get started") → ask what next year's budget holds, who is authorized for the larger amount, where remaining funding would come from. Value ("that's all we think it's worth") → name the yellow light, state that you believe the results are achievable but not below your range, and ask what they think you should do.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any budget mismatch surfaced in Resources.
**AVOID WHEN:** Arguing value when the issue is logistics, or exploring logistics when the issue is value — the diagnostic question is what keeps you from picking wrong.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — recurring branch structure in the authors' money conversation.

### [REAL:BELIEF3] Name the three beliefs behind a value gap
**Move:** When you are far apart on worth, state that one of three things is usually happening — they do not believe in the value discussed, they do not believe *your* work will produce it, or they believe they can get the same value elsewhere for less — and ask which. Then: revisit the numbers together for belief one; invite the concerns openly for belief two; for belief three, agree they *should* buy cheaper if results are truly identical, and offer to spend about half an hour reviewing the competing proposal (nothing proprietary, just scope and promised results), recommending the competitor if it matches and flagging radical differences if it does not. If they still want your proposal anyway, say it makes no sense to produce one that is doomed unless you can jointly find a difference that makes a difference.
**APPLY WHEN:** The mismatch is about worth rather than logistics, and impact was already established as far exceeding the investment.
**AVOID WHEN:** Early in the relationship, before an impact number exists — the move depends on the value/investment gap being visible to both.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — dialogue examples only.

### Qualifying — Decisions (process, access, criteria)

### [REAL:GRID] Work the grid
**Move:** Build a four-column map with the client: the discrete go/no-go steps to a confident yes or no; the specific decision made at each step (what recommendation actually travels forward — a shortlist, a pros/cons pack, or a single recommendation with justification); a target date for each; and who is involved in each. Do not ask role or rationale yet — get that directly from each person. Ask for the steps *before* asking who decides, because a "who decides" question implies the person in front of you is unimportant, invites an inflated "I do," and yields incomplete lists when the steps are fuzzy in their own mind. Map roles afterwards: initiator, gatekeeper, champion, influencer, user, decision maker, ratifier — people often hold several.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any multi-stakeholder purchase; committee or staged selection processes.
**AVOID WHEN:** Solo buyer with authority to sign — the grid is overhead.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=service/consulting/saas · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the authors' named method, taught and used with clients; no outcome dataset.

### [REAL:NOISOK] Make "no" an equal answer
**Move:** Frame the steps question so both outcomes are legitimate: what steps will the organization need to take to feel confident saying either "yes, this makes sense, let's do it" or "no, we have better uses for our resources." Say explicitly that no is okay. When the client is clearly going to pick *someone*, ask instead what steps they need to be confident that whatever choice they make is the right one.
**APPLY WHEN:** Eliciting process, dates and stakeholders — and again when setting the End in Mind for a presentation.
**AVOID WHEN:** Never — but it fails if the framing is cosmetic; the moment the client senses they are being steered, information quality drops.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — stated as a consistent behavioural pattern the authors rely on.

### [REAL:STEPSWAP] Influence the sequence, not just record it
**Move:** After mapping the steps, evaluate them twice: would the client make a good decision following this path, and does this path serve you. If not, propose reordering, streamlining, adding a missing step, or a different process — argued from the client's interest. Illustrating case: a several-hundred-million-dollar deal where operations was set to choose the technology and then recommend upward; the seller argued the executive committee should first decide what best serves the business, with operations then validating workability. The buyer agreed, the seller won executive backing that pulled operations along, and the coached team believed the reversal decided the outcome.
**APPLY WHEN:** The default sequence favours a competitor's strength, or puts a technical filter before the business rationale your solution depends on.
**AVOID WHEN:** The reordering serves only you and cannot be defended as better for the client's decision — visible self-interest burns trust with the gatekeeper.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=high · stakeholders=committee · offer=service/consulting/saas · market=enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one large coached deal, outcome attributed by the participants themselves.

### [REAL:RATIFIER] Decision maker or ratifier test
**Move:** To decide whether a senior name is worth political capital, ask: of the last ten recommendations sent to that person, how many were rejected or pushed back for more scrutiny. Roughly four or five means a real decision maker — go see them. About one, or never, means a ratifier — skip. Separately, never equate organizational authority with buying authority.
**APPLY WHEN:** A CEO or EVP sits above a selection committee and access is costly or contested.
**AVOID WHEN:** Substituted for judgment in ambiguous cases — the authors describe making a gut call between a selection committee and an executive group and being right, while a competitor withdrew over the same ambiguity.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=high · stakeholders=committee · offer=service/consulting · market=enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — two competitive war stories, including a competitor who lost by talking only to the EVP while the delegated buyer felt disrespected.

### [REAL:MISSINGWHO] Hunt the stakeholder nobody named
**Move:** Ask every stakeholder who else you should be talking to. Mine the Opportunity answers about context (who else is affected) and constraints (what stopped this before) for names and functions that never appeared on the grid, then raise those names or roles directly and watch the reaction. The goal is to reach them before they form a position, not to meet them for the first time in the presentation room.
**APPLY WHEN:** Committee deals, cross-departmental impact, anything with a political history.
**AVOID WHEN:** Fishing for names purely to expand footprint with no diagnostic purpose.

### [REAL:ADVOCATE] Don't ask for access — advocate for it
**Move:** Never ask "can I see the decision makers" — the reflexive answer is no. State the intent and the logic instead: your only goal is a solution that truly meets their needs; based on their own description, these named people own those needs; you cannot prepare a relevant, intelligent proposal without talking to them; they know their company better than you do — so how should you set that up? Hand the mechanics back to them.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any point where a gatekeeper controls access to the owners of the problem; a single contact is gatekeeping a committee decision.
**AVOID WHEN:** Before you have earned standing through good qualifying — leverage comes from the client already feeling understood; also where the gatekeeper has a stated political reason to block that this script would only harden.

### [REAL:RECIPROC] Equivalence of actions — 30 minutes each
**Move:** Trade explicitly: you will invest substantial time developing and presenting ideas with no guarantee of winning, provided they invest roughly thirty minutes per key stakeholder so those ideas are relevant. Offer phone as an acceptable format. Treat refusal of any equivalence — while still expecting a full proposal — as a strong yellow light, and argue it from the client's own interest: guessing produces worse choices for them, not just for you.
**APPLY WHEN:** A proposal of meaningful cost is being requested; access is being rationed.
**AVOID WHEN:** Low-effort proposals where the exchange is unbalanced in your favour and the ask looks disproportionate.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — presented as a standard negotiating frame.

### [REAL:MATCHLEAD] Match and lead the gatekeeper's objection
**Move:** Meet the objection where it is before moving it — the idea attributed to Milton Erickson, who never resisted a client's starting point — then ask whether the reason for refusing can become the reason for granting. "They're too busy" → agree the last thing anyone wants is to waste their time, which is precisely the risk of an irrelevant group presentation; thirty minutes each, by phone if needed, protects their time. "It wouldn't be fair to other vendors" → their fiduciary duty is fairness to their own shareholders and to getting a solution that fits, and no one can prescribe without diagnosing; competitors who do not think access matters will not ask, so granting it to those who ask is itself fair; alternatively let every vendor take as much stakeholder time as they want and make use of that time part of the selection criteria, since how a vendor sells is a free sample of how they solve. Treat the objection list as finite and rehearse replies.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any predictable refusal (too busy / it's my job / I can fill you in / it's in the RFP / they don't see vendors / fairness / policy / put it in writing).
**AVOID WHEN:** Deployed without the underlying intent — the authors insist intent counts more than technique and that their exact wording will not fit every speaker; build your own language from the rationale.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — recurring objection taxonomy; the Erickson lineage is illustrative, not evidence.

### [REAL:ROLEREV] Role reversal against "I'll interview them for you"
**Move:** Accept the offer graciously, then reverse roles: if you went to their company to gather stakeholder input for them, you would do your best and still deliver filtered, lower-fidelity information stripped of nuance, and you would not ask the questions they would ask. Then shrink the ask to one test: is there a single stakeholder they know well enough that the three of you could talk for about thirty minutes, after which *they* judge whether it was worth it and decide whether to extend it. Risk is one conversation.
**APPLY WHEN:** A sponsor or gatekeeper offers to act as proxy interviewer.
**AVOID WHEN:** The proxy genuinely has the expertise and independence to run the inquiry — rare, and worse when interviewing their own superiors.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — scripted pattern with a stated mechanism (dilution, distortion, generalization).

### [REAL:WRITTENQ] Convert "put it in writing" into a scheduled conversation
**Move:** Concede the written questions but attach a thirty-minute conversation to walk them through — questions sent in advance so nothing is a mystery, the stakeholder spared composing written answers, and both sides able to ask follow-ups. Argue the mechanism: written exchange has no clarification loop and forces additional rounds.
**APPLY WHEN:** Procurement or RFP policy mandates written Q&A.
**AVOID WHEN:** Policy is genuinely closed to examination — then fall back to the access ladder rather than fighting.

**Evidence:** assertion — reasoned from communication mechanics, no cases cited.

### [REAL:BACKDOOR] Leave a back door before you advocate
**Move:** Before making the access argument, hand the decision back explicitly — this is their responsibility, your job is to support their decision, whatever they decide is what you will go with, do they mind if you share some thoughts. Then advocate. If they refuse, you exit without having painted yourself into a corner: thank them for listening, restate that your job is to support them, accept the decision. Frame the whole thing as something to fight *for*, not to fight *about*.
**APPLY WHEN:** Every access push, especially with a gatekeeper who owns the process.
**AVOID WHEN:** You are actually willing to walk and want that visible — the deliberate no-back-door stance is a different, higher-risk move.

**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — standard preparatory framing in the method.

### [REAL:BLOCKED4] Four options when access is blocked
**Move:** Choose deliberately among: (1) *chunk it down* — you do not need agreement to see everyone in every step, only the people in the step you are about to enter, before they decide; within a step ask for one stakeholder the counterpart feels safe with, meet as a threesome, extend only if they judge it valuable, leaving them in control. (2) *Go around* — high risk, and you personally should not do it; have someone senior in your firm make peer contact using the same lines of inquiry, and give the gatekeeper an honest heads-up that you will not be in that meeting. (3) *Decide not to play* — treat blocked access as a fatal flaw and gracefully offer to exit. (4) *Play anyway* — proceed to Exact Solution knowing it will not be exact, demand extra presentation time, and open with an Opportunity dialogue: you have enough background for a good hypothesis but would not make a final recommendation without their perspective; may we ask some questions first.
**APPLY WHEN:** Two or more attempts to resolve the gatekeeper's objection have failed.
**AVOID WHEN:** Going around before exhausting chunking — the political cost can exceed the information gained; the authors frame it as a risk trade, not a default.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=service/consulting · market=enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — enumerated options with stated risk trade-offs.

### [REAL:WALKTALK] Decline to propose rather than guess
**Move:** When access to the true owners is denied on a high-visibility deal, respect the refusal out loud, disagree openly, and decline to submit — on the grounds that guessing wastes everyone's time and risks your firm looking either ignorant or arrogant about something you do not understand. Requires backing from your own management before you do it.
**APPLY WHEN:** High-stakes, high-visibility opportunity; you are a credible provider; guessing genuinely cannot produce a defensible proposal.
**AVOID WHEN:** Without internal cover, or as a bluff — labelled high-risk; the one told case ended with the board reversing the gatekeeper and awarding the business, but the seller spent the night fearing for her career.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=high · stakeholders=committee · offer=service/consulting · market=enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — a single Fortune 100 case with a favourable outcome; survivorship risk half-acknowledged by the authors.

### [REAL:ALTERNATIVES] Put do-nothing and do-it-themselves on the table
**Move:** Name the three real alternatives — do nothing, do it themselves, do it with someone else — and ask about each: is doing nothing an option, are there competing uses of funds that could outrank this, have they considered doing it in-house. For any yes, ask what would drive the decision one way or the other, i.e. the specific criteria. On external competition, ask who they are talking to out of curiosity; if they will not name names, the count and type still inform you — fewer competitors suggests due diligence is done and selection is near, more suggests they are still gathering information, and unusual competitors signal reasons worth understanding. Then get past names to criteria: given every vendor will claim good people and good results, what will they need to see, hear or experience to tell which is genuinely best.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any competitive or discretionary purchase; when criteria are vague abstractions like "creative," "leading edge," "value-added."
**AVOID WHEN:** Competitive focus displaces client focus — competitor knowledge helps only when it illuminates the client's choice.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting/saas · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — standard criteria elicitation with a stated heuristic on competitor counts.

### [REAL:INCUMBENT-HEADON] Name the incumbent preference and price the switch
**Move:** Where an incumbent or insider favourite exists, say so from their side of the table — in their shoes you might also prefer people you already trust — then ask directly whether they have been heading that way, and what the motivation to change actually is (genuine opportunity or a market/price check). Assume a transition cost: parity loses. Ask what they would have to see, hear or experience from you for this to become a genuinely tough choice — and find that out early, then deliver exactly that.
**APPLY WHEN:** A long incumbent relationship, a new executive with prior-firm ties, an RFP that looks like a re-bid, or a suspected compliance third bid.
**AVOID WHEN:** You cannot in fact deliver the differentiator they name — the question invites a commitment you then have to meet.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one observed consultant visit that used the question and won the account; the transition-cost principle itself is asserted without cases.

### [REAL:WINLOSE] Surface who wins and who loses
**Move:** Ask directly whether anyone stands to lose if the solution is adopted, who might be perceived as winning or losing, whether anyone has a vested interest in this *not* happening. Perceived winners are champion candidates; perceived losers may sabotage. Two responses: co-opt (talk to them and look for a way to meet their needs) or counteract (if they will not explore honestly, ask sponsors how to mitigate their influence).
**APPLY WHEN:** Cross-departmental solutions; anything that redistributes budget, headcount or status.
**AVOID WHEN:** Counteracting before attempting co-option — the ordering is deliberate.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=service/consulting/saas · market=enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — recurring political pattern, no data.

### [REAL:PERSONAL] Ask about personal stake
**Move:** With sufficient time and rapport, ask on a 1-10 scale how important it is to them *personally* that the project succeed, and how they would personally win or lose if it is adopted. Use the answers to align individual interest with organizational priority, and where objectives conflict, look for solution design that resolves the conflict — which itself adds value.
**APPLY WHEN:** Late in stakeholder interviews, once trust exists.
**AVOID WHEN:** Early or with thin rapport, where it reads as leverage-hunting; and where the honest use is not alignment but exploitation of private incentive.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=service/consulting · market=enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — standard practice with time and rapport as stated prerequisites.
### Winning — presentation readiness and the Meeting Plan

### [REAL:READYGATE] Readiness gate before any presentation
**Move:** Refuse to present until ORD discussions are complete with the influencers and deciders, so their judging criteria are already known to you, and until you are presenting in person, to the right people, for the right amount of time. Treat presenting to open rather than to close — presenting at the first opportunity, to people not authorized to say yes — as the root cause of low win rates.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any complex opportunity approaching a proposal or demo.
**AVOID WHEN:** Note the organizational counter-pressure: when win rates fall, management often demands *more* proposals, which degrades quality further.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=service/consulting/saas · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — stated as the two major keys to winning consistently; no win-rate dataset.

### [REAL:WEARETHEPROPOSAL] "We are the proposal" — trade paper for a meeting
**Move:** When asked to send a proposal, propose the meeting instead: you believe you understand their objectives, resources and decision process; you want to take it to your team, develop your best thinking on a solution that exactly fits, then have your team meet theirs so they can challenge it with their best thinking; you are confident some of it will excite them and something will be missing or improvable; afterwards you write it up formally and they decide. The written artifact becomes a confirmation letter or agreement, not a selling document. If they insist on writing, work down a ladder: an in-person pre-proposal draft session with one or more stakeholders → a phone review of main elements → agreement to *present*, not merely deliver, the written proposal in person. Only if all fail, mail it — a major yellow light.
**APPLY WHEN:** "Send me a proposal" arrives before any presentation has been scheduled.
**AVOID WHEN:** Orals or demos already follow proposals as standard process, so the argument is unnecessary; and where policy is genuinely fixed, use the ladder rather than resisting.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — argued from what in-person adds (vocal and visual cues, interaction, flexibility, rapport, closure); the five-minutes-per-proposal statistic is an unnamed study.

### [REAL:RIGHTROOM] Present only when the deciders are in the room
**Move:** Use the leverage earned in qualifying — a client who feels understood and whose curiosity has not been prematurely satisfied — to insist that everyone necessary to the decision attends; where that is impossible in one meeting, get advance agreement to meet the remainder subsequently. Do not let a sponsor carry your case upward: they cannot answer questions with your authority or credibility, and are especially unlikely to push back on their own superiors' faulty assumptions.
**APPLY WHEN:** A key decision maker drops out, or the client proposes to relay your pitch internally.
**AVOID WHEN:** The cost of insisting is unaffordable and no decision authority is at stake — the illustrating case is expensive: four executives flew Chicago→London, learned the CEO had left for a customer emergency, declined to present to the leadership team alone, flew home, returned two weeks later and won a substantial account.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=high · stakeholders=committee · offer=service/consulting · market=enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one told case plus reasoning.

### [REAL:ENOUGHTIME] Negotiate enough presentation time
**Move:** Refuse the default hour (45 minutes of talk, 15 for questions, in practice starting late and overrunning until Q&A is sacrificed and no decision happens). Argue that the riskiest point in the buyer's process is the worst place to constrain dialogue, and that if time is the real concern, spending enough to decide well is the best use of it. If you were denied stakeholder access beforehand, build in *more* time so mutual agreement on Opportunity and decision criteria can be reached before the solution is discussed.
**APPLY WHEN:** Scheduling any final or decision-enabling presentation.
**AVOID WHEN:** A hard scheduling constraint is genuine — then reduce content, not the decision step.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — repeated observation of how allotted-hour meetings end.

### [REAL:ENDINMIND] Start with the End in Mind and get agreement on it
**Move:** Define exactly what you want the client to say, do or decide by the end, then secure their agreement to that outcome — ideally by testing the agenda before the meeting, otherwise at the very start. Give the conclusion up front and spend the meeting showing how you got there. Calibrate to stage: initiating ≈ "should we be talking?", qualifying ≈ "should we keep talking?", enabling decisions ≈ "should you do this with us?". In competitive finals prefer "is this a solution that truly meets your needs?" or "do you feel you have all the information to make a well-informed decision?" over asking them to pick you on the spot. Word it as a genuine two-way choice.
**APPLY WHEN:** Every client meeting and call, not only finals.
**AVOID WHEN:** Four named deficiencies — no clear End in Mind; one that overreaches for the stage; one worded as the answer you want ("we're confident you'll say you want to work with us"), which provokes resistance; and failure to get agreement before or at the start, illustrated by the authors' own CEO meeting where a rambling sponsor intro swallowed the plan and nothing was decided but "keep talking."
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting/saas · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — core taught format, with the authors' own failure as the cautionary case.

### [REAL:STEPSTONES] Key beliefs as stepping stones — nothing extra
**Move:** Build 3-7 stepping stones between the opening and the End in Mind, preferring the *client's* beliefs (what they must intellectually and emotionally believe before deciding) and adding your own only where necessary. Elicit them during stakeholder interviews: what would you want covered that lets you make a good decision whether you choose us or someone else; what would you like to see, hear or experience. Typical solution beliefs: it resolves their problems and produces their results; it is implementable with available resources; it meets key stakeholders' criteria; the economics are compelling; it beats the alternatives. Typical provider beliefs: competent, credible, compatible. Give only what each belief needs — extra information wastes time, confuses, and creates doubts and objections that would never otherwise have existed.
**APPLY WHEN:** Structuring any decision-enabling presentation.
**AVOID WHEN:** Dumping information in one glut with questions held to the end; making content about your firm rather than their issues; leaving information unlinked to their criteria and the End in Mind.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting/saas · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — format reported successful across diverse applications worldwide; no comparative data.

### [REAL:CHECKOFF] Ask for a decision on each belief as you go
**Move:** For each key belief, present the proof or proposed action, then explicitly check it off with the client rather than waiting for the end — otherwise you run yellow lights or lose green-light momentum. Size the mechanism to the group: a free-form question for one or two people ("how comfortable are you that this has been addressed?", "we should check this off or know that we can't — what would you like to do?"); a 1-10 scale with defined anchors; red/yellow/green cards or a verbal show of colours; fist-to-five finger votes; anonymous electronic voting pads for large groups. Plan in advance how you will ask, per belief and for the final End in Mind.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any presentation built on key beliefs; multi-stakeholder rooms where silent dissent is likely.
**AVOID WHEN:** Asked inappropriately it reads as manipulative or coercive, and clients may decide against you even while liking the solution; note also that some people never award a 10, so 8-9 may be the ceiling and may be enough.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting/saas · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — multiple mechanisms the authors report using with clients.

### [REAL:REDIRECT] Find the real question before answering
**Move:** Treat a client question as possibly masking the real one and use redirection = listening + softening + a redirection question. Listen to their exact wording to shape your question; soften first so you do not break the expected question→answer pattern ("good question…", "several things come to mind…", "the short answer is…", or a brief factual answer), then redirect ("I sense you're asking for a reason — is company size important to your decision?"; "typically three to six months — do you have time pressures we should know about?"). Answering selectively with a question also keeps the client talking rather than listening to you. Hard caveat: if they ask the same question twice, that *is* the real question — answer it as well as you can. In groups, resist the reflex to answer at all: questions may be asked to think aloud, to influence others, to signal a position, or to test how you react.
**APPLY WHEN:** Presentations and group meetings, especially with vague or high-abstraction questions; early calls where client talk-time is the objective.
**AVOID WHEN:** Overused it becomes an irritating device and reads as evasion; a direct question deserving a direct answer must get one; and do not derail a client who is using questions to think.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=solo/few/committee · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — the anchor case is a salesperson asked "how big is your company?" who answered his own fear by inflating size and lost to a buyer who wanted a small attentive vendor; the real question was about service.

### [REAL:STEPSPEC] Specify every next step three ways
**Move:** Whenever the decision is interim, make each remaining step carry a go/no-go decision, a date, and named participants, agreed by both sides. Be prepared to ask for or offer next steps at the end of every meeting.
**APPLY WHEN:** Multi-step buying processes; the meeting closed something but not the deal.
**AVOID WHEN:** Nothing — but a step list without a go/no-go decision attached is the failure mode, not an alternative.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=low/mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting/saas · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the book's standard closing discipline, restated in the quick reference.

### [REAL:AGENDAPRE] Build the agenda from key beliefs and pre-position it
**Move:** Convert the End in Mind and each key belief into agenda lines, then send them to the client well in advance covering four elements: agreement on the meeting's End in Mind; agreement that the agenda is appropriate, complete and sufficient to enable that decision; what the client can do beforehand to be prepared; and what information you need in advance to use the time well. Invite them to add, modify or delete. Example structure from a live deal: is there something worth finding → is the required time investment appropriate and feasible → do the economics make sense → decide whether to proceed.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any scheduled decision meeting or second call; any meeting where the client would otherwise arrive unprepared or agenda-less.
**AVOID WHEN:** A true first call — the book says an agenda is unnecessary there, though framing one for the follow-up is useful; sending it too close to the meeting defeats it, and an agenda handed out at the start is the named failure case.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=low/mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — the NewLogo agenda and pre-positioning email, with the CFO replying that it was exactly what should be covered; that meeting ended in a verbal commitment.

### Resolving yellow lights (nonprice)

### [REAL:EGOCHECK] Check your ego at the door
**Move:** When challenged, do not defend, attack, or seek approval. Hold a deliberately calm, observational stance — watch the objection arrive and note what it is doing before responding, treating your own need to look smart or be liked as the thing to suspend.
**APPLY WHEN:** The client challenges your competence, price or fit; pressure is rising; you feel the urge to counterpunch.
**AVOID WHEN:** Calm becomes stonewalling — a flat non-response to a legitimate concern reads as evasion, not composure.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — a shared trait of the top consultants the authors coach; illustrated with a borrowed bullfighting metaphor, not measured.

### [REAL:WRONGOK] Be willing to be wrong
**Move:** Treat the discovery of a fatal flaw in your own solution as a shared win, not a loss. State openly that you want to find the flaw as much as the client does, and let that stance license you to keep listening instead of pushing.
**APPLY WHEN:** You are convinced you are right and the client keeps resisting; complex, high-consequence implementations.
**AVOID WHEN:** Performed as a rhetorical humility move to disarm — it functions only if you would actually walk.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — supported by the claim that clients they work with lost hundreds of millions operationally, and billions in market value, on deals they wish they had lost.

### [REAL:NEVERNOTHING] Never Do Something for Nothing redirection
**Move:** When asked at the end of a presentation to go away and produce something (more detail, references, a written summary, a presentation to a newly named stakeholder) instead of deciding, run two steps. First define success: ask what specifically they want to see that would let them decide either way. Second, walk verbally into the future: propose that a week from now they have exactly that and it makes sense — then what happens? If the answer is not a decision, push back plainly that even a perfect delivery would not produce a yes or a no, and ask what is missing.
**APPLY WHEN:** The End in Mind was asked for and the reply is a task rather than a decision; task-force, committee or "we need to study it" deflections.
**AVOID WHEN:** The request genuinely is the last missing decision input and you already know it is favourable — then just get it and get the decision; also weak where the buyer's process legitimately requires an artifact you have not produced.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the authors' standing rule, leaning on Argyris that deferral language often encodes unspeakable non-support.

### [REAL:ACKPHRASE] Acknowledge without agreeing
**Move:** Signal vocally and visibly that you are slowing down for the concern before touching its content — appreciate the honesty, name the question as tough and worth working through, or state that if no good answer exists you would not want either side to proceed. Empathy and appreciation, deliberately without agreement.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any objection lands, especially a blunt one about your competence or focus.
**AVOID WHEN:** The acknowledging phrase becomes a scripted tic preceding an unchanged rebuttal — clients read the mismatch.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=service/consulting/saas · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** assertion — a list of sample phrasings offered without outcome data.

### [REAL:UNPACKWORD] Unpack the vague word in the objection
**Move:** Before answering, interrogate the load-bearing noun. For "we'd lose control if we outsource," ask what control means here, who loses it over what, what evidence tells them they have or lack it, what happens when it is lost, how they concluded outsourcing reduces it, how that weighs against the advantages that made them consider outsourcing, and what they would do if the concern were resolved. Use the answers to pick the resolution: outsourcing gives more of what control means to them, trades low-priority control for higher-priority control, current control is not producing results, or not acting loses more control.
**APPLY WHEN:** The objection is worded abstractly (control, risk, culture, fit, flexibility) and you cannot state its mechanism back.
**AVOID WHEN:** The concern is already concrete and specified — further interrogation stalls and feels like cross-examination.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — worked example plus the general failure mode of answering the wrong concern.

### [REAL:WWHTH] "What would have to happen…?"
**Move:** Ask the client to state their own criterion for resolving the concern, deliberately phrased impersonally — what would have to happen, what would have to be in place, what would they need to see or hear or experience, what would they check off in their mind and gut, how would they become convinced. Do not ask what *you* or your company would have to do; resolution may not involve you at all. Precede it by declining to press your own claims.
**APPLY WHEN:** A yellow light is stated as a dissatisfaction ("I'm not confident you have industry experience"); you want the client's own bar rather than your imagined one.
**AVOID WHEN:** The client cannot articulate criteria and the question becomes an interrogation loop; or the criterion, once stated aloud, would harden a position they were holding loosely.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=low/mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — rests on the maxim that a client-stated criterion converts better than a seller-stated claim, and on the claim that client criteria are usually less stringent than feared; asserted, not measured.

### [REAL:HYPOSCOUT] Hypothetical advance scouting
**Move:** Before or after eliciting criteria, pose a for-discussion-only hypothetical: suppose this concern were resolved / suppose the business unit leaders did buy in / suppose it could be done in six months — where would we be, what would you do, what would you decide? Use the answer to choose the resolution path and to expose whether resolving this concern would actually move the decision.
**APPLY WHEN:** You suspect the stated concern is not the real blocker; multiple issues on the table.
**AVOID WHEN:** Repeated hypotheticals start reading as pinning the buyer down; also useless if the person cannot decide anyway.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — standard practice with sample phrasings.

### [REAL:YLBATCH] Choose: resolve each yellow light or collect them all
**Move:** Consciously decide whether to handle each concern as it appears or to surface the full list first. Batching lets you spot commonalities, sequence by priority (biggest first), or build momentum by clearing easy ones before the hard one.
**APPLY WHEN:** Concerns arrive serially and each resolution spawns another — a sign that the list, not the item, is the real object.
**AVOID WHEN:** Surfacing the full list would manufacture objections the client had not formed, in an otherwise green meeting.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** assertion — offered as a judgment call with no supporting evidence.

### [REAL:FACTFIX] Resolve with facts and data
**Move:** Treat many objections as information defects — a missing feature they think you lack, a wrong number, an absent fact — and simply supply or correct it while you are still present. Where an internal belief is the obstacle (e.g. "we lose on price"), bring the win/loss data that contradicts it and the analysis of which behaviours actually correlate with wins.
**APPLY WHEN:** The concern is factual or empirical and you have credible data at hand.
**AVOID WHEN:** The objection is a proxy for a political or emotional issue — data then hardens the position; also avoid when your "data" is marketing material rather than measured outcomes.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=service/consulting/saas · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — Randy's win/loss engagement is the single worked case; the "research on objections shows" framing has no named source.

### [REAL:THIRDPARTY] Bracketed third-party story
**Move:** Use a comparable client's outcome to answer a concern (the feel/felt/found pattern), but pre-empt the "we're unique" rejection by bracketing: acknowledge their situation is unique, say they may or may not find the story useful, tell it, then ask whether they can see how it might apply here. Persuasiveness rises with how closely organization and situation match, and with measured results behind the story.
**APPLY WHEN:** The concern is about feasibility or precedent and you have a genuinely similar reference.
**AVOID WHEN:** The comparison is loose, the client has signalled they resent being compared, or there is no measured evidence behind the story.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — long-standing common practice; failure conditions stated without measurement.

### [REAL:OWNMEDICINE] Borrow the client's own sales argument
**Move:** Note that the client faces the same objection from their own customers, and ask what they say when their prospect challenges them on exactly that point (new niche, premium price, lack of direct experience). Then reuse their own reasoning back to them.
**APPLY WHEN:** The client sells something themselves and the objection has a mirror in their market.
**AVOID WHEN:** The client is a non-commercial buyer with no analogous sales motion, or the analogy is strained enough to feel like a trap.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — Mahan's retail shoe-chain example mapped onto computer systems.

### [REAL:METAPREP] Pre-build metaphors and stories for recurring objections
**Move:** Take your most frequent and hardest objections and deliberately develop metaphors for each — collected or original — build several per objection and compare reactions, treat storytelling as a trainable competence, and look for experiential metaphors the client can see or do in the room.
**APPLY WHEN:** Preparation time between cycles; objections you meet repeatedly.
**AVOID WHEN:** Improvised in the moment — an untested metaphor that misfires costs credibility.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=service/consulting/saas · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** assertion — a preparation recommendation with no outcome data.

### [REAL:IMPWEIGH] Reframe by changing the criterion's relative importance
**Move:** When you cannot meet a stated criterion, isolate it against everything already agreed: suppose all the results are real and obtainable, customers and shareholders win, and the single unresolved item is this one — would that kill the whole deal?
**APPLY WHEN:** A single unmeetable criterion (IP ownership, a guarantee, a certification) sits against a large agreed value case.
**AVOID WHEN:** The criterion is a genuine hard constraint (legal, regulatory, security) — pressing it reads as dismissing a real requirement.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — one of three standard reframes with sample phrasing.

### [REAL:NOONECAN] Reframe by showing no one can meet the criterion
**Move:** Ask, framed as curiosity, whether any firm actually guaranteed the criterion at this project's scale — every person with eight-plus years in their industry, or the cost savings without control over hiring and firing — and if someone claimed it, whether that claim seems reasonable to them.
**APPLY WHEN:** A competitor has promised something you believe is not deliverable; the criterion is an inherited spec rather than a considered requirement.
**AVOID WHEN:** A competitor genuinely can meet it — you lose credibility instantly.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — sample phrasings from the authors' practice.

### [REAL:LINKSUB] Find the link, substitute the link
**Move:** Treat the criterion as half of an unspoken if-then belief: if X, then something good happens. Ask what X gives them or does for them (better quality? faster ramp? on-time delivery? lower training cost?). Then offer the *consequence* rather than the criterion — e.g. if the real driver is hitting a politically committed deadline, ask whether they care more about years of experience or about the ability to deliver on time.
**APPLY WHEN:** The criterion is a proxy metric (years of experience, headcount, certification count) rather than the outcome itself.
**AVOID WHEN:** The client has already tested the substitution and rejected it; or the proxy is contractually mandated.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — worked eight-years-of-experience example; framing borrowed from NLP-style belief work, not research.

### [REAL:LINKBREAK] Break the if-then link
**Move:** If substitution fails, attack the belief itself from several angles: move opposite to expectation (why eight years and not twenty — more would be better?) so their defence of eight supplies your argument for four; ask whether they have long-tenured people they would *not* put on this time-sensitive project, and why; raise the one-year-of-experience-repeated-ten-times pattern; argue that ten-year-old methods would disqualify you with their own peers and that blended teams outperform homogeneous long-tenured ones; cite firms famous for developing leaders by rotating top people across industries — asking for open-minded consideration rather than agreement.
**APPLY WHEN:** The client will not lower the criterion's importance and believes it is obtainable elsewhere; you have prepared the angles in advance.
**AVOID WHEN:** Delivered as debate rather than inquiry — each angle is a question first; and drop it once the client has conceded, since further pushing reads as winning an argument.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — five concrete phrasings from field work; no measured conversion.

### [REAL:YLPRECEPT] Five precepts for the hardest yellow lights
**Move:** Run the toughest objections through a fixed checklist: (1) intent first — ask how turning this to green serves the client's interest, and if there is no good answer, stop; (2) solve for unknowns — what do their key words mean, what is the underlying objective, where did they give the "if" without the "then"; (3) create options — multiple ways to acknowledge/understand/resolve, common interests, differently-valued interests, how others removed the same roadblock; (4) prepare and practise until the language lands without raising hackles and stays congruent with your own personality; (5) increase self-awareness of the fears that push you into defend-or-attack mode.
**APPLY WHEN:** Deal review or coaching on a recurring hard objection; pre-presentation preparation.
**AVOID WHEN:** Used live as a mental checklist mid-conversation — it competes with listening.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the authors' coaching framework, repeated across engagements.

### Price and negotiation

### [REAL:NODEALPT] Set target and no-deal point before negotiating
**Move:** Test a price range with the client before the final presentation so the number is never a surprise. Choose an aggressive yet realistic target, and separately articulate the no-deal point — the level below which walking away beats the business. Then advocate for the target as if it were the no-deal point; if you concede, concede small, slowly, and always in exchange for value.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any negotiated engagement, before entering a pricing conversation at all.
**AVOID WHEN:** Anchoring so high that credibility is lost; and never accept below the articulated no-deal point — accepting below it, and failing to close above it, are named as the two grievous errors.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the authors' negotiation guidance; the "higher targets yield higher returns" support is unnamed negotiation research, so treat that claim as assertion.

### [REAL:PRICELAST] Take price off the table to test if it is the last issue
**Move:** State that the price was set to produce the results they want and that moving it would require trade-offs, then ask them to suppose price were agreed — what would they do? An unequivocal "we'd go with you" means price is the last issue and is negotiable. Anything else means something important is unresolved; say so, ask what is missing, and resolve all nonprice issues first.
**APPLY WHEN:** The buyer opens on price before other concerns are visibly closed.
**AVOID WHEN:** Price truly is the only remaining item and the test just delays; also poorly suited to procurement-run processes where the counterpart is not the decision maker.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — general rule with scripted phrasing.

### [REAL:SCOPELEVER] Price = scope × timing × division of labor
**Move:** Refuse a lower price at identical scope, timing and labour split; instead convert the discount request into a lever question — can they live with fewer results, different timing, or taking on more of the work themselves without compromising the outcome? If they want all three unchanged, state plainly that this is how the price was derived.
**APPLY WHEN:** The buyer wants a discount without changing what they are buying.
**AVOID WHEN:** Reducing scope would produce an outcome you would not stand behind — trading quality for a signature contradicts the book's own fit rule.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the authors' standing framework for concession structure.

### [REAL:PRICEEQUIV] Trade price equivalents that keep you whole
**Move:** If you do move on price, extract non-cash value in exchange: skip the written proposal and pass the saved cost through, get marketing or advertising value, get R&D collaboration and measurable results you can write up, get additional business signed now against reduced business-development cost, or get early payment against reduced receivables cost.
**APPLY WHEN:** Price is the last issue and the buyer psychologically needs to win a concession.
**AVOID WHEN:** The exchange is nominal — a fake trade teaches the buyer the price was soft; also inapplicable to large gaps.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — enumerated exchanges from the authors' practice.

### [REAL:SLOWCEDE] Concede slowly and in small amounts
**Move:** Keep concessions small and hard-won; the satisfaction a buyer takes from a concession tracks how difficult it was to obtain, not its size. Reserve trade-offs for small reductions only.
**APPLY WHEN:** Late-stage negotiation where all nonprice issues are closed.
**AVOID WHEN:** The gap is large — large price variance is classified as a selling problem, not a negotiating one, so go back and rebuild value instead.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** assertion — attributed to unnamed negotiation research without a citable source.

### [REAL:PLOYANS] Prepared answers to the four standard price ploys
**Move:** Meet each ploy with a conditional trade rather than a discount. "There's more in the pipeline" → happy to reduce selling cost if they sign some of it now, how much? "We need to see how you do first" → keep the price, agree in advance that additional business without new selling cost earns the discount later. "Our name will win you business" → that is already priced in, so let's discuss what they would actually do in that regard. "All our vendors give 10%" → it was disclosed up front and is reflected in the price, but it is an investment expecting a return, so future bids revert to standard rates.
**APPLY WHEN:** Final stage, all nonprice issues resolved, the buyer wants a symbolic win.
**AVOID WHEN:** Applied before nonprice issues are closed, or scripted so tightly it reads as canned deflection.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — four scripted exchanges the authors present as recurring.

### [REAL:HOLDTERMS] Hold the line on best-and-final and on terms
**Move:** Price the engagement to the team quality the results require and say so — if they want the A team, that is what it costs; if they will compromise on people, the price can move. Treat payment terms as equal in weight to price and state that up front. When purchasing later asks for best-and-final and reverts to standard policy terms, resend the identical agreement without commentary, and do not take the attempt personally.
**APPLY WHEN:** A late procurement-stage squeeze after the business decision makers have already chosen you.
**AVOID WHEN:** You have not actually established the value case, or you would in fact accept less — bluffing here is discoverable.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=high · stakeholders=committee · offer=service/consulting · market=enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one multimillion-dollar deal of the authors' own firm (rates challenged against a well-known firm's partners; 90-day RFP terms vs required advance payment; identical agreement re-sent, buyer proceeded).

### Results — after yes, no, or no decision

### [REAL:PREEMPTNEG] Preemptive negotiation of future yellow lights
**Move:** Right after the win, while the relationship is strong, propose a conversation about what could go wrong. Open by asking whether they have ever seen an installation of this scale go perfectly; when they say no, say neither have you, and propose talking through likely challenges in advance so they can be prevented or moved through faster. Let the client list theirs first and work each one ("if that happens, what should we do?", "how would you like to avoid that?"), then add yours — scope change, missed commitments and deadlines on their side, personnel changes.
**APPLY WHEN:** Immediately post-signature on complex implementations, before delivery starts.
**AVOID WHEN:** Framed as liability-shifting or contract hardening — the value is the shared communication protocol, not the record.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any/expansion · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — named as an underutilized practice the authors run with clients; no measured effect.

### [REAL:HANDOFF] Transfer the discovery to the delivery team
**Move:** Work closely with the implementers so what was bought is what gets delivered — the issues, evidence, impact, context and constraints gathered in qualifying are the handoff payload, not just the contract.
**APPLY WHEN:** Sales and delivery are different people; complex service or product implementations.
**AVOID WHEN:** Solo delivery, where no handoff exists — though the discovery record still drives scope.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting/saas · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** assertion — stated as critical without support.

### [REAL:MEASURERETAIN] Keep measuring results and run an account strategy
**Move:** Maintain pressure on both your company and the client to measure outcomes, so that when a new executive asks what all that spend produced there is an answer. Keep asking how you can help this client succeed and use the Initiating tools to bring new ideas to existing accounts.
**APPLY WHEN:** Post-delivery, ongoing accounts; before renewal or executive turnover.
**AVOID WHEN:** Measurement theatre — vanity metrics that will not survive an incoming executive's scrutiny.
**Deal-gate:** motion=expansion · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting/saas · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — supported by a cited HBR article (Shapiro, Slywotzky, Doyle, 1994) naming account retention as the largest determinant of profitability in service businesses; the operational advice is the authors'.

### [REAL:WINLOSS] Run a real win/loss analysis
**Move:** After a loss (or a win), get the actual reasons rather than assuming. Go beyond stated importance by using mathematical analysis to infer which attributes and behaviours correlate with winning and losing, then predict the sales lift from a measurable increase in those behaviours and target training accordingly.
**APPLY WHEN:** Enough closed deals to analyse; a sales organization deciding where to invest in skills.
**AVOID WHEN:** Single-deal post-mortems treated as statistically meaningful; and note stated-importance data is what buyers say, not necessarily what drove them.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the authors' own consulting method; the predicted-percentage claim is self-reported and client-specific.

### [REAL:FORCEDECIDE] Force a yes-or-no path on a stalled deal
**Move:** After a reasonable contemplation window, put the fork explicitly: either this is a good investment and every day of delay costs money, or it is not and keeping it alive wastes both sides' time — so let's define a series of steps at the end of which they can comfortably say yes or no, with no perfectly acceptable.
**APPLY WHEN:** The client will neither commit nor decline; the opportunity is consuming pipeline attention.
**AVOID WHEN:** Used as pressure with no genuine willingness to take the no; or when a known external event (budget cycle, reorg) explains the pause.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the book's standing script for no-decision, consistent with the "no is okay" rule.

### Sales management and method health

### [REAL:METH-TRIAGE] Three-way triage when the method is not producing results
**Move:** When results lag, do not default to "more training." Ask in order: are we actually applying the method (fix = increase application), applying it unskillfully (fix = increase skill), or is the method itself wrong (fix = tear it apart, re-evaluate, replace)? When results are good, reinforce, repeat, reward, improve.
**APPLY WHEN:** Sales-leadership review of a team that adopted a methodology and is underperforming.
**AVOID WHEN:** Sample size is too small to distinguish the three causes — a handful of losses cannot separate skill from method.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=low/mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** assertion — the authors' own operating loop, no dataset behind the triage.

### [REAL:SPI] Sales Progression Index as a coaching instrument
**Move:** Score an opportunity by the flow of meaningful information between client and consultant, separating what must come from (or be confirmed by) the client from what is a composite of your own beliefs and elicited opinions. Read a small information flow as a low-probability opportunity and either exit gracefully or proceed with caution. Use it primarily to coach the seller on increasing information flow before presenting, not primarily to forecast.
**APPLY WHEN:** Sales management, pipeline review, coaching conversations.
**AVOID WHEN:** Used mainly as a forecasting number — the book explicitly ranks the coaching use higher; scoring is optional and some clients drop it.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — a FranklinCovey tool customized per client, used across their client base.

## Where this book stops working

- **Transactional, one-time purchases.** The authors state their own limit: the book targets B2B relationships intended to repeat and is tilted toward the complex sale — many people, many interactions, significant money, non-trivial business issues. Simpler sales can run the same process faster and shallower, which is exactly where the lens should be quiet.
- **No dialogue on offer.** The whole method presumes a diagnostic conversation is possible. No-contact RFPs, portal-only procurement, and closed policy processes strip out the mechanism; what remains is a set of exit rules (RFP-HUMAN-RULE, BLOCKED4, WALKTALK), not a way to sell.
- **Solo buyer with signing authority.** The grid, the access ladder, the Meeting Plan and the readiness gate all assume a committee with staged decisions. Against a single owner they are overhead that slows a green deal.
- **Low-ACV, high-volume motions.** 95/5 prospecting, thirty minutes per stakeholder, pre-call hypothesis testing and multi-meeting qualification cannot be funded at small deal sizes. The authors themselves concede that if volume cold calling works for you, keep doing it.
- **Buyers who already completed their diagnosis** and are buying a specified commodity on price and speed: move-off-the-solution reads as deflection, and repeated peeling looks like an inability to answer.
- **Confidentiality-constrained deals.** The core qualification metric — free flow of client-stated information — misreads regulated or security-constrained silence as disinterest.
- **Deals where value creation and value split diverge.** The "we both want the same thing" premise holds for making the pie bigger and fails for dividing it; the price chapter is where the book is thinnest, leaning on unnamed negotiation research.
- **Anywhere intent is faked.** TAKEAWAY, WALKTALK, PRICELAST and NODEALPT are all incongruence-detectors: run as gambits without genuine willingness to lose the deal, they misfire worse than not running them.

## What the book debunks

- Prefixing "selling" with a virtuous adjective (consultative, solution, value-based, integrity) does not remove the buyer's suspicion that something is being done *to* them; ethical sellers are still judged guilty until proven innocent.
- The three default seller postures all fail: **Telling** looks arrogant or ignorant, kills buy-in and hides bigger opportunities; **Accepting** the client's stated want ignores that the client can be wrong and will still blame you; **Guessing** gets renamed diagnosis or assessment and formalized as a proposal — the longer the proposal, the more guessing it encodes.
- A deal "qualified" by consultant conviction rather than client statements is the core self-deception. In the pipeline scrub, consultants asserted urgent client problems but could not point to client words confirming importance, evidence or impact, could not say how success would be measured, had not discussed time/people/money, and lacked stakeholder access.
- **Fluency is mistaken for comprehension.** Davenport's observation is cited: the better an organization knows a concept, the less likely it agrees on one meaning. Sellers skip the meaning question fearing they will look ignorant, when asking reads as attentiveness.
- Late red lights are almost always ignored early yellows: in the billion-dollar outsourcing loss, the client said at the very first meeting they believed they could achieve the same cost reductions in-house; the objection went unaddressed for 18 months and killed the deal, though the postmortem found viable resolutions had existed. Red lights are not failure — making them needlessly expensive is.
- The standard organizational response to poor qualification is to chase more opportunities rather than qualify better, which lowers win rate and raises cost. Likewise when win rates fall, management demands more proposals.
- **"Sales is a numbers game"** is challenged directly: more calls per hour means less attention per call, more rejection, then more calls — a self-reinforcing bad system. Cold calling is rejected outright as high-rejection, low-yield and unpleasant for buyers.
- **"Money's no problem" is false by default.** Guessing at price until the proposal presentation (mutual mystification) is the named cardinal error; consultants have out-spent the client's entire budget on proposal development and discovered it months later, when two minutes of asking would have shown it.
- **"Start at the top, stay at the top" and "get to the economic buyer"** both fail in the book's own cases — one competitor lost by ignoring a delegated buyer who then chose against them; another withdrew because it could not reach an executive group that turned out to be ratifiers. Organizational authority is not buying authority. Asking "who decides?" first corrupts the answer.
- Believing a **written proposal sells**, that a **sponsor can present on your behalf**, or that a **proxy interviewer** substitutes for direct contact (dilution, distortion, generalization are unavoidable) — all named failures. The generic seller agenda (introductions → company overview → products → client list → next steps) is seller-centered and does not enable a decision.
- **Information-rich, decision-poor presentations actively manufacture objections** that would never otherwise have existed. An End in Mind phrased as the answer you want triggers reactance.
- **Rapport must precede the hard question** — inverted: the soft-worded hard question *builds* rapport, and bailing to a safe question protects the seller, not the client.
- **Resolving before understanding**: counterpunching answers a concern the client did not raise, answers it badly, or introduces new ones. Certainty is a liability — consultants sure they are right stop listening. And not every yellow light should be turned green: the target is fit, not conversion.
- **Good negotiation cannot substitute for bad selling.** Instant discounting teaches the buyer the quoted price was a lie; the common inverted pattern is conceding readily down to the no-deal point and only then arguing hard. Capitulating on price and still losing is documented, not hypothetical.
- Skipping the constraints question out of fear of talking the client into self-serving is self-defeating — if that is their best choice they reach it anyway, later and more expensively for you.
- Winning and moving on leaves behind more revenue than the next pursuit; "we'll get back to you" and "let's stay in touch" are named as the polite forms of a no; and "go away and do something" is frequently a substitute for unspeakable non-support.
- Buyer-side dysfunction is treated as a mirror, not an excuse: formulaic no-contact RFPs suppress creativity and drive good providers to no-bid, which harms the buyer.
- The authors' own honest carve-outs: personality-style systems (Jung-derived quadrants, MBTI) were deliberately excluded from the method; drip/permission marketing was excluded too; and if volume cold calling is working for you, keep doing it. "The question they ask is never the real question" is explicitly hedged as overstatement for emphasis.

## Numbers that matter

- **Reach and scale (self-reported):** coursework taught in 40+ countries, 9 languages; first self-published edition sold 100,000+ copies; FranklinCovey company self-description of ~1,500 associates, 35 international offices serving 140+ countries, clients including 90% of the Fortune 100 and 75%+ of the Fortune 500 (marketing copy).
- **RFP audit (one client's own study, self-reported):** 46 unsolicited-RFP responses over 18 months, average direct cost ~$80,000 each, win rate zero — a single hypothetical win would have carried ~$3,680,000 of pursuit cost.
- **Pipeline scrub:** several billion dollars across ~400 deals; the leader wanted to pursue at most 200.
- **No-Guessing word exercise (self-reported, tens of thousands of participants over years):** groups of 4-5 each write 10+ synonyms for a common business term; participants estimate 3-4 words in common (range 1 to 6+); actual overlap is almost always zero.
- **Wasted pipeline (ES Research Group, third-party but unverified here):** 65% of salespeople pursue worthless deals; 80% of lost opportunities trace to inadequate or nonexistent qualification and sales planning.
- **Decision failure (Paul Nutt, *Why Decisions Fail*, 356 companies — the only substantial named external study):** more than 50% of decisions failed, being abandoned, only partially implemented, or never adopted.
- **Account profitability (HBR 1994, Shapiro/Slywotzky/Doyle):** five determinants — retention, dominance from preferred-vendor position, realized price, selling and service cost, account selection — with retention the single largest in service businesses.
- **Executive access (Gardner/Bistritz/Klompmaker white paper):** 84% chance an executive agrees to talk when referred by someone she knows inside her company; 44% when referred by someone outside it; 44% when contacted outside the office, e.g. at a conference. Cold-call baseline cited: ~100 calls → ~10 appointments → 1-2 sales.
- **Rules of thumb (unsourced/self-reported):** 80% of impact sits in 20% of issues; 95/5 replaces 80/20 for prospecting (top 5 prospects); when information matters and the client could not get it alone, ~80% of the time they want help; management horizon for value-over-time 2-3 years; investment range Y ≤ X + 25-50%; ~30 minutes per stakeholder; 1-10 scale reading 8-10 motivating, 7 keep talking, ≤6 yellow; 3-7 stepping stones; 7 Meeting Plan elements; 3-5 questions per first meeting (against the 53-question negative example); habit formation 30-40 days; 20-30 minutes/week on network expansion, first leads within ~60 days, 5+ inbound potential clients per good referral source per year.
- **Worked impact examples:** margin 28% → 30% on ~$100M target-market sales ≈ $2M/year, ~$4-6M over 2-3 years. Quality 10 → 5 rejections per 1,000 units at ~$1,000 each, 3M units/year ≈ $15M/year, ~$30-45M over 2-3 years (roll-up variant: $25-30M/year halved to $12.5-15M/year). Cost of delay: a $10M/year issue deferred 3 years = $30M. Turnover 38% company vs 20% industry. NewLogo: impact estimated at 2-7% of a revenue segment, 5% used ≈ $36M/year; the CFO's counter-anchor was $3M; a historically 6-12-month cycle executed in under 60 days, contract signed 3 weeks after verbal commitment. We-Do-It: 100 potentials → 5 → 3, all 3 won.
- **Win/loss engagement (client-specific, self-reported):** deals won at prices up to 30% above competitors and lost at prices up to 30% below; sellers ranked price and relationships as most important to buyers while buyers ranked them low and ranked understanding their business challenges high; modelled 6-8% sales increase from demonstrable gains in three skill areas.
- **Unsourced:** the claim that decision makers spend about five minutes per proposal, looking mainly at price and a summary; the price-anchoring and concession-satisfaction claims attributed to unnamed "negotiation research."

## Blind spots of this book

- **No independent validation of its own method.** Everything methodological — ORDER, the Meeting Plan, yellow-light resolution, the five P's, the 20 questions, the SPI — is FranklinCovey practitioner material. The named external citations (Nutt, HBR 1994, the referral white paper, ES Research) support context, never a technique's effect. `practitioner-n-many` is the honest ceiling, and a substantial minority of moves sit below it at anecdote or assertion.
- **Survivorship in the war stories.** The told cases end in wins — the declined proposal that the board reversed, the London flight home, the reordered decision sequence. The losing runs of the same high-risk moves are not reported.
- **Transactional, self-serve, and product-led motions are outside its field of view.** It has nothing on pricing pages, trials, PLG loops, channel or marketplace selling, or buyer journeys that complete without a seller.
- **Marketing and demand generation are excluded by choice.** Drip and permission marketing are explicitly out of scope; inbound is treated as a referral-network by-product rather than a system.
- **Buyer-side power asymmetry is under-modelled.** The advice assumes a buyer who can be argued into better process. Against professional procurement running a deliberately sealed process, the book offers exit rather than strategy.
- **Cultural and personality variance is deliberately unhandled.** Personality-style frameworks were excluded, and the authors admit their exact wording will not fit every speaker — but no substitute calibration is supplied beyond "build your own language."
- **The economics of the method itself are never costed.** Thirty minutes per stakeholder, multi-meeting qualification and pre-call hypothesis work have a real cost per deal that is never compared against the win-rate gain claimed.
- **Two soft appendices carry no support:** the NLP-derived resource-state/anchor routine and the four-Vs congruence model are presented as technique with no evidence of effect.
- **Team, compensation and quota design are absent.** The book coaches the individual consultant and, briefly, the coach — it does not address how comp plans, territory design or forecasting rituals push sellers into exactly the behaviours it condemns.
