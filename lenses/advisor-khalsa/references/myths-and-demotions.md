# advisor-khalsa — myths and demotions

What this tradition debunks, what it demotes inside itself, and which of its own numbers must
never leave this lens as fact. Applies to the honest-discovery layer of services deals.

---

## 1. Myths this lens attacks in other sales practice

- **"Consultative / solution / value-based / integrity selling" as a fix.** Prefixing "selling"
  with a virtuous adjective does not remove the buyer's suspicion that something is being done
  *to* them. Ethical sellers are still judged guilty until proven innocent; the adjective is
  not the remedy, behaviour over time is.
- **All three default seller postures fail.** *Telling* looks arrogant or ignorant, kills
  buy-in and hides larger opportunities. *Accepting* the client's stated want ignores that the
  client can be wrong and will still blame you. *Guessing* is the most dangerous because it
  gets renamed diagnosis or assessment and formalized in a proposal — and the longer the
  proposal, the more guessing it encodes. Countered by [REAL:LSM], [REAL:NINEQ].
- **A deal "qualified" by consultant conviction.** The core self-deception of the layer: in the
  ~400-deal pipeline scrub, sellers asserted urgent client problems but could not point to
  client words confirming importance, evidence or impact, could not say how success would be
  measured, had not discussed time, people or money, and had no stakeholder access.
  Countered by [REAL:INFO-FLOW-QUAL].
- **Fluency mistaken for comprehension.** Davenport's observation is cited: the better an
  organization knows a concept, the less it agrees on a single meaning. Sellers skip the
  meaning question fearing they will look ignorant, when asking actually reads as attentiveness.
  Countered by [REAL:KEYWORD-MEANING].
- **"Sales is a numbers game."** Challenged directly: more calls per hour means less attention
  per call, which means more rejection, which demands more calls — a self-reinforcing bad
  system. Cold calling is rejected outright as high-rejection, low-yield and unpleasant for
  buyers. Countered by [REAL:RULE955], [REAL:NOCOLD].
- **"Money's no problem."** False by default. Guessing at price until proposal presentation
  (mutual mystification) is the named cardinal error; consultants have out-spent the client's
  entire budget on proposal development and found out months later, when two minutes of asking
  would have shown it. Countered by [REAL:MONEY3].
- **"Start at the top, stay at the top" / "get to the economic buyer."** Both fail in the book's
  own cases: one competitor lost by ignoring a delegated buyer who then chose against them;
  another withdrew because it could not reach an executive group that turned out to be
  ratifiers. Organizational authority is not buying authority. Additionally, asking "who
  decides?" *first* corrupts the answer — it invites an inflated "I do" and an incomplete list.
  Countered by [REAL:GRID], [REAL:RATIFIER].
- **The written proposal sells.** It does not; a sponsor cannot present on your behalf (they
  lack your authority and credibility and will not challenge their own superiors' faulty
  assumptions), and a proxy interviewer cannot substitute for direct contact — dilution,
  distortion and generalization are unavoidable. Countered by [REAL:WEARETHEPROPOSAL],
  [REAL:RIGHTROOM], [REAL:ROLEREV].
- **The generic seller agenda** (introductions → company overview → products → client list →
  next steps) is seller-centered and enables no decision. Information-rich, decision-poor
  presentations actively manufacture objections that would never otherwise have existed, and an
  End in Mind phrased as the answer you want triggers reactance.
- **"Build rapport, then ask the hard question."** Inverted here: the soft-worded hard question
  *builds* rapport, and bailing out to a safe factual question protects the seller, not the
  client. Countered by [REAL:UNASKED-Q].
- **Resolve first, understand later.** Counterpunching answers a concern the client did not
  raise, answers it badly, or introduces new ones. Certainty is treated as a liability:
  consultants sure they are right stop listening.
- **"Every yellow light should be turned green."** Explicitly rejected — the target is fit, not
  conversion. Red lights are not failure; making them needlessly expensive is.
- **Good negotiation rescues bad selling.** It does not. Instant discounting teaches the buyer
  that the quoted price was a lie; the common inverted pattern is conceding readily down to the
  no-deal point and only then arguing hard. Capitulating on price and still losing is
  documented in the book, not hypothetical. Large price variance is classified as a *selling*
  problem, not a negotiating one.
- **The organizational reflex to chase more deals.** Poor qualification is answered with more
  opportunities rather than better qualification, which lowers win rate and raises cost; when
  win rates fall, management demands more proposals, degrading them further.
- **Skipping the constraints question** for fear of talking the client into self-serving is
  self-defeating — if in-house is genuinely their best choice, they reach it anyway, later and
  more expensively for you.
- **Polite forms of no.** "We'll get back to you" and "let's stay in touch" are named as no;
  "go away and do something" is frequently a substitute for unspeakable non-support
  (Argyris). Countered by [REAL:NEVERNOTHING], [REAL:FORCEDECIDE].
- **Buyer-side dysfunction as an excuse.** Formulaic no-contact RFPs suppress creativity and
  drive good providers to no-bid, which harms the buyer — but the lens treats this as a mirror
  for the seller's own process, not as a grievance.

## 2. Demotions and carve-outs the authors make themselves

These are the tradition's own honest limits. Quoting the method past them is misuse.

- **Personality-style systems** (Jung-derived quadrants, MBTI and relatives) were deliberately
  excluded from the method. Do not reintroduce buyer-type frameworks under this lens.
- **Drip and permission marketing** are explicitly out of scope; inbound is treated as a
  by-product of the referral network, not as a system.
- **Volume cold calling** is conceded: if it is working for you, keep doing it. [REAL:RULE955]
  and [REAL:NOCOLD] do not apply to genuinely transactional high-volume motions.
- **"The question they ask is never the real question"** is explicitly hedged by the authors as
  overstatement for emphasis — [REAL:REDIRECT] must carry that hedge, and a question asked
  twice *is* the real question.
- **[REAL:SPI] is a coaching instrument first, a forecasting number second** — the book ranks
  the coaching use higher, scoring is optional, and some clients drop the scoring entirely.
- **The RFP-access rule was broken on purpose sometimes** and the company was still served —
  [REAL:RFP-HUMAN-RULE] is a policy with priced exceptions, not an absolute.

## 3. Documented failure modes of the techniques themselves

- **Incongruence detectors misfire when faked.** [REAL:TAKEAWAY], [REAL:WALKTALK],
  [REAL:PRICELAST] and [REAL:NODEALPT] only function if the seller is genuinely willing to lose
  the deal. Run as gambits, they land worse than not running them at all.
- **Yellow-light discipline is abandoned exactly when it matters.** The authors' own failure
  case: ego in the room (a VIP guest, appearances to protect) is when [REAL:YELLOW-SLOW] gets
  dropped. In the billion-dollar outsourcing loss, the client stated the killing objection —
  that they believed they could achieve the same savings in-house — at the *first* meeting;
  it went unaddressed for 18 months, and the postmortem found viable resolutions had existed.
- **Checklists become interrogations.** [REAL:TWENTYQ] run in one sitting, [REAL:SOFT-EVIDENCE-QS]
  read out sequentially, [REAL:PEEL-ONION] stacked without narration between layers, and
  [REAL:KEYWORD-MEANING] applied to every term — all convert dialogue into cross-examination.
  The book's own negative anchor: a buyer handed 53 questions for a one-hour meeting.
- **The End in Mind fails four specific ways:** absent; overreaching for the stage; worded as
  the answer you want (provokes resistance); or never agreed before or at the start — the
  authors' own CEO meeting died this way, swallowed by a rambling sponsor introduction and
  ending in "keep talking".
- **[REAL:CHECKOFF] asked clumsily reads as coercive** — clients have decided against sellers
  while liking the solution. Also, some people never award a 10; 8–9 may be the real ceiling.
- **[REAL:MATCHLEAD] and every scripted response** fail when the wording is transplanted without
  the intent behind it; the authors state plainly that their exact language will not fit every
  speaker and supply no substitute calibration beyond "build your own".
- **Going around the gatekeeper** ([REAL:BLOCKED4] option 2) is explicitly a risk trade, not a
  default; the political cost routinely exceeds the information gained.
- **[REAL:WALKTALK] carries career risk**, half-acknowledged: the single told case ends in a win
  after the seller spent the night fearing for her job.
- **[REAL:ANCHORSTATE] and [REAL:FOURVS]** are soft appendices with no evidence of effect;
  presenting either as a mechanism (rather than a personal ritual) is unsupported.

## 4. Numbers that must not be quoted as fact

All of the following are self-reported by the authors or their client, or third-party but
unverified. They may be cited as *what the book claims*, never as an established rate.

- **Reach / marketing copy:** coursework in 40+ countries and 9 languages; 100,000+ copies of
  the first self-published edition; FranklinCovey's self-description (~1,500 associates, 35
  international offices, 140+ countries, 90% of the Fortune 100, 75%+ of the Fortune 500).
- **RFP audit:** 46 unsolicited-RFP responses over 18 months, ~$80,000 average direct cost each,
  win rate zero, ~$3,680,000 of pursuit cost behind a single hypothetical win. One client's own
  study, self-reported.
- **Pipeline scrub:** several billion dollars across ~400 deals, with the leader wanting to
  pursue at most 200.
- **Word-overlap exercise:** tens of thousands of participants, predicted 3–4 shared synonyms,
  actual overlap almost always zero. Self-collected workshop data, no control.
- **ES Research:** 65% of salespeople pursue worthless deals; 80% of lost opportunities trace to
  inadequate or nonexistent qualification. Third party, unverified here.
- **Win/loss engagement:** deals won at prices up to 30% above competitors and lost at prices up
  to 30% below; sellers ranked price and relationships as most important to buyers while buyers
  ranked them low and ranked understanding their business challenges high; a modelled 6–8% sales
  increase from demonstrable gains in three skill areas. Client-specific and self-reported —
  the 6–8% in particular is a *model output*, not an observed lift.
- **Rules of thumb with no source:** 80% of impact in 20% of issues; 95/5 for prospecting;
  ~80% of the time the client wants help getting evidence they cannot get alone; a 2–3 year
  management horizon; Y ≤ X + 25–50% for the investment range; ~30 minutes per stakeholder;
  the 1–10 read (8–10 motivating, 7 keep talking, ≤6 yellow); 3–7 stepping stones; 3–5
  questions per first meeting; 30–40 days to form a habit; 20–30 minutes/week on network
  expansion, first leads within ~60 days, 5+ inbound prospects per good referral source per year.
- **Unsourced assertions:** that decision makers spend about five minutes per proposal looking
  mainly at price and a summary; the price-anchoring and concession-satisfaction claims
  attributed to unnamed "negotiation research".
- **Worked impact examples are illustrations, not benchmarks:** the margin 28→30% ≈ $2M/yr case,
  the quality 10→5 rejections per 1,000 units ≈ $15M/yr case, the $10M/yr deferred three years
  = $30M case, the NewLogo $36M estimate against the CFO's $3M counter-anchor, and We-Do-It's
  100 → 5 → 3 with all three won. Every one is the authors' own arithmetic on their own deals.

## 5. Structural blind spots — where this lens should stay quiet

- **No independent validation of the method.** ORDER, the Meeting Plan, yellow-light resolution,
  the five P's, the 20 questions and the SPI are all FranklinCovey practitioner material. Every
  named external citation supports context, never a technique's effect. FranklinCovey sells this
  methodology as training; the "evidence" inside the book is also its marketing.
- **Survivorship in the war stories.** The told cases end in wins — the declined proposal the
  board reversed, the flight home from London, the reordered decision sequence. The losing runs
  of the same high-risk moves are absent.
- **Transactional, self-serve and product-led motions are outside the field of view.** Nothing
  on pricing pages, trials, PLG loops, channel or marketplace selling, or buyer journeys that
  complete without a seller.
- **No dialogue on offer = no method.** With no-contact RFPs, portal-only procurement and closed
  policy processes, what remains is a set of exit rules ([REAL:RFP-HUMAN-RULE], [REAL:BLOCKED4],
  [REAL:WALKTALK]), not a way to sell.
- **Solo buyer with signing authority.** The grid, the access ladder, the Meeting Plan and the
  readiness gate all assume a committee with staged decisions; against a single owner they are
  overhead that slows a green deal.
- **Low-ACV, high-volume motions cannot fund the method** — 95/5 prospecting, 30 minutes per
  stakeholder, pre-call hypothesis testing and multi-meeting qualification have a real per-deal
  cost that the book never compares against the win-rate gain it claims.
- **Buyers who already completed their own diagnosis** and are buying a specified commodity on
  price and speed: move-off-the-solution reads as deflection, repeated peeling as inability to
  answer.
- **Confidentiality-constrained deals.** The central metric misreads regulated or
  security-constrained silence as disinterest.
- **Value creation versus value split.** The "we both want the same thing" premise holds for
  making the pie bigger and fails for dividing it — which is exactly why the price chapter is
  the thinnest, leaning on unnamed negotiation research.
- **Buyer-side power asymmetry is under-modelled.** Against professional procurement running a
  deliberately sealed process, the book offers exit rather than strategy.
- **Cultural and personality variance is unhandled by choice**, with no substitute calibration.
- **Team, compensation and quota design are absent.** The book coaches the individual consultant
  and briefly the coach; it never addresses how comp plans, territory design and forecasting
  rituals push sellers into precisely the behaviours it condemns.
