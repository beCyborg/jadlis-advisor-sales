# Resonate: Present Visual Stories that Transform Audiences — digest

## What this book is for the lens

This is the lens's architecture source for the prepared set-piece: the finals presentation, the exec readout, the launch keynote, the investor pitch. Its mechanism claim is that persuasion runs on a deliberately maintained imbalance — the presenter states the agreed present, states a contradicting possible future, and keeps oscillating until a call to action closes the gap. It beats the dialogue-oriented sales canon on three things no other source in the council covers at this depth: how to sequence a long persuasive block so attention survives it, how to engineer one moment the buyer still retells two weeks later at decision time, and how to build the message so a champion can re-deliver it accurately in a room you are not in. It is weakest exactly where the other sources are strong — questioning, negotiation, pipeline mechanics — and it says so itself.

## Core model

Reports organise facts by topic; stories organise scenes dramatically; a presentation sits between them and is an *explanation*. Duarte builds a **presentation form** by adapting Freytag's pyramid, Syd Field's three-act paradigm and the Campbell/Vogler **Hero's Journey**, then validates it post hoc by overlaying it on Jobs's 2007 iPhone launch and King's 1963 "I Have a Dream" — the **sparkline**. Unlike drama it has no single climax; it oscillates. Beginning ≤10% of runtime states *what is*; a **call to adventure** opens the gap; the middle alternates on three axes (content, emotional register, delivery mode); a **call to action** names discrete tasks split across four actor temperaments (doers, suppliers, influencers, innovators); the ending sits above the start in **new bliss**.

Stance: the audience is the hero (Luke), the presenter the mentor (Yoda), because the audience executes the change. Content is chosen against a destination — the **big idea** (one sentence, point of view plus stakes) and a **from/to** map on two registers, *being* (belief) and *doing* (behaviour). Resistance is structural, not personal: adoption costs a **sacrifice**, so name it and pay a proportional **reward** from seven categories. Content is produced divergently (Ch.5, "Create Meaningful Content": pan for gold, keep generating past the obvious idea, balance ethos/pathos/logos and Olson's four organs), then convergently (filter against the big idea, cluster into 3–5 MECE topics, rewrite neutral topics as charged sentences). Ch.6 "Structure Reveals Insights" moves the deck out of the software onto a wall and picks one of eight organisational patterns. Ch.7 adds the **S.T.A.R. moment** (Something They'll Always Remember) in five types. Ch.8 models delivery as signal transmission degraded by four kinds of noise (credibility, semantic, experiential, bias), corrected by hostile screening and rehearsal, both gated by Conway's Law. Ch.9 and the ethics chapter close the loop: adoption needs a *series* of presentations, and the craft works equally well on false claims, so it needs its own governance.

## Moves

### Stage A — Account prep and audience research

### [RESO:HEROGRID] Run the six-cell audience grid
**Move:** Before drafting, answer six question sets about the specific people in the room — Lifestyle (what is likable about them, where they spend time), Knowledge (what they already know, their sources, their biases), Motivation and Desire (what is lacking, what gets them moving), Values (how they spend time and money, what unites or incites them), Influence (who and what shapes their decisions, how they decide), Respect (how they give and receive it). Keep going until you are describing people rather than job titles.
**APPLY WHEN:** Account planning before a decisive meeting; champion-mapping in committee deals, where the Influence cell maps directly onto buying-committee dynamics.
**AVOID WHEN:** The answers would be pure invention — guessed answers create false confidence. Prefer discovery calls over imagination whenever you have access.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise/stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — Duarte's Visual Story methodology worksheets across client engagements; no outcome data.

### [RESO:DEEPPREP] Research past the attendee list
**Move:** Buy or scan their trade publications, read the annual report and press releases, find their own past presentations, read the top blogs and social conversation in their space, and where possible run a short survey of the audience. Accept that most of what you learn never appears in the deck — its job is to produce empathy.
**APPLY WHEN:** High-stakes single meeting; unfamiliar industry; a large deal where one session sets direction.
**AVOID WHEN:** Volume/transactional motion where per-meeting prep cost exceeds deal value; also when the research is substituting for simply asking the buyer.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/founder-led · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=enterprise/consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — the author's preparation for a beer-manufacturer keynote.

### [RESO:SEGMENT-LEVERAGE] Segment for maximum supporters per unit of effort
**Move:** Split the audience into subgroups and pick the one most likely to adopt with least effort — the group whose adoption pulls the others — then build the core message for them while keeping the wider room addressed.
**APPLY WHEN:** Large or mixed audiences; conference and webinar demand generation; internal rollouts; committee deals with an identifiable swing group.
**AVOID WHEN:** The segmentation would be clichéd or stereotyped — broad or generalised segmentation makes people feel like a statistic and can offend.
**Deal-gate:** motion=inbound/founder-led/expansion · acv=any · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — a 48-person beer-executive engagement (34 male, 14 female, 11 countries) as the single illustration.

### [RESO:PROCESSSEG] Locate them in a process and move them exactly one phase
**Move:** Instead of segmenting by who they are, segment by where they are stuck, and aim the session at the next phase only. Named ladders: project process (analyze, design, develop, implement, evaluate); sales cycle (awareness, interest, desire, evaluation, action, loyalty); adoption (innovators, early adopters, majority, laggards).
**APPLY WHEN:** Pipeline work — the book's most directly sales-mechanical move; use it to set the single goal of a call, e.g. move the account from interest to evaluation.
**AVOID WHEN:** The audience is scattered across phases with no dominant cluster; aiming at a phase most of the room has already passed wastes the meeting.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=transactional/consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** assertion — the stage lists are presented as common taxonomies without sourcing.

### [RESO:ROLESHIFT] Address each sub-audience in its own register
**Move:** When one room holds groups with different emotional stakes, speak to each in turn in language fitted to it, then reconnect them to the shared whole — changing your own role (analyst, peer, advocate) as you move between them.
**APPLY WHEN:** Situational segmentation exists — mixed-level rooms, all-hands after bad news, deals where the economic buyer and the end users sit together.
**AVOID WHEN:** Conventional demographic or political splits would be divisive; also when the role-shifting exceeds your actual standing and reads as performance.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=high · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=enterprise/stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — Reagan's Challenger address, read through Eidenmuller's *Great Speeches for Better Speaking*.

### [RESO:ONEPERSON] Aim the delivery at one person in the room
**Move:** Deliver as if speaking to a single specific listener whose situation you genuinely understand, rather than to the aggregate. The anchor is Martha Graham's account of a bereaved mother who could not cry until she saw *Lamentation* — the effect landed on one person and that was sufficient.
**APPLY WHEN:** Committee presentations where one economic buyer or one blocker dominates; large-audience talks where generic framing has flattened the message.
**AVOID WHEN:** The decision genuinely requires broad consensus and you would visibly neglect the rest of the room.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=any · market=stage/consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — Graham's retold story, sourced to a video transcription.

### [RESO:KAIROSCALIBRATE] Set the emotional/analytical ratio to the audience type
**Move:** Classify the audience as broad (visceral, story-weighted, receptive to emotion, reached through heart and gut) versus analytical (cerebral, report-weighted, suspicious of emotion, reached through the head), then set the ratio: for a stereotypically analytical room keep the emotional share tiny but never zero — at minimum open and close with *why*; for an emotionally driven room give a few proof points rather than twenty detail slides. Duarte adds *kairos* (the right thing at the right moment) to ethos/pathos/logos as the timing check.
**APPLY WHEN:** Deciding how much story to bring to economists, scientists, engineers, researchers or procurement — or conversely to a sales force.
**AVOID WHEN:** Overamplified in either direction — imbalance is explicitly said to damage credibility, and overdone emotion is read as manipulation.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=enterprise/investor/stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — a stated calibration heuristic with a comparison table; no data behind the two audience profiles.

### [RESO:PREFRAME] Manage the pre-meeting materials as part of the first impression
**Move:** Audit everything the audience received before entering the room — invitation wording, agenda framing, email tone, your bio — and shape each to the impression you want; then decide deliberately what they experience first (room, lighting, music, opening screen, your clothing, how you enter). Make the similarities and differences between you and them explicit early so the room finishes sizing you up quickly.
**APPLY WHEN:** First meetings with a new committee; any event where you are one of several vendors.
**AVOID WHEN:** The staging tips into a gimmick — the first impression should reveal character, motivation, ability and vulnerability, not stage a trick.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=enterprise/stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — the author's contrast between her own rushed workshop openings and Garr Reynolds's warm pre-session behaviour.

### Stage B — Defining the ask before any content exists

### [RESO:BIGIDEA3] Test the whole message against three criteria
**Move:** Compress the entire presentation to one sentence and check it: it states your point of view rather than a topic; it names what is at stake in adopting or refusing; it is a complete sentence with a noun and a verb, ideally containing "you." If it reads like "Q3 update" or "the new platform," it fails.
**APPLY WHEN:** Every proposal, QBR, board pitch or client sales call, before any slide exists.
**AVOID WHEN:** Nothing genuine is at stake — gravity must match real severity, and a manufactured stake produces the manipulation backfire.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — worked examples, including a client sales call reframed as software giving customers record access, saving employee time and raising margins by 2 percent.

### [RESO:FROMTO] Map the from/to on both registers before generating content
**Move:** Write where the audience is now and where they must be, separately for the inward change (belief, identity, feeling) and the outward change (observable behaviour), then a plain benefit line stating what they get. Treat the outward change as the evidence that the inward one landed, and generate no material until all three are fixed.
**APPLY WHEN:** Multi-meeting cycles, deal reviews, any high-stakes set-piece where you control the agenda — board readout, kickoff, all-hands, investor pitch, big-deal final presentation; also before rebuilding a deck for a stalled account.
**AVOID WHEN:** Discovery or first calls where you do not yet know their current state — you would be inventing the "from" and speaking to a fiction. Also when the situation genuinely needs a document: with no from/to worth defining, distribute a report instead.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led/expansion · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=stage/investor/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — modelled on the screenplay character arc, including the story-analyst practice of judging a script by comparing first and last page; no dataset.

### [RESO:MOVEMATRIX] Build content as a move-from / move-to / benefit / personal-story matrix
**Move:** For each theme in the talk write four cells: move from being X to being Y (internal), move from doing X to doing Y (external), the benefit that follows, and one personal story showing you have lived it — including a story where you were reluctant, wrong or coached hard.
**APPLY WHEN:** Internal change leadership, team enablement, and any presentation where your credibility rests on having made the same change yourself.
**AVOID WHEN:** Personal-failure stories would undercut authority with a buyer who has no relationship with you yet; the vulnerability move assumes a floor of standing.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led/expansion · acv=any · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — reverse-engineered from Beth Comstock's GE "Growth in a Downturn?" presentation across four themes.

### [RESO:PAINPLEASURE] Set the two-sided stake
**Move:** Frame the big idea so that refusing it raises expected pain and lowers expected pleasure while accepting it does the reverse. Concretely: state what deteriorates if nothing changes, in terms this specific audience feels.
**APPLY WHEN:** Status-quo bias is the real competitor; the buyer agrees the problem exists but not that it is urgent.
**AVOID WHEN:** The threat would be exaggerated, or the audience has no lived experience of the risk — precisely the condition that made the author's own downturn warning read as manipulation.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative/stage
**Evidence:** assertion for the two-emotion reduction; a named Kotter HBR piece (Jan 2007) is cited for change efforts beginning with hard looks at competitive position, crises and timely opportunities.

### [RESO:SACRIFICE] Price the sacrifice before you ask
**Move:** Answer in writing what the buyer gives up to say yes — which beliefs get abandoned, what it costs in time and money — and what they perceive as risk: physical, emotional, reputational, and who they will have to confront. Then acknowledge that cost out loud instead of pretending adoption is free.
**APPLY WHEN:** A champion must spend political capital; switching costs are real; any change that visibly makes someone wrong.
**AVOID WHEN:** Naming the sacrifice would introduce a doubt the buyer did not have — the technique acknowledges known costs, it does not invent them.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=enterprise/consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — a Duarte worksheet framed on the Hero's Journey threshold-reluctance stage.

### [RESO:REWARD7] Name the reward in a category the audience actually wants
**Move:** Choose the payoff from seven types — basic needs, security, savings of time or money, prize, recognition, relationship, destiny — and check it across three scopes: benefit to them personally, to their sphere of influence, and to humanity or the planet where warranted. Keep it proportional to the sacrifice you just named.
**APPLY WHEN:** Closing sections; ROI conversations that currently cover only savings; mission-driven or internal-change audiences where recognition and relationship outperform money.
**AVOID WHEN:** The reward is disproportionate to a small ask, or only one scope is credible and the other two are padding.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — categories asserted, echoing a Maslow-like ladder without citing it.

### [RESO:MENTORGIFT] Define what you hand over
**Move:** For the same meeting, answer three questions — Guidance (what insight helps them navigate), Confidence (how you reduce their reluctance), Tools (what skill, roadmap or artifact they keep) — and make sure at least one item is valuable even if they never buy.
**APPLY WHEN:** Consultative and enterprise cycles where trust compounds across meetings; the discovery-to-proposal transition.
**AVOID WHEN:** Giving away the deliverable is the business model, or "free value" is being used to avoid asking for the commitment.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=any · offer=service/consulting/saas · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — framed on the Mr. Miyagi mentor pattern; the book notes mentors do gain something, but the hero must gain more.

### Stage C — Content generation and filtering

### [RESO:PANFORGOLD] Mine wide before you invent anything
**Move:** Sweep every available source for raw material — industry studies, competitor messaging, news, partner programmes, surveys — going both wide and into adjacent topics, and collect competitor messages specifically so you can position against them rather than echo them.
**APPLY WHEN:** Preparing a high-stakes deck where differentiation from other vendors matters; you are one of several presenters.
**AVOID WHEN:** The substitute is a recycled peer deck — reusing someone else's slides is flagged as a way to fail to connect.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=enterprise/stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the firm's standard content-prep process.

### [RESO:ROUND34] Keep generating past the obvious idea
**Move:** Do not stop at the first plausible idea; run repeated rounds of generation on the theme with judgment suspended, on the expectation that the good material appears around the third or fourth pass.
**APPLY WHEN:** Message and positioning work with real time before the meeting.
**AVOID WHEN:** Same-day prep — the method is time-expensive by design.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=stage
**Evidence:** assertion — "third or fourth round" is stated without supporting data.

### [RESO:NIGHTNOTES] Accumulate raw material over weeks, then script
**Move:** Keep an ongoing capture of thoughts, observations, quotations and impressions relevant to the idea, and let a pattern emerge from the accumulation before writing the detailed script.
**APPLY WHEN:** Major narratives you will reuse — category pitch, founder story, board narrative — where you have weeks rather than hours.
**AVOID WHEN:** A deal-cycle deliverable is due tomorrow; this is a slow method and substitutes badly for a structured outline under time pressure.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — biographical account of Martha Graham writing at night until the pattern appeared.

### [RESO:THREEAPPEALS] Balance ethos, logos and pathos deliberately
**Move:** Audit the material for three separate appeals — shared values and experience (credibility), claim-plus-evidence structure (logic), appeals to pain or pleasure (emotion) — and add whichever is missing instead of letting one dominate.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any persuasive oral delivery, especially where the deck currently reads as a report.
**AVOID WHEN:** Written scientific or technical reports — flat and factual is conceded to be fine there.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=stage/investor
**Evidence:** external-research — Aristotle's *Rhetoric* named as the source of the tripartite scheme; it is a rhetorical framework, not an empirical study.

### [RESO:FOURORGANS] Diagnose and deliberately cross your own register
**Move:** Identify your natural communication register on Olson's head / heart / gut / groin scale, then build material from the registers you avoid: generate from the lower, instinctive registers first, move up to the head to analyse what you produced, and cycle back and forth.
**APPLY WHEN:** A technical seller facing a mixed or emotionally driven buyer, or a charismatic seller facing an analytical committee.
**AVOID WHEN:** The audience is homogeneously analytical and evidence-hungry — emotion-only delivery destroys credibility there.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=any · market=stage/investor
**Evidence:** external-research — attributed to Randy Olson's named four-organs framework; the framework itself is rhetorical, not measured.

### [RESO:POLARLIST] Write the intelligent opposite of every claim
**Move:** Take each brainstormed idea and write its strongest counter-position; work down the full list of contrasting elements, then repeat the exercise in reverse order to shake out more; keep the counter-arguments you never intend to use.
**APPLY WHEN:** Committee sales where an internal champion has to defend your position without you in the room.
**AVOID WHEN:** You lack the standing to state the opposing view credibly — a strawman version costs more than silence.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=enterprise/stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the firm's exercise; the applause study cited nearby speaks to contrast's effect, not to this drill.

### [RESO:GAPFRAME] Frame the gap in a form other than past-versus-future
**Move:** Rather than defaulting to today-versus-tomorrow, pick whichever contrast fits — the customer without your product versus with it, the world from their point of view versus from yours — any gap between where the audience is and where your perspective would put them.
**APPLY WHEN:** The "today is bad, tomorrow is good" framing would insult the buyer's own past decisions or their current vendor.
**AVOID WHEN:** The audience feels no gap at all — the whole architecture assumes a change is being proposed.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=consultative/stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — core to the sparkline method across the analysed speeches.

### [RESO:HUMANIZEFEATURE] Attach a human to every feature
**Move:** For each capability you would otherwise list, name the person it affects and tell the short story of that effect; hold the technical explanation until after the audience cares — the trick first, the exposure of the trick second.
**APPLY WHEN:** Technical product demos and capability readouts; the Cisco case rewrote a unified-communications feature list as a small-business owner's story.
**AVOID WHEN:** The buyer explicitly asked for spec-level comparison and has already bought the "why."
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=product/saas · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — a single named client case (Cisco Systems, "Hop to It").

### [RESO:STORYCATALOG] Build a personal story catalog by people, places and things
**Move:** Instead of recalling stories chronologically, walk memory through people (family tree plus non-hierarchical links — teacher/student, boss/coworker, friend/enemy), places (room by room, sketching detail) and things (sentimental objects sketched in their usual setting); record the emotion each memory carried and file it for reuse.
**APPLY WHEN:** You need a credible personal anecdote to carry one specific emotion and can prepare in advance.
**AVOID WHEN:** Improvising in the moment — the point of the catalog is that the story was pre-selected for the emotion you need.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led/any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=stage/consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — the author's own floor-plan sketch surfacing a childhood story.

### [RESO:SHORTSTORY] Run dry information through a compressed Hero's Journey
**Move:** Convert a fact block into a short story shape — hero introduced with a reason to root for them, conflict set up without revealing the resolution, a complication raising the stakes, a solution that is not easy, incremental steps to resolution, and a final movement lifting the hero from one state to another.
**APPLY WHEN:** The material is inherently dry and must be repeated accurately by people who were not in the room.
**AVOID WHEN:** The buyer's own situation is the "story" and dramatising it would feel presumptuous.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=stage/consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — template adapted from the named Hero's Journey structure and applied across client work.

### [RESO:NUMBERNARRATIVE] Give every important number scale, comparison and context
**Move:** For each figure that matters, do one of three things: express its scale against something of familiar size; compare it to a similar-magnitude number from a different domain; or explain the environmental and strategic factors driving the movement on the chart.
**APPLY WHEN:** Business reviews, quarterly readouts, market-size claims — anywhere a number is expected to move a decision.
**AVOID WHEN:** The comparison is strained; Otellini's "cars at 470,000 mph" only lands because the underlying ratio is real.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — three named worked examples (WaterPartners.org, Intel/Otellini, a Duarte vision deck) plus Stephen Few's *Now You See It*.

### [RESO:MURDERDARLINGS] Filter every idea against the one big idea
**Move:** After generating, switch modes: re-read the big idea and delete everything that does not distinctly support it, including material you are attached to — then re-check that you did not prune the contrast out along the way.
**APPLY WHEN:** Before any presentation with a fixed runtime whose audience sees many similar decks.
**AVOID WHEN:** Filtering before generating — the sequence requires divergence to be exhausted first, or you converge on the obvious idea.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** external-research — the divergent/convergent distinction is credited to J. P. Guilford (1967); the filtering rule itself is practitioner method (Tim Brown's funnel).

### [RESO:MECE] Cluster the survivors into 3–5 MECE topics
**Move:** Spread the surviving ideas into roughly three (up to five) major topics that do not overlap and leave nothing relevant out — McKinsey's mutually-exclusive/collectively-exhaustive test — then hang three to five supporting ideas under each.
**APPLY WHEN:** Structuring any multi-message presentation, especially where an omission would be noticed (skipping one competitor, one region, one risk).
**AVOID WHEN:** The content genuinely has one point — forcing three topics manufactures filler.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=enterprise/stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — MECE named as a McKinsey thinking process, applied here as a clustering rule.

### [RESO:TOPICTOMESSAGE] Rewrite neutral topics as charged sentences
**Move:** Convert each one-word topic into a full sentence carrying an emotional charge and, where needed, the contrast: "Market" becomes a statement that an aggressive competitor is taking share; "Acquisition" becomes a statement that this one will work *because* lessons were taken from the failed prior one.
**APPLY WHEN:** The audience carries a known negative history you would otherwise skip past; agenda slides currently read as nouns.
**AVOID WHEN:** The charge would overstate a certainty you cannot defend.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — a worked topic-to-message table from an acquisition all-hands.

### Stage D — Structuring the set-piece

### [RESO:SPATIALLAYOUT] Pull the deck out of the software and lay it out spatially
**Move:** Print or sticky-note the messages and arrange them on a wall or the floor, then assess the whole rather than the parts — where the weight sits, how many supporting points hang off each message, which sections are missing, whether one big idea holds it together.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any multi-message deck before slide production; also as the artifact a review team reads, since structure is absorbed faster from an outline than from slides.
**AVOID WHEN:** Not stated as contraindicated by the book; skip only when the talk carries a single message.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=enterprise/stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the firm's standard method plus one client anecdote (a CMO who replaced the deck with an outline for her CEO).

### [RESO:PICKPATTERN] Choose an organisational pattern deliberately
**Move:** Instead of defaulting to topical, pick the arrangement that fits the message — chronological, sequential, spatial or climactic when you want story feel; problem-solution, compare-contrast, cause-effect or advantage-disadvantage when you need persuasion, since contrast is built into those four. Then signpost the pattern verbally or visually so the audience always knows where they are.
**APPLY WHEN:** Persuasive proposals (problem-solution, cause-effect), competitive bake-offs (compare-contrast), risk-heavy decisions (advantage-disadvantage).
**AVOID WHEN:** The pattern is chosen for novelty rather than fit — the rule is whichever makes most sense for the message.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — a standard rhetorical taxonomy applied to presentations.

### [RESO:REORDERQ3] Re-sequence fixed material for the emotional endpoint you want
**Move:** Fix the desired end-state feeling first ("confident and motivated to help"), then reorder the existing points so each builds on the last and the sequence culminates rather than trails off, adding a small dose of emotional appeal at the crescendo.
**APPLY WHEN:** QBRs, quarterly updates, renewal and expansion reviews where the facts are fixed and only order is negotiable.
**AVOID WHEN:** Reordering would bury a material negative the audience is entitled to hear early.
**Deal-gate:** motion=expansion/any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=enterprise/stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one before/after example (a Q3 update), no measured outcome.

### [RESO:SONATA3] Set the theme, vary it, restate it transformed
**Move:** Open by establishing the central idea plainly enough that it stays recognisable when altered; spend the middle varying and pressure-testing it so the audience can hear the distance travelled; close by restating the original idea now visibly changed. Structure is what lets the listener anticipate what comes next.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any structured presentation over roughly ten minutes; QBRs and strategy reviews where a recurring theme carries the argument.
**AVOID WHEN:** Discovery calls and unscripted conversations, where a pre-composed three-part arc stops you following the buyer.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — an analogy argued from a sonata analysis of Mozart's *Eine kleine Nachtmusik*, first movement.

### [RESO:WHATIS-BASELINE] Open by stating the agreed current reality
**Move:** Begin with a concise, accurate description of the buyer's present situation — what everyone already accepts as true, including the problem they live with — and keep it to roughly the first tenth of your time.
**APPLY WHEN:** Discovery has given you enough real detail to be specific; any presentation to people whose context you must prove you understand.
**AVOID WHEN:** You have not done discovery — a generic current-state slide signals no homework and destroys the credibility it was meant to build. Also skip when the buyer called the meeting to hear the proposal only.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=stage/consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — from the author's speech-corpus analysis plus firm practice; the 10% ceiling is a rule of thumb, not measured.

### [RESO:CALLADVENTURE] Manufacture the gap as one explicit turning point
**Move:** Immediately after the baseline, deliver one memorable statement of *what could be* that visibly contradicts the current reality, so the room feels the imbalance for the first time at a single identifiable moment. Make it explicit rather than implied.
**APPLY WHEN:** You hold a genuinely different point of view and evidence of stakes; the deal's real competitor is "do nothing."
**AVOID WHEN:** The gap is manufactured or the stakes exaggerated — gravity must match real severity, no more and no less; inflating it is the documented manipulation failure mode.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — mapped post hoc onto the 2007 iPhone launch and the 1963 King speech; paralleled to McKee's inciting incident.

### [RESO:TRIPLECONTRAST] Oscillate the middle on several dimensions at once
**Move:** Build the body so it moves back and forth on content (what is versus what could be, their view versus yours), on emotional register (analytical versus emotional), and on delivery (traditional versus non-traditional — a demo, handing something over, leaving the podium, having them do something); never leave three consecutive beats in the same position. The musical restatement of the same rule: vary key (departure from and return to a home tonic), dynamics (loud/soft, both sudden and gradual) and texture (unison versus a lead line with accompaniment, few ideas in play versus many).
**APPLY WHEN:** Sessions long enough to lose attention (20+ minutes); rooms with mixed roles and mixed engagement; recorded content; any deck reviewers called flat or monotonous.
**AVOID WHEN:** Short, transactional, single-decision conversations, where oscillation only delays the ask — engineered variation inside a five-minute status read is noise; also avoid non-traditional delivery with audiences whose culture reads it as gimmickry.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=any · market=stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the sparkline analysis method across the book's case studies (Zander's 2008 TED talk annotated on all three axes); the musical layer is analogy extended by assertion.

### [RESO:BEATS] Inventory and interleave analytical and emotional slides like film beats
**Move:** Label each slide analytical (diagram, feature, data, evidence, example, case study, exhibit, system, process, facts, documentation) or emotional (story, benefit, analogy or metaphor, prop or dramatisation, suspenseful reveal, shocking statement, evocative image, invitation to wonder, humour, surprise, offer); then convert some analytical items into emotional ones and interleave, so the register shifts continuously instead of running one long analytical block.
**APPLY WHEN:** Long-form presentations (30+ minutes) where attention decays; decks currently built entirely from the analytical column.
**AVOID WHEN:** Short technical Q&A; and where the audience is stereotypically analytical, the emotional share must stay small.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=stage/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the screenwriting "beats" analogy is named craft practice, not measured.

### [RESO:CLIFFHANGER] Break the story open to deliver the hard data
**Move:** Suspend the narrative at its peak of tension, use that window to explain the mechanism, technology or data, then return and resolve — the audience holds information the characters do not, which is what keeps them attentive through the analytical block.
**APPLY WHEN:** You must present dense technical proof to an audience that would otherwise disengage.
**AVOID WHEN:** Time is tight enough that you might not get back to the resolution — an unresolved cliffhanger reads as disorganisation.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=product/saas · market=stage/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — demonstrated in the Cisco case study only.

### [RESO:MASTERSTORY] Thread one master story through the whole talk
**Move:** Choose a single story with a strong central image, tell it early, thread references to its motif through every subsequent section, and close by returning to the opening story so the audience re-reads it with what they now know.
**APPLY WHEN:** Long-form talks whose theme must survive the room; recurring audiences you address repeatedly.
**AVOID WHEN:** The motif is too thin to bear repetition, at which point it reads as a gimmick.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one analysed sermon (John Ortberg, Menlo Park Presbyterian).

### Stage E — Artifact production and time discipline

### [RESO:ONEIDEASLIDE] One idea per slide, sketched before software
**Move:** Give each message its own slide (slides are free), sketch each as a small thumbnail on paper first so the constraint forces simple words and pictures, and prefer large type over dense prose when you have no image. Convert bullet lists to visuals by circling the verbs or nouns and identifying the relationship among them — flow (process), structure (classification), cluster (groupings), radiate (links and nodes), influence (cause and effect).
**APPLY WHEN:** Building any deck meant to be presented rather than read.
**AVOID WHEN:** The artifact is a leave-behind document — a document should be dense on purpose.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=stage/enterprise

### [RESO:SLIDEDIET] Strip slides to under three seconds and move prose to the notes

**APPLY WHEN:** Live presentation with a screen behind you.
**AVOID WHEN:** The deliverable is a read-alone document — a boss asking for a five-slide overview is asking for a document, not a presentation.

### [RESO:UNDERTIME] Take less time than you were given
**Move:** Impose your own constraint below the slot offered — target roughly 40 minutes when given an hour — and let the restriction force clear structure and hard filtering down to only the imperative messages.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any fixed-slot presentation, especially where Q&A is worth more than monologue.
**AVOID WHEN:** No contraindication is named; the counter-example is Lincoln taking two minutes of an available two hours and being judged superior to the two-hour speaker.

### [RESO:OUTLINEFIRST] Give the senior reviewer an outline, not a slide walkthrough

**APPLY WHEN:** Internal prep with an executive sponsor who habitually derails slide walkthroughs; also with a buyer-side champion reviewing your deck before the committee meeting.
**AVOID WHEN:** The reviewer's objection is to the visuals or the story feel — an outline hides exactly that.

### [RESO:TWOFRAMEWORKS] Build a written framework first, then a visual one

**APPLY WHEN:** High-stakes finals presentations, launch keynotes, anything worth a team's time; whenever slide-building has been starting before the argument exists.

### [RESO:TEAMLIFT] Let collaborators modify the vision, then credit them
**Move:** Hand collaborators a suggestion and let them expand it rather than executing your version exactly, and explicitly credit the people who built the presentation you deliver. The persona on stage is one person; the artifact is a team output.
**APPLY WHEN:** Sales teams with dedicated design, solutions-engineering or proposal functions; recurring pitch programmes.
**AVOID WHEN:** No shared vision exists yet — open modification without a set spine yields a committee deck, exactly the failure the rest of the book targets.

### Stage F — Opening the meeting and establishing credibility

### [RESO:MENTORSTANCE] Flip from hero to mentor
**Move:** Rewrite the deck so the buyer is the protagonist doing the heavy lifting and you are the guide supplying insight, confidence and tools. Audit every slide: if the subject of the sentence is you or your company, it belongs elsewhere. Aim for the buyer leaving with something usable they did not walk in with.
**APPLY WHEN:** Big-room or exec-level pitch where adoption depends on the audience's own will — QBRs, board pitches, kickoff pitches, keynote-style demand generation.
**AVOID WHEN:** The buyer explicitly asked for a vendor-qualification or credentials review; procurement and security reviews where "about us" is the agenda item.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — two decades of the firm's client decks; the mechanism is argued by analogy to the Campbell/Vogler mentor archetype, not tested.

### [RESO:NOABOUTUS] Move the credentials slide out of the deck
**Move:** Strip founding date, HQ, headcount, market cap, logo wall and "recognised leader" claims from the opening; put them in a leave-behind or appendix and open instead on the audience's current reality.
**APPLY WHEN:** The first three slides are company facts and the audience is senior and time-boxed.
**AVOID WHEN:** You are unknown to the buyer and credibility is the live blocker — then place a compact, humble qualification inside common ground rather than deleting it.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — two anonymised real credential slides shown as negative examples; no outcome data.

### [RESO:SAFETYORANGE] Deliberately clash with the category script
**Move:** Before writing, list what every competitor and your own past decks say, then choose framing that visibly contradicts the expected script. Treat "sounds like everyone else" as the primary defect, not "sounds risky."
**APPLY WHEN:** Crowded category; the buyer has heard three similar pitches this month; the message keeps drawing "sounds interesting" and no movement.
**AVOID WHEN:** The buyer is risk-averse or regulated and the contrast would read as unserious — conservative committees can punish novelty in the framing itself.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=stage/consultative

### [RESO:COMMONGROUND] Establish overlap before asserting difference

**APPLY WHEN:** First substantive meeting; cross-industry or cross-culture selling; whenever credibility is being silently assessed.
**AVOID WHEN:** The overlap is fabricated or thin — invented commonality is detectable and costs more than it buys.

### [RESO:ARRIVEEARLY] Arrive with time to spare and work the room
**Move:** Stop cramming work in before the session; be in the room early, upbeat, shaking hands and asking attendees questions, so the audience perceives you have all the time in the world for them before you say your first scripted word.
**APPLY WHEN:** Workshops, briefings, any session you host.
**AVOID WHEN:** No contraindication is named in the book.

### [RESO:DEJARGON] Translate specialist language into the audience's vocabulary
**Move:** Identify every specialised term and acronym in your material and prepare a lay translation; switch registers deliberately depending on who is in the room; never use words outside the listener's vocabulary; treat internal cross-department acronyms as jargon too.
**APPLY WHEN:** Technical seller facing business buyers; scientist or engineer pitching funders; cross-functional committees.
**AVOID WHEN:** The audience *is* specialist and simplification would read as condescension.

### [RESO:SHAREDTEXT] Borrow the audience's own canon

**APPLY WHEN:** You know the buyer's stated values, mission language or public commitments and your proposal genuinely advances them.
**AVOID WHEN:** You cannot honestly claim alignment, or the borrowed text is contested inside the organisation — then it reads as flattery or as taking a side in someone else's fight.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=enterprise/stage

### [RESO:OWNANGLE] Lead with the view only you hold
**Move:** Surface the part of your perspective that differs from the market consensus instead of sanding it off to fit expectations — the differences are what make the position interesting and what deliver a new insight on the topic.
**APPLY WHEN:** Crowded categories, competitive evaluations where every vendor sounds identical, thought-leadership content.
**AVOID WHEN:** The buyer holds a locked-in requirements list and idiosyncrasy scores as risk; also when the "unique view" is untested opinion presented as fact.

### [RESO:HONESTFLAW] Share a real failure, not a polished one
**Move:** Include a genuine account of something you got wrong and how you recovered rather than presenting as flawless; the stated mechanism is reciprocity of openness — the audience opens only if you do.
**APPLY WHEN:** Trust is the bottleneck; renewal or recovery conversations after a service failure; founder-led sales where the buyer is betting on the person.
**AVOID WHEN:** The failure is material to the buying decision and disclosing it casually substitutes for a real remediation plan; also avoid manufactured "humble" failures, which are read instantly as technique.

### Stage G — Carrying the middle: proof, emotion, delivery craft

### [RESO:FACTPLUSFEEL] Pair each proof point with an emotional beat
**Move:** Keep the facts, then attach to each one a human consequence — who is affected and what it feels like — alternating fact, story, fact, story instead of front-loading all the data.
**APPLY WHEN:** An analytical buyer who agrees on paper but does not move; deals stalled at "we're aligned, let's revisit next quarter."
**AVOID WHEN:** Technical evaluation or bake-off stage where the audience is scoring against a spec sheet — emotional layering there reads as evasion; and it is not a licence for half the deck to be feelings.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=stage/consultative

### [RESO:MODESHIFT] Plan several delivery-mode changes per hour
**Move:** Deliberately schedule changes of mode — alternate media, a second presenter, audience interaction, physical movement, a dramatic gesture — several within an hour, each planned rather than improvised, and lower your dependence on slides so the audience feels they interacted with a person.
**APPLY WHEN:** Long sessions; audiences saturated with vendor presentations.
**AVOID WHEN:** Unrehearsed — mode changes must be carefully planned or they read as chaos.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=stage/enterprise

### [RESO:EMBODY] Show the feeling instead of naming it
**Move:** Convey the emotional stakes through demonstration — a lived example, an artifact, a re-enacted moment — instead of asserting that the situation is painful or exciting. The target is that the audience feels it, not that they agree with a description of it.
**APPLY WHEN:** The decision is blocked by inertia rather than by information; the pain is well known but not felt at executive level.
**AVOID WHEN:** Procurement, technical scoring, or a buyer who explicitly asked for data — emotional demonstration where evidence was requested reads as evasion.

### [RESO:VISUALWORD] Speak in images, not abstractions
**Move:** Replace abstract state descriptions with concrete visual language that renders the before and after as places the listener can picture — the cited example moves from a dark, desolate valley to a sunlit path.
**APPLY WHEN:** Describing the cost of the status quo and the post-purchase state; anywhere your category vocabulary has gone abstract.
**AVOID WHEN:** The audience wants specification-level precision — vivid language substituting for numbers is a known credibility leak with technical buyers.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=stage/consultative

### [RESO:PAUSEBREAK] Score the pauses into the script

**APPLY WHEN:** Delivering a high-stakes claim, a price, a number or a closing ask; recorded presentations where the audience cannot signal for a beat.
**AVOID WHEN:** Conversational discovery — engineered pauses in a two-person call read as theatrical or manipulative.

**APPLY WHEN:** Long-form talks (15+ minutes) where a flat tempo would flatten attention.
**AVOID WHEN:** Short pitches and demos — there is not enough runway to build a crescendo, and forced tempo shifts sound erratic.  <!-- privacy-ok: термин из книжного конспекта, не финансы владельца -->

**Move:** Set how rapidly you alternate between the current painful state and the proposed future according to how activated the room already is — fast alternation for an energised, aligned audience; slower, more evidenced swings for a cold or sceptical one.
**APPLY WHEN:** Kickoff talks, user conferences, all-hands, investor days, any already-warm room.
**AVOID WHEN:** A first discovery call or a hostile committee — rapid what-is/what-could-be swings without established facts read as hype.

**AVOID WHEN:** Small rooms, video calls or committee reviews — there is no applause mechanism, and engineered applause lines land as grandstanding.

### Stage H — Engineering the memorable moment

### [RESO:STARPLAN] Engineer one S.T.A.R. moment per presentation
**Move:** Decide in advance which single moment will be the one they retell, build it from one of the five types (memorable dramatisation, repeatable sound bite, evocative visual, emotive storytelling, shocking statistic), and place it where it magnifies the central claim rather than distracting from it. Check it against the audience's temperament before committing.
**APPLY WHEN:** Competitive bake-offs, investor pitches, launch keynotes — any setting where the decision is made days or weeks after you present.
**AVOID WHEN:** The device would be kitschy, cliché or emotionally overcharged for the room; the author's own example is not building something melodramatic for an audience of biochemists.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=high · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=stage/investor/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — four named worked cases (Feynman, Gates, Jobs, Pollan) plus the firm's client practice.

### [RESO:PROPDEMO] Dramatise the abstract number with a physical prop
**Move:** Convert the key quantitative claim into a physical demonstration performed live — the O-ring clamped in ice water; the jar of mosquitoes released after the malaria funding comparison; the burger placed on stage early as an unexplained prop and later paired with crude oil poured glass by glass.
**APPLY WHEN:** The number is genuinely striking, the physical version is honest to it, and you control the room enough to stage it.
**AVOID WHEN:** The demonstration overstates the underlying data, or the setting (small meeting room, video call) makes theatre feel forced.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — three named single cases (Feynman/Challenger, Gates/TED 2009, Pollan/Pop!Tech 2009).

### [RESO:SOUNDBITE] Plant and coordinate a sound bite
**Move:** Write a handful of short, salient phrases; use identical wording in the talk and in the press release so reporters pick up the intended line; include at least one 15–30 second block obviously suited to broadcast; then lock it in three ways — say it more than once, pause afterwards so people can write it down, and put the words on screen.
**APPLY WHEN:** Launches, analyst briefings, anything filmed or live-tweeted; also internal all-hands needing a rally cry.
**AVOID WHEN:** The phrase is a slogan without substance — it has to carry the big idea, not decorate it.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=stage/investor
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — Jobs at Macworld 2007 and the Obama "Yes We Can" example, plus the firm's press-coordination practice.

### [RESO:RHETDEVICE] Shape the key line with a repeatable rhetorical device
**Move:** Build the line you want quoted using one of four devices: imitation of a famous phrase; repetition of words at the start of a series; repetition in the middle of a series; repetition at the end of a series (the Gettysburg "of the people, by the people, for the people" pattern).
**APPLY WHEN:** Crafting the single line you want repeated back to you.
**AVOID WHEN:** Used across a whole talk — the devices work as punctuation, not as texture.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — classical devices with named exemplars (Dickens, Paul, Lincoln).

### [RESO:REFRAIN] Repeat one fixed phrase as a refrain
**Move:** Choose the single phrase carrying the core promise and repeat it verbatim at intervals, most densely near the end, so the audience leaves holding the exact wording.
**APPLY WHEN:** You need a champion to carry your framing into rooms you are not in; launches and category narratives.
**AVOID WHEN:** Technical evaluations and short calls — repetition without emotional altitude sounds like a sales slogan and erodes trust.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — observed in one speech, no comparative test.

### [RESO:PAIREDIMAGE] Pair images to create conflicted emotion
**Move:** Use one full-screen image instead of decorated bullets, or pair two visually similar images with opposite meanings so the contrast carries the argument — ink-stained fingers as the symbol of a first free vote in Iraq (2005) against coerced voting under Mugabe in Zimbabwe (2008); a pristine ocean beside washed-up refuse.
**APPLY WHEN:** The point is abstract (trust, risk, waste, freedom) and words underperform.
**AVOID WHEN:** The imagery outruns what your data supports, which makes the pairing feel manipulative.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — named public examples plus a Conservation International campaign.

### [RESO:STATVISUAL] Rebuild neutral data as a visual story to force urgency
**Move:** When years of neutrally presented data have failed to produce action, pull out the key statistics and stage them as a sequence of images with a tightened runtime — data unchanged, emotional urgency added.
**APPLY WHEN:** A stalled account or market where the facts are already accepted but nothing moves; annual index or benchmark reports.
**AVOID WHEN:** Neutrality is the source of your authority and the audience would read dramatisation as advocacy — the Rauch Foundation had to explicitly keep its "presents data neutrally, does not take sides" position.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=enterprise/stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — one case (Rauch Foundation / Long Island Index, *The Clock is Ticking*, 2010); the "reaction was electric" claim is the client's own report.

### Stage I — Resistance, risk and honest framing

### [RESO:INOCULATE] State the objection before they do, inside the talk
**Move:** Enumerate the likely refusals across six buckets — comfort zone, fear, vulnerabilities, misunderstanding, obstacles, politics — then voice the strongest ones yourself, empathetically, before the audience raises them, placing the answers in the body of the presentation rather than deferring to Q&A. Treat resistance as protection of something they value, pair each stated objection with reassurance rather than rebuttal, and state plainly what is in it for them, their organisation, or a larger cause.
**APPLY WHEN:** A known sceptic is in the room; committee deals with a rival internal agenda; the proposal asks for something costly or identity-relevant; re-engagement after a stalled evaluation.
**AVOID WHEN:** The objection is not actually live, or the group is small enough to simply ask — pre-empting a wrong objection plants it. Also avoid voicing the "politics" reading in a way that exposes an ally.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=enterprise/stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the inoculation analogy plus a named Kotter & Schlesinger HBR piece (July 2008) on the range of reactions to change, from passive resistance to active undermining to sincere embrace; no measured effect.

### [RESO:ANTICIPATERESPONSE] Script alternate sections for anticipated reactions
**Move:** While rehearsing, write down the reactions each point might provoke and prepare alternate passages for each branch; also note where and how you will stand at each stage of the talk.
**APPLY WHEN:** Presentations where reactions are predictable and time is too tight for improvised recovery.
**AVOID WHEN:** Over-scripting kills responsiveness — per Bernstein, the goal is that it still sounds like an unrehearsed conversation.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=high · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=stage/investor
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — Bernstein's archived scripts.

### [RESO:PLAINRISK] State the risk in the headline, not the footnote
**Move:** When you must deliver bad news or a caveat to a decision body, put the warning in the loudest position — a single stark line naming the problem outright — instead of distributing it through dense supporting detail the room can nod past. The counterfactual offered is that one blunt slide calling Enron's practices risky and in need of investigation might have changed the outcome.
**APPLY WHEN:** Presenting risk, audit, security or compliance findings to a committee; when your own margin notes disagree with your slide's tone; when the buyer's team has an incentive not to hear you.
**AVOID WHEN:** The risk is speculative and unsubstantiated — a stark headline on a soft claim destroys credibility; also when a softer private channel would actually get the fix made.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=high · stakeholders=committee · offer=service/consulting · market=enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — a reconstruction of the February 1999 Arthur Andersen audit-committee presentation from press and DOJ sources; the "would have worked" claim is the author's counterfactual.

### [RESO:SLICKCHECK] Suspect your own polish
**Move:** Before delivering, check whether the persuasive force of the deck exceeds the strength of the underlying evidence. If listeners would leave with more confidence than the data supports, cut the polish or add the qualification. The pattern flagged is slick delivery that leaves the room confused but agreeing because it sounded too good to argue with.
**APPLY WHEN:** Presenting projections, forward-looking numbers, or a novel structure the buyer cannot easily verify; when the audience is asked to commit money on your framing alone.
**AVOID WHEN:** Never a reason to under-sell verifiable results — the check is on unsupported claims, not on clarity itself.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=enterprise/investor
**Evidence:** assertion — a moral position illustrated by the Enron narrative rather than tested.

### [RESO:NOBACKDOWN] Do not retreat from a conviction at the first pushback
**Move:** When you believe the claim, restate it confidently instead of softening it at the first sign of resistance, and be willing to name the thing everyone in the room is avoiding, on the Emperor's-New-Clothes model.
**APPLY WHEN:** You have evidence and the room is avoiding an obvious problem; late-stage deals where a buyer's stated plan will predictably fail.
**AVOID WHEN:** You are wrong or under-informed — the book supplies no calibration test, so this is the move most easily degraded into stubbornness; also risky where the person you contradict controls the budget and has no face-saving exit.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=consulting/service · market=enterprise/consultative
**Evidence:** assertion — moral exhortation with a folk-tale illustration.

### Stage J — Close and call to action

### [RESO:FOURACTORS] Give each actor type an action they can actually take
**Move:** In the call to action, list discrete tasks split across four temperaments — doers (assemble, decide, gather, respond, try), suppliers (acquire, fund, resource, support), influencers (activate, adopt, empower, promote), innovators (create, discover, invent, pioneer) — so each person can find at least one action native to their type, and flag which task is most critical.
**APPLY WHEN:** Committee deals where the room contains budget holder, champion, doer and sceptic; multi-threaded enterprise sales; internal-champion enablement.
**AVOID WHEN:** A single decision-maker — a menu of four actions dilutes one clear ask; also when only one action actually matters and the rest are filler.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=enterprise/stage
**Evidence:** assertion — the temperament taxonomy is stated without a cited source or validation.

### [RESO:NEWBLISS] End above where you started
**Move:** Never finish on the to-do list. After the call to action, restate the key points and describe vividly what the buyer's world looks like once the idea is adopted — personally, for their sphere, and larger where warranted — closing by asserting it is not only possible but the better choice.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any presentation designed to end in commitment.
**AVOID WHEN:** An aspirational close would clash with a sober, numbers-only culture, or the audience has already agreed and just wants next steps confirmed.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=stage
**Evidence:** assertion — justified by a "principle of recency" that is named but not sourced, plus a Peter Guber quotation.

### [RESO:ROLETOMISSION] Connect each contributor's role to the bigger mission
**Move:** In the ask, explicitly assign the audience a share of the mission so individual roles read as participation in the outcome rather than as tasks — Kennedy's framing that it would not be one man going to the moon but an entire nation, which the NASA janitor repeated back as putting the first man on the moon.
**APPLY WHEN:** Internal all-hands, partner and channel kickoffs, post-sale rollouts where implementation depends on people who did not buy.
**AVOID WHEN:** The mission framing is not backed by real stakes — grandiosity without substance reads as spin.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led/channel/expansion · acv=any · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=stage/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — the JFK/janitor story, widely retold and unverifiable as told.

### Stage K — Rehearsal, screening and investment sizing

### [RESO:HOSTILESCREEN] Host a screening with chosen naysayers
**Move:** Before delivering, assemble a small group matching the target audience's profile — analysts, employees, trusted customers, a focus group — deliberately including naysayers who will scrutinise and challenge; hand each a printout of slides and notes; run the whole presentation once, then walk it section by section; budget roughly three times the runtime for the review; take the feedback without "yeah, but" defences and rework. If your own organisation is a sugar-sweet or dysfunctional review environment, source the critics outside it.
**APPLY WHEN:** High-value pitches, board presentations, grant and funding presentations, anything you will deliver repeatedly.
**AVOID WHEN:** You are not actually willing to change the material — a screening you ignore burns the reviewers' goodwill.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=high · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=enterprise/investor/stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the firm's process, reinforced by the Covert and Bernstein cases; Conway's Law cited as the rationale.

### [RESO:REHEARSE20] Rehearse across different audience types until the feedback stops
**Move:** Rehearse to varied reviewers deliberately drawn from different backgrounds, collecting feedback and reworking after each pass, and keep going until reviewers say nothing needs changing; afterwards review the recording, identify specific improvements, and iterate again for the next delivery.
**APPLY WHEN:** A single high-stakes presentation with a large discrete payoff; recurring talks you will give many times.
**AVOID WHEN:** The time does not exist — this is an expensive move (Covert ran twenty rehearsals; Bernstein's scripts show up to ten revisions).
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=high · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=investor/stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — two named cases (Markus Covert's NIH Pioneer Award, Leonard Bernstein's Young People's Concerts); the "rehearsal won the grant" claim is the participant's own attribution.

### [RESO:PROPINVEST] Size preparation time to the value at stake
**Move:** Set preparation hours as a function of what the idea or deal is worth, and treat under-preparation as an implicit statement that you value it less; assume the polished performers you benchmark against rehearsed heavily.
**APPLY WHEN:** Deciding rehearsal budget across a pipeline; high-ACV finals presentations; board and investor sessions.
**AVOID WHEN:** Transactional, high-volume motions where per-deal prep cost exceeds margin — there the leverage is a reusable script, not bespoke agonising.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=high · stakeholders=committee · offer=any · market=enterprise/investor
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — drawn from the firm's work across the assembled case set, though presented as exhortation.

### Stage L — After the room: spread and multi-cycle adoption

### [RESO:USABLENOTSMART] Optimise the deliverable for the buyer's retelling
**Move:** Build the findings so the buyer can repeat them to their own stakeholders without you present — plain language, one message per unit, the implication stated rather than left as an exercise. Score yourself on "did they understand it," not on "did we sound rigorous."
**APPLY WHEN:** Competitive evaluations, consulting or agency bake-offs, any deal where the person you present to must resell internally to a committee that never met you.
**AVOID WHEN:** The evaluator is a domain specialist scoring technical depth — oversimplifying reads as thin, and the fix there is layered depth, not removal.
**Deal-gate:** motion=inbound/outbound · acv=high · stakeholders=committee · offer=consulting/service · market=enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — a single dinner-conversation account of a firm that lost a multimillion-dollar engagement despite the client conceding they were the smarter team.

### [RESO:REPEATSPREAD] Build the message to survive the recording
**Move:** Compress the core idea into something short and clear enough to be carried verbatim by listeners, and assume the recording outlives the event — so the pitch must stand on its own for viewers who were never in the room.
**APPLY WHEN:** Launches, keynotes, webinars, recorded demos, category-creation narratives, anything a champion will forward.
**AVOID WHEN:** Confidential or account-specific pitches where circulation is a liability; also weak for deals won on private negotiation rather than narrative spread.
**Deal-gate:** motion=inbound/founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=product/saas/info-product · market=stage
**Evidence:** assertion — supported by view-count examples, with no link established between views and outcomes.

### [RESO:MILESTONESERIES] Plan a series of presentations across the adoption cycle
**Move:** Map the communication milestones in the initiative's life cycle and treat each as a scheduled presentation, using each one to re-align the team and adjust strategy rather than assuming one delivery closes the question. The discussions that produce a presentation are argued to carry as much value as the presentation itself.
**APPLY WHEN:** Long enterprise cycles, internal change programmes, product launches, anything requiring sustained multi-stakeholder buy-in.
**AVOID WHEN:** Transactional single-call sales, where repeated set-piece presentations are pure overhead.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led/expansion/channel · acv=high · stakeholders=committee · offer=product/saas/consulting · market=enterprise/stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — stated as a pattern across the book's featured presenters, plus Gore delivering his climate presentation hundreds of times.

### [RESO:REJECTREPS] Treat early rejection as a stage, not a verdict
**Move:** Keep presenting the idea through the rejection phase, moving it from rejected to considered to adopted, on the expectation that genuinely new ideas are refused first. Cummings self-published after fourteen publishers declined and listed their names inside the book.
**APPLY WHEN:** Category creation, new-budget-line sales, ideas with no existing buying process.
**AVOID WHEN:** The rejections are telling you something true about fit or pricing — the book supplies no criterion for distinguishing persistence from denial, which is this move's main weakness in sales use.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=stage/consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — biographical, and a survivorship-selected example.

### Stage N — Presenter development

### [RESO:CONFESS] Personalise the ask by admitting your own failure at the same change
**Move:** Alongside each "move to," record a short first-person account of when you personally did the opposite and what it cost you, then use it as the credibility bridge when you ask the audience to change.
**APPLY WHEN:** You are asking peers or subordinates for behaviour change and hierarchical authority would produce compliance rather than buy-in.
**AVOID WHEN:** The confession touches the current deal's competence question — a buyer evaluating your delivery capability reads it as risk, not candour.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led/expansion · acv=any · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=service/consulting · market=stage/consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — the book's own "Personalize" entries are the author's self-reports.

### [RESO:FEARREPS] Treat stage fright as reps, not identity
**Move:** Keep presenting through visible failure rather than delegating the presentation away; the model case is a performer who clung to the wall, could not manage her hands and fled to her dressing room, then repeated the attempt until she became formidable.
**APPLY WHEN:** A founder or subject-matter expert avoids customer-facing talks; internal decisions about who should own the pitch.
**AVOID WHEN:** The specific deal is too consequential to be a training rep — then rehearse elsewhere and let the strongest presenter take that room.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — de Mille's biography of Martha Graham.

### [RESO:RULESFIRST] Earn the right to break the form
**Move:** Learn the conventional form to competence before deviating from it, and deviate on purpose and for meaning rather than from ignorance. Cummings trained in traditional forms — sonnets, blues, acrostics — and studied to write more clearly before he began fragmenting words for effect.
**APPLY WHEN:** You want an unconventional pitch format, an anti-deck, or a deliberately provocative narrative in a conservative market.
**AVOID WHEN:** You have not yet demonstrated command of the standard format to this buyer — unearned deviation reads as unprofessional rather than as a signal.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=stage/consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — biography of Cummings.

## Where this book stops working

- **Transactional and high-velocity motions.** Almost every move assumes preparation hours that only pay back above a mid/high ACV. Deep audience research, three-to-four rounds of idea generation, spatial layout, a screening at 3× runtime and up to twenty rehearsals are unrecoverable costs on a small deal. Below that line the leverage is a reusable script, and the book has nothing to say about building one.
- **Two-way conversations where the buyer controls the agenda.** The entire architecture presumes you own the room's time and sequence. On a discovery call the buyer owns both, so the arc collapses: what transfers is one prepared memorable moment, one number given scale, one contrast frame — not a 40-minute structured build. Explicitly flagged as failing here: the fast what-is/what-could-be oscillation, engineered pauses, applause beats, tempo crescendos, and a pre-composed three-part theme structure that stops you following the buyer.
- **Single decision-maker deals.** The four-actor call to action, the sub-audience register shifts and the MECE topic clustering are committee instruments; aimed at one person they dilute the ask.
- **Technical scoring, procurement, security review and bake-off spec comparison.** Emotional layering, human-story framing, dramatised statistics and vivid language all read as evasion when the audience is scoring against a spec sheet or has explicitly asked for data. Credentials sections, which the book wants deleted, are the agenda item in vendor-qualification and security reviews.
- **Regulated and risk-averse buyers.** Deliberate category-clashing framing and unconventional delivery can be penalised by conservative committees; the book's own remedy (be more distinctive) is the wrong direction there.
- **No-gap situations.** With no change genuinely on the table, the architecture has nothing to work on and the book says so: distribute a report instead.
- **Manufactured urgency.** The gap must match real severity. The author's own 2007 internal downturn talk is the documented case of a technically defensible urgency frame read as manipulation by an audience with no experience to validate it — a year to recover.
- **Small rooms and video calls.** Physical props, staged demonstrations, room staging and applause design assume a stage and a crowd; in a meeting room or on a call they feel forced.

## What the book debunks

- **"A slide deck built in PowerPoint is a presentation."** Documents distributed as slides ("slideuments") are reports and should be handed out, not projected; documents are dense on purpose, presentations sparse on purpose, and they are two separate artifacts.
- **"Better-looking slides fix a weak message."** Lipstick on a pig; visual polish is downstream of the message, not a substitute (the author's own earlier book is positioned as the visual layer, this one as the message layer).
- **"Facts persuade on their own."** Fact-after-fact delivery makes the audience decide what matters; unfiltered presentations draw a negative reaction that today's audiences publish to their own followers. Logical agreement is not personal conviction.
- **"So pile on more emotion instead."** The mirror failure: an analytical audience reads missing proof as lost credibility, and emotional overshoot is read directly as manipulation.
- **"Presentations fail from too little information."** They fail from too much. Nobody has ever asked for a longer presentation.
- **"The audience will follow my own associative path."** Subject-matter experts link ideas that are adjacent only in their own heads; an unorganised talk is a private neurotic pathway that leads the audience into dead ends.
- **"The form is a formula."** Rigid adherence makes presentations predictable and self-defeating; Field's single-climax paradigm only partly applies since presentations oscillate rather than peak once, and stages of the Hero's Journey can be reordered because a presentation is not bound by chronology. Following a form does not force formulaic output — no two sonatas sound alike.
- **"Memorable moments are spontaneous."** They are planned and rehearsed; Armstrong spent 6 hours 40 minutes on the lunar surface composing his first-step line. A moment that lands but distracts from the big idea is still a failure, and kitschy execution reads as a bad camp skit.
- **"Great presenters are naturals."** Called false outright; the book's exemplars agonised over wording, structure and delivery. Mastery also does not transfer across formats — Graham was a master communicator in dance and froze badly as a speaker.
- **"Superior analysis wins on its merits."** The anchor case has the smarter consulting team losing a multimillion-dollar engagement because its findings were locked inside dense, smart-sounding slides. Density is not rigour; "brilliance obscured" is the named failure. McClintock's genuine discovery went unrecognised from the 1940s to the 1970s partly on delivery.
- **"Content validity survives a bad first impression."** Aristotle's contrary position is cited and then rejected as unrealistic for audiences that judge within seconds.
- **"Sugar-sweet internal review is review."** Six named dysfunctional environments — Conceited Captain, Political Paranoia, Message Magic, Vacuum Visionary, Lackey Leader, Customer Cold-Shoulder — mean the honest critic often has to come from outside the organisation (Conway's Law: the presentation cannot exceed the process that produced it).
- **"Demographics tell you who the audience is."** Rejected as a basis for connection; so is the imagine-them-in-their-underwear advice. Ownership of any misunderstanding sits with the presenter, not the audience.
- **"Resistance is irrational."** Usually rational from the buyer's seat — reputation risk from spending company money on an unpredictable outcome — though some people do resist for its own sake. Benefit stated only from the presenter's side counts as no benefit. Leaving resistance to Q&A is the named failure.
- **"I communicated it once, so I'm done."** Adoption needs repeated deliveries across the life cycle; Gore had given his climate talk hundreds of times before the film.
- **"Absence of objection means agreement."** Enron routed internal dissenters to other departments; silence in the room measured nothing. A competent presenter who hedges is functionally silent — the audience hears the tone, not the appendix.
- **"Presentation skill is morally neutral in effect."** It is amoral in the strong sense: the same structure that lands an honest idea lands a lie faster, and in regulated selling the transmitted presentation is itself the charged artifact.
- **"Skipping the failure and announcing the successor works."** Failing to acknowledge a prior failure before announcing its replacement makes the audience assume the new one will fail too.
- **"Slides and speaker reinforce each other."** They compete for one processing channel — the audience will read or listen, never both — and overusing slides diminishes the human connection that justified holding the meeting.

## Blind spots of this book

- **Selection on success with no control group.** The presentation form was derived by fitting patterns onto speeches already known to have worked — Jobs, King, Gore, Feynman, Cummings, Graham, Hitchcock. No failed presentations were analysed, so sparkline conformance is a heuristic, never a predictor. The CODA figures in particular validate "break the rules and persist" only through the people it worked for.
- **No stopping rule for persistence.** Nothing distinguishes an idea that failed because it was communicated badly from an idea that failed because it was bad. "Communicate it better" can absorb genuine disconfirming feedback indefinitely, and the rejection-phase advice has no calibration against being wrong. The same gap appears in the transparency keys: no boundary on how much vulnerability is too much, and no test for when conviction has become stubbornness.
- **No deal mechanics at all.** Nothing on discovery questioning, live objection handling in dialogue, negotiation, pricing, qualification, forecasting or pipeline. The one nod to a sales process (the awareness→loyalty ladder) is an unsourced taxonomy used as a targeting device.
- **One-to-many by construction.** Every mechanism assumes a presenter with a stage, an agenda and controlled time. The book never addresses the asymmetry of a buyer-run meeting, and its remote/asynchronous coverage is nil — a 2010 vintage that predates the video call as the default sales room.
- **Effect claims stop at the wrong outcome.** The one real empirical citation measures applause; the reach citations measure views; the rehearsal case attributes a grant win to rehearsal on the participant's own say-so. Nothing in the book measures a purchase, a renewal or a decision.
- **Ethics chapter is a conscience, not a control.** It correctly names that the craft is amoral, but the only safeguard offered is the presenter's own honesty check — no external gate, no test the buyer can apply, no account of what happens when incentives push the other way.
- **Audience-type taxonomies are asserted.** The four actor temperaments, the seven reward categories, the six resistance buckets, the broad-versus-analytical audience profiles and the head/heart/gut/groin registers are all presented without validation; they are useful checklists, not measured constructs.
- **Visual source loss.** The book is heavily visual and several artifacts — sparklines, the Hero's Journey wheel, the presentation-form diagram, the four-actor table — carried their content in figures that did not survive text conversion. Some figure-only detail behind these moves is unrecoverable from the text alone.
