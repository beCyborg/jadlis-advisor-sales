# The Mom Test — digest

## What this book is for the lens

It is the lens's anti-false-positive layer. Where most sales sources optimise how you move a deal, this one optimises whether the deal is real at all — by refusing opinions, hypotheticals and compliments as evidence and accepting only facts about past behaviour plus commitments that cost the counterparty something. Its mechanism is cheap and checkable: people rarely lie about what they already did, and they cannot fake giving up time, reputation or cash.

Two things it does better than the other sources in the lens: (1) it names the exact linguistic tells of worthless data and gives a repair for each in-flight; (2) it converts "the meeting went well" into a binary — a defined next step in the real funnel, or nothing happened. Everything else — segmentation drills, access hacks, note hygiene, team learning distribution — exists to make those two work repeatedly.

## Core model

**The three rules (Ch. 1, "The Mom Test").** Talk about their life, not your idea. Ask about specifics in the past, not generics or the future. Talk less, listen more. A question passing all three survives even a maximally biased respondent.

**Three bad-data types (Ch. 2).** Compliments, fluff (generics, hypotheticals, future tense), ideas/feature requests. Each has a repair: deflect and return to facts; anchor to a specific past event; dig beneath the request to the motivation. Two upstream generators: approval-seeking ("The Pathos Problem") and pitchiness.

**Importance filter (Ch. 3).** A non-biasing question can still be worthless. A question matters only if a surprising answer would change or kill the business. Founders systematically avoid exactly those, so they are planned in advance. Two structural errors sit beside avoidance: the premature zoom (drilling into a problem area before establishing the person cares about the area) and ignoring the elephant (obsessing over the interesting failure point while budget, distribution or legal goes unexamined). Market risk (do they want it, will they pay, are there enough) is testable in conversation; product risk (can I build it, grow it, retain them) is not.

**Casual over formal (Ch. 4).** Steve Blank's problem/solution/sales sequencing is right; the one-hour-meeting format is wrong for early stage. A one-hour meeting costs ~4 hours all-in and sets a demo expectation. Under-formalising is a discipline, not laziness.

**Commitment and advancement (Ch. 5).** Once the product is visible, better questions stop protecting you; commitments do. Commitment = giving up something valued (time, reputation, cash). Advancement = moving to the next real funnel stage. Meetings do not "go well" — they succeed or fail. The failure product is the zombie lead.

**Finding conversations (Ch. 6).** Cold outreach is a bootstrapping device whose goal is to stop being needed; warm intros are the target state. Inverting the flow — hosting, teaching, publishing — converts you from suspicious approacher to credible convener.

**Segmentation and slicing (Ch. 7).** Startups drown in options. A segment is finished only when it names a group *and* a place to find them, and when problems and goals come back consistent across conversations.

**Running the process (Ch. 8).** Prep is bounded (three big questions, target commitment, written guesses, desk research), review is light (walk notes with the team, update beliefs and the next three questions, debrief the craft), attendance is two people, notes are symbol-tagged and permanently retrievable. Learning cannot be outsourced before a working business model exists.

**The loop.** Pick a findable segment → set three learning goals and the target commitment → check conversations are the right instrument → talk (casual, Mom-Test-passing, deflect/anchor/dig, notes, push for commitment) → review with the team, transfer notes, update beliefs, reset the three questions. Timescale: ~1 hour of prep, 1–2 weeks of concentrated conversation, then learning alongside building.

## Moves

### Choosing whom to talk to — segmentation and stakeholders

### [FOUND:SEGSPEC] Narrow to one buyer before serving all
**Move:** Pick the single sub-group most likely to buy today and commit to it; drop features and messaging aimed at the others until that group is served. Broaden back out later, deliberately.
**APPLY WHEN:** Feedback across discovery calls contradicts itself; every feature is somebody's favourite; you cannot cut anything without an uproar.
**AVOID WHEN:** You already have consistent problem/goal signal — further slicing just shrinks reachable volume; also not a substitute for a real ICP once revenue data exists.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=product/saas · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — author's own advertising startup, where "advertisers" as a segment made every experiment un-falsifiable until they narrowed to edgy creative agencies.

### [FOUND:SEGMIX] Mixed-feedback diagnostic
**Move:** When interviews produce inconsistent must-have features and must-solve problems, treat that as a segmentation defect rather than a research defect — stop iterating on question quality and re-cut the segment.
**APPLY WHEN:** Question technique is already good (Mom-Test-passing, pushing for commitment) yet the picture stays incoherent.
**AVOID WHEN:** The inconsistency traces to talking to non-buyers or wrong stakeholders — fix who you are talking to first.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=product/saas · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — recurring pattern across founders the author coached; explicitly diagnosed in a "sales organisations" case.

### [FOUND:TAXEXP] Borrow an industry taxonomy from an expert
**Move:** When you cannot tell which sub-segments exist, spend a conversation with an industry insider purely to get the category map of the industry, then choose a starting cell from that map.
**APPLY WHEN:** Entering an unfamiliar vertical; the segment names you use are outsider labels.
**AVOID WHEN:** No such access exists — then guess and slice; also do not let the expert's taxonomy substitute for talking to actual buyers.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=product/saas · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** assertion — recommended without a supporting case.

### [FOUND:SLICEQ] Customer-slicing question ladder
**Move:** Run the fixed sequence over a segment — who inside wants it most, does everyone or only some buy, what problem or goal drives them, does everyone share that motivation, what other motivations exist, who else has those motivations — then repeat on whichever outputs are still generic.
**APPLY WHEN:** Pre-product or pre-PMF, choosing whom to interview next; also when re-cutting a segment after mixed feedback.
**AVOID WHEN:** It becomes an all-day theorising exercise — the book explicitly warns against planning instead of contacting people.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=product/saas · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the author's standard workshop drill, illustrated with a public-speaking-app worked example.

### [FOUND:WHOWHERE] Who-where pairs as the segment definition
**Move:** Refuse to call a segment finished until each entry names both a group and a concrete place to find them (admissions offices, department advisors, Amazon's upcoming-release list plus Twitter outreach, Toastmasters meetups). Keep slicing until the "where" is answerable.
**APPLY WHEN:** Planning outreach for discovery; when your outreach plan is "walk around and see who I meet".
**AVOID WHEN:** Findability is being optimised at the expense of buying power — an easy-to-reach group with no budget still fails the profitability criterion.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led/outbound · acv=any · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=product/saas · market=transactional/consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — stated as a rule of thumb and demonstrated across several segment examples.

### [FOUND:WORKARND] Target existing coping behaviour, not just demographics
**Move:** List what the group already does to survive the problem (googling tips, buying books, therapy, workshops, on-the-job training) and reach them at those behaviours — buy the search terms they use, sponsor the podcast they listen to, propose a live call-in segment, show up at the meetup.
**APPLY WHEN:** The demographic exists but is diffuse; you need warm, self-selected conversation volume.
**AVOID WHEN:** People who ignore the problem entirely are in scope — non-actors are probably not customers, so behaviour-based targeting deliberately excludes a slice of the market.
**Deal-gate:** motion=inbound/founder-led · acv=micro/low · stakeholders=solo · offer=product/saas/info-product · market=transactional
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — author's own choice to start at a Toastmasters meetup for the speaking-confidence example.

### [FOUND:SEG3CRIT] Three-criteria pick among candidate segments
**Move:** Rank surviving who-where pairs on profitable, easy to reach, and personally rewarding to serve, and start with the top one; treat the "rewarding" criterion as real, since discovery is a grind if you dislike the industry.
**APPLY WHEN:** Multiple viable slices and no data to separate them.
**AVOID WHEN:** Revenue data already exists — let actual buying behaviour choose instead of preference.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=product/saas · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the author's standing selection heuristic.

### [FOUND:SHELFTEST] Commitment-shaped segment test with a channel partner
**Move:** Instead of asking a channel partner for opinions, ask for a small concrete resource (shelf space for a few units) inside the narrowest viable slice of that channel, then return in a week to debrief what actually happened, and use the outcome to open access to end customers.
**APPLY WHEN:** Physical or channel-mediated products where the partner is a gatekeeper; early validation with no product data.
**AVOID WHEN:** Software or direct-sale motions where no gatekeeper exists; also when the partner's incentives differ enough that a yes means nothing.
**Deal-gate:** motion=channel/founder-led · acv=micro/low · stakeholders=few · offer=product · market=transactional
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — the superfood-condiment founder, single case.

### [FOUND:STAKEMAP] Enumerate every affected party before interviewing
**Move:** Before a discovery round, write out all sides of the transaction — end user, payer, approver, blocker, and any dependency partner — and check each has been talked to; treat an unmapped party as a known gap rather than an unknown.
**APPLY WHEN:** B2B with a real buying process, marketplaces, products bought by one party and used by another, or any partner-dependent business model.
**AVOID WHEN:** Genuinely single-actor consumer purchase — mapping adds no information.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led/outbound · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=saas/service/product · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — presented as a recurring failure category with several structural examples (kids' apps, school products, marketplaces).

### [FOUND:USERNOTVIP] Interview the representative user, not the impressive title
**Move:** Deliberately allocate discovery time to the actual end user even when that population is unglamorous, and resist substituting senior contacts because they read better in reporting.
**APPLY WHEN:** The buyer and the user differ; when your interview log is dominated by executives.
**AVOID WHEN:** The purchase decision genuinely sits with the senior party and usage is irrelevant — then user interviews inform product but not the deal.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=product/saas · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — author's interactive-advertising product, where he interviewed many suited buyers and zero of the teenagers who were the assumed users.

### Getting the conversation — access and lead multiplication

### [FOUND:COLDSTART] Bootstrap with cold outreach, ignore the rejection rate
**Move:** Hustle the first one or two conversations from any channel available (cold calls, cold LinkedIn messages to senior people), treat mass non-response as meaningless, and convert each accepted conversation into intros by being respectful of their time and genuinely useful.
**APPLY WHEN:** No network in the target industry; starting from zero.
**AVOID WHEN:** Judging demand by cold-response rate — it measures channel tolerance, not problem severity.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/founder-led · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — a team reaching C-level execs at major UK retailers via cold LinkedIn messages after near-universal silence.

### [FOUND:GOODEXCUSE] Manufacture a legitimate excuse to talk
**Move:** Find a pretext that is true and flattering to them — praising the product and asking about its backstory, or academic research if a PhD student is on the team — and use it to reach staff, then the manager, then the owner. Never mention your idea.
**APPLY WHEN:** Local/SMB segments where owners are physically reachable and gatekeepers refuse abstract requests.
**AVOID WHEN:** The pretext is false — the technique depends on genuinely caring about the topic.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=micro/low · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=product/service · market=transactional
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — cafe founder stalled for two weeks; a single flagged-down waitress produced the manager and the owner's contact details.

### [FOUND:IMMERSE] Immerse yourself where the segment lives
**Move:** Enter the community as a participant rather than a researcher — speak at their events, do favours for organisers, be permanently present — until connections and conversations arrive without asking.
**APPLY WHEN:** High-status segments you cannot reach cold; long-horizon markets.
**AVOID WHEN:** Costly in months; and immersion does not guarantee the segment is worth serving — the author immersed in the speaking circuit and then abandoned the segment.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=any · market=consultative/stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — conference-circuit immersion targeting speakers charging $5–50k per talk.

### [FOUND:HOSTMEETUP] Organise the event instead of attending it
**Move:** Convene your target segment under a plain banner (e.g. a professionals' happy hour). The act of sending the invitations confers assumed credibility and makes problem conversations easy; it also bootstraps industry standing.
**APPLY WHEN:** New industry or new geography; when you need many conversations fast.
**AVOID WHEN:** Nothing contraindicated in the text — the author notes only that almost nobody actually does it.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** assertion — called the most unfair trick the author knows, with no case study attached.

### [FOUND:TEACH] Teach as a learning and selling channel
**Move:** Convert expertise plus a strong opinion into teaching — conference talks, workshops, videos, blogging, free consulting or office hours — then talk to the keenest attendees. Use it to test which parts of the message resonate before building.
**APPLY WHEN:** You have domain expertise and an opinionated view of how the work should be done.
**AVOID WHEN:** No genuine expertise yet — the combination is what makes it work.
**Deal-gate:** motion=inbound/founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=saas/service/consulting/info-product · market=any
**Evidence:** assertion — presented as an under-valued channel without a specific case.

### [FOUND:BLOGCRED] Blog for credibility, not for traffic
**Move:** Publish about the industry and send cold emails from that domain, so recipients can check you out and self-qualify you as interesting. If you already have an audience, simply post the request. Treat the writing itself as rehearsal for better conversations.
**APPLY WHEN:** Cold outreach with no other credibility signal.
**AVOID WHEN:** Expecting audience size to matter — the author reports the effect worked with no traffic at all.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/inbound · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — meetings granted because recipients checked the author's industry blog.

### [FOUND:CONVENEELITE] Host a peer knowledge exchange to borrow status
**Move:** Organise a recurring call between the recognised leaders of your target market on a topic you need to understand, open for others to dial in and listen. Playing host absorbs their credibility and yields direct access to decision makers.
**APPLY WHEN:** Selling into prestigious, hard-to-reach institutions where both the problem and the access are opaque.
**AVOID WHEN:** Copying it verbatim — the book insists on adapting the mechanism to your own situation.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=high · stakeholders=committee · offer=saas/service/consulting · market=enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — second-hand account of a founder targeting top universities.

### [FOUND:ASKTHEROOM] Ask the nearest room for the introduction
**Move:** State the specific person-type you need out loud to any group you are in, offer something small in return, and take the intros that surface. Assume any target is a couple of hops away.
**APPLY WHEN:** You need a hard-to-reach profession, or (easier) a common consumer type.
**AVOID WHEN:** Nothing flagged; the constraint is remembering to ask.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — three McKinsey intros obtained in a coworking space in exchange for three beers.

### [FOUND:ADVISORENGINE] Use advisors, professors and investors as an intro engine
**Move:** Recruit a small advisory board with modest equity whose main function is credible introductions, and meet each monthly so the intro flow is weekly without burdening anyone. Add professors (reachable, connected to funders who like new projects) and top-tier investors (portfolio plus cold intros, and leverage to recruit better advisors). Recruit advisors out of your best early customer conversations.
**APPLY WHEN:** Unknown founder entering an industry with high walls; B2B especially.
**AVOID WHEN:** Equity-for-intros is inappropriate — the mechanism assumes an equity-bearing venture.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=product/saas/service · market=enterprise/investor
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — 5 advisors at roughly half a percent equity each in the author's first company.

### [FOUND:CASHFAVOURS] Call in the empty offers of help
**Move:** Go back through people who said "let me know how I can help", reply to the old thread, and ask for the specific intro, packaging it so it costs them little. Accept a high ignore rate.
**APPLY WHEN:** Backed into a corner with no other route to conversations.
**AVOID WHEN:** As a habit — the author flags it as slightly annoying and capable of burning bridges.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — offered with an explicit caution about repeated use.

### [FOUND:WHOELSE] Close every conversation with a referral ask
**Move:** End each conversation by asking who else you should talk to. Treat refusal as data: either the meeting was mishandled (too formal, too pitchy, too clingy) or they do not care about the problem — and discount their praise accordingly.
**APPLY WHEN:** Every early conversation, warm or cold.
**AVOID WHEN:** Pressing after a soft no — let it go, take the negative signal instead.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the book's stated primary lead-multiplication mechanism.

### Framing and format of the conversation

### [FOUND:VFWPA] Vision / Framing / Weakness / Pedestal / Ask
**Move:** Request the meeting in five beats: state the problem or vision you are attacking without naming your idea; frame the stage you are at and, if true, that you have nothing to sell; admit the specific gap in your understanding; show why this person in particular can fill it; ask for the time. Order and compression are flexible — move the weakness earlier if the opening reads as sales spam. Mnemonic: "Very Few Wizards Properly Ask".
**APPLY WHEN:** Warm intros, or when a real meeting is unavoidable (full hour needed, senior person outside your network).
**AVOID WHEN:** Cold approaches — the author explicitly scopes this to warm intros and calls cold a different, riskier game.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led/outbound · acv=any · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise/investor
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — two of the author's own emails reproduced with the beats annotated.

### [FOUND:REINS] Take the reins in the first minute
**Move:** Open the meeting by restating the email framing, cite the introducer as authority if there was one, deliver the pedestal line, and move straight into your first good question about something specific you researched. Own the agenda and propose the next steps without being aggressive.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any scheduled meeting, especially where the counterpart may start interrogating you about your idea.
**AVOID WHEN:** Nothing flagged, beyond not being a jerk about control.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led/outbound · acv=any · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise/investor
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — annotated opening script from the author's practice.

### [FOUND:CASUALCHAT] Replace the meeting with a conversation
**Move:** When you encounter the right person, drop straight into your most important question instead of proposing a meeting. Do not announce that you are starting a business or that this is research; just be interested in their day, their annoyances and how they handle them.
**APPLY WHEN:** Industry events, meetups, parties, queues, anywhere your segment congregates; early problem-stage learning.
**AVOID WHEN:** You need a full hour with a senior person, or you have a product to demo — then ask for a real meeting and frame it properly.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=micro/low/mid · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=any · market=transactional/consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — office-manager example where the real problem turned out to be debt collection rather than efficiency, learned over beers without any meeting.

### [FOUND:MINIMALINFO] Reveal the minimum that still steers
**Move:** Give as little about your idea as possible while nudging the conversation toward the area you need. Prefer leading with an observation about their world over an explanation of your product.
**APPLY WHEN:** Problem-stage learning, including inside meetings that were formally scheduled.
**AVOID WHEN:** Commitment stage — at that point the idea must be on the table.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — investor-dealflow idea disproved in 5 minutes of smalltalk (a dozen sticky notes on the wall) before the counterpart realised a product was being discussed.

### [FOUND:MEETINGBLOCK] Budget the sales-stage meeting explicitly
**Move:** Once you have a product and meetings turn sales-oriented, block 30+ minutes and allocate it: ~5 lost to lateness, ~5 to hello, ~5 to goals/problems/budget questions, ~10 to showing the product, ~5 to next steps and advancement.
**APPLY WHEN:** Product exists; scheduled B2B meetings.
**AVOID WHEN:** Problem-stage learning — that fits in 5–15 minutes and does not justify a calendar block.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led/outbound · acv=low/mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=saas/service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — stated as the author's working structure.

### [FOUND:INPERSON] Start in person, not on a call
**Move:** Default to meeting face to face for early conversations; treat calls as a considered trade-off rather than an escape from awkwardness. Choose the medium consciously, knowing calls compress into scripted interviews and do not build the relationships that produce intros.
**APPLY WHEN:** Early learning conversations where volume is not yet the bottleneck.
**AVOID WHEN:** The book concedes others in the field successfully favour calls; treat this as author preference, not a law.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=any · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** assertion — reasoned preference, explicitly contradicted by other practitioners the author names as credible.

### [FOUND:ADVISORFLIP] Go looking for advisors, not customers
**Move:** Reframe the meeting internally as evaluating whether this person would make a good industry advisor. You need not say so out loud. The switch removes neediness, gives you a clear purpose and puts you in control because you are now assessing them.
**APPLY WHEN:** Pre-product conversations where the temptation to sell keeps leaking in.
**AVOID WHEN:** Real sales meetings with a product to move — the flip would then be avoidance.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** assertion — argued from a willpower-as-finite-resource premise, cited without a named source.

### Preparing the conversation and distributing the learning

### [FOUND:LISTOF3] The three questions, per person type and per batch
**Move:** Before any conversation or batch of conversations, agree with the team on the three things you most need to learn — from the business as a whole and from that specific type of person — chosen for murkiness or importance rather than for being answerable, deliberately including the frightening ones. Keep separate lists per customer or partner type, do not re-ask what you already have reliable answers to, accumulate answers across people, and refresh the list after each batch.
**APPLY WHEN:** Always, including opportunistic encounters — the list is what lets you skip the coffee-exchange ritual and ask something real on the spot; especially when meetings feel productive but the business is not moving.
**AVOID WHEN:** Turning the list into a fixed script read to everyone; also when conversations are simply not the right tool for the question at hand — the book allows skipping interviews entirely.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — standing practice in both the question-selection and process chapters; explicitly linked to making casual conversations work and to the rule that a conversation without a learning goal should not happen.

### [FOUND:PREMORTQ] Two risk-surfacing prep questions for the team
**Move:** Ask the team two questions and take the gut answer: if this company failed, what most likely killed it, and what would have to be true for it to be a huge success. Use the answers to pick which risks the next conversations attack.
**APPLY WHEN:** A cofounder resists doing customer conversations at all; when prep keeps landing on comfortable questions.
**AVOID WHEN:** Turned into a long strategy session — gut-level rigour is stated as sufficient.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=product/saas · market=stage/any
**Evidence:** assertion — the second question is credited to Lafley/Martin's strategy work, but no study or dataset backs the pair; the illustration is the author's own cartoon-community startup.

### [FOUND:PREPGUESS] Written pre-meeting belief skeleton
**Move:** Spend up to an hour writing your best guesses about what this specific person cares about and wants, then run the conversation as an update to those beliefs rather than a blank-slate exploration.
**APPLY WHEN:** Unfamiliar buyer type; conversations that tend to wander.
**AVOID WHEN:** The segment is tight and familiar — the skeleton is then rarely needed; and never let prep expand toward a week, which the book labels stalling.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=product/saas · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the author's routine, with an explicit time cap.

### [FOUND:DESKFIRST] Answer by desk research before asking a human
**Move:** During prep, route any question the internet can answer to desk research, and do a five-minute LinkedIn and company-site check before a business meeting; save live time for what cannot be looked up.
**APPLY WHEN:** Every scheduled business conversation.
**AVOID WHEN:** Research becomes a substitute for the meeting itself.
**Deal-gate:** motion=outbound/founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=saas/service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — stated as standing practice with a five-minute cost estimate.

### [FOUND:TEAMPREP] Whole-founding-team prep and post-meeting review
**Move:** Prep with business and product both in the room so each side's concerns enter the conversation; afterward walk the notes and key quotes with the team, update beliefs and plans, and debrief the meta-level — which questions worked, which signals were missed.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any multi-founder team; whenever one person is doing all customer contact.
**AVOID WHEN:** Solo founder — then substitute writing notes into shared permanent storage and reviewing them yourself.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=product/saas · market=stage/any
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — author reports bottlenecking hard enough that his CTO quit over constant direction changes.

### [FOUND:TWOSEAT] Two people per meeting, split roles
**Move:** Bring exactly two people: one leads the conversation, one takes notes and intervenes when the lead asks a bad question or misses a thread worth digging into. If shy and without a cofounder, borrow a friend and take the note-taker seat for the first few.
**APPLY WHEN:** Early discovery meetings, especially while interviewing skill is still forming.
**AVOID WHEN:** Group-on-group meetings, or when the interviewee would find a crowd intimidating; three interviewers is named as too many.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=product/saas · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the author's standing meeting configuration.

### Question craft inside the conversation

### [FOUND:MOMTEST3] The three rules
**Move:** Run every question through three filters before asking — is it about their life rather than my idea, is it about a specific past event rather than a generic or a hypothetical, and am I listening more than talking. If a question fails, rewrite it toward their existing behaviour.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any pre-product or pre-commitment conversation; whenever you catch yourself hoping for a particular answer.
**AVOID WHEN:** You already have a product and need a purchase decision — then commitments, not questions, are the instrument. Also weak with deep industry experts, whose informed opinion has real content.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=product/saas · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — author's own failed company plus repeated work with founders; no controlled comparison.

### [FOUND:LASTTIME] Anchor to the last concrete instance
**Move:** Replace "what do you usually do" with "what was the last thing you did" / "when did that last happen" / "talk me through it". When someone answers generically, ask for the most recent actual example and walk through it step by step.
**APPLY WHEN:** Early discovery; any answer containing "usually", "always", "never".
**AVOID WHEN:** The behaviour genuinely has no precedent (brand-new category) — then you are testing product risk, not market risk.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — presented as the book's central operating rule, illustrated by many dialogues.

### [FOUND:WHYBOTHER] "Why do you bother?"
**Move:** When someone describes a task or a requested feature, ask why they do it at all. Follow the chain until you hit the underlying goal, then design against the goal rather than the stated task.
**APPLY WHEN:** The customer states a perceived problem or a workaround they treat as normal.
**AVOID WHEN:** Repeated too often it reads as interrogation; drop it once you already know their goal.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=product/saas/service · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — finance team asking for messaging tools whose real need was version certainty (a Dropbox-shaped need).

### [FOUND:IMPLICATIONS] "What are the implications?"
**Move:** After a stated problem, ask what actually happens because of it — cost, delay, headcount, lost revenue. Use the answer to separate pay-to-fix problems from tolerable annoyances, and as a first pricing signal.
**APPLY WHEN:** Someone describes a problem with heat and emotion; before you decide to build anything against it.
**AVOID WHEN:** Never skip it because the emotion looked convincing — loud framing frequently sits on top of a working workaround.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — respondent who called a workflow a disaster, then revealed interns had made it fine.

### [FOUND:WALKTHRU] Be shown the workflow, not told about it
**Move:** Ask to watch the task, or failing that ask them to narrate the last occurrence end to end: tools used, people involved, constraints of the day, hand-offs, integrations. Prefer observed behaviour over stated preference.
**APPLY WHEN:** You need to know where the product must fit and what it must integrate with; B2B discovery especially.
**AVOID WHEN:** You need the *why* behind a preference — observation shows what, not why; that still requires questions.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=product/saas/service · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — repeated claim that observation reveals inefficiencies the customer cannot self-report.

### [FOUND:WHATELSE] "What else have you tried?" (the search test)
**Move:** After any enthusiastic problem statement, ask what they have already tried, whether they searched for a solution, and what it cost them. If they never even searched, treat the enthusiasm as noise.
**APPLY WHEN:** You hear "I would definitely pay for that" or similar future-tense enthusiasm.
**AVOID WHEN:** The category is genuinely unsearchable or unknown to them — then absence of search is weaker evidence.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=transactional/consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — respondent who "definitely" would pay but had never googled; also the untied-shoelaces double-knot argument.

### [FOUND:CURRENTSPEND] "How are you dealing with it now?" as a price anchor
**Move:** Ask what the current solution is and what it costs per month or year. Use the spread between cheap workaround and expensive incumbent to choose which one you position against — replacing a £100/month tool and replacing a £120k/year agency are different businesses.
**APPLY WHEN:** You need pricing orientation and a positioning decision; mid/late discovery.
**AVOID WHEN:** Treating the number as their willingness to pay for you — it bounds the ballpark, not the deal.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=low/mid/high · stakeholders=any · offer=saas/service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — presented as standard practice with worked numbers.

### [FOUND:MONEYPATH] "Where does the money come from?"
**Move:** In B2B, ask whose budget the purchase would come from and who else can kill it. Accumulate these answers across conversations into a repeatable map of the buying process.
**APPLY WHEN:** B2B discovery, especially when your contact is clearly not the budget owner.
**AVOID WHEN:** Consumer purchases where the buyer is the user (still sometimes useful, e.g. household decisions).
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led/outbound · acv=mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=saas/service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — framed as a must-ask in B2B; no dataset.

### [FOUND:WHATSHOULDIASK] "Is there anything else I should have asked?"
**Move:** Near the end, invite the other person to repair your line of questioning. Treat it as a temporary crutch to be dropped as your industry knowledge grows.
**APPLY WHEN:** You are new to the industry and suspect you are missing the central issue.
**AVOID WHEN:** You already know the domain — it then signals unpreparedness.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** assertion — stated as reliably effective, no supporting case given.

### [FOUND:DREAMSET] The dream-product question as a set-up only
**Move:** "What would your dream product do?" is acceptable only as a springboard: immediately follow with why they want each element, what constraint it removes, and what they do today instead. Never collect the answer as a feature list.
**APPLY WHEN:** Conversation has stalled and you need to surface motivations.
**AVOID WHEN:** You have no time or intention to run the follow-ups — alone it produces build-by-committee noise.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=product/saas · market=any
**Evidence:** assertion — classified as "sort-of-okay" by the author's judgement.

### [FOUND:SCARYQ] Plant one terrifying question per conversation
**Move:** Before each conversation, find the question whose answer could disprove the business, and ask it. If avoidance persists, ask what you would instruct a delegate to do, then do that.
**APPLY WHEN:** Every conversation, at any stage.
**AVOID WHEN:** Never legitimately skipped; but scale it to the relationship — a scary question can end a fragile first contact early (which is itself information).
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — unasked legal/content-ownership question the author estimates cost at least half a million dollars.

### [FOUND:BROADFIRST] Start broad, zoom only on a volunteered signal
**Move:** Open with the person's actual priorities (big goals, biggest annoyances, costs, what they are trying to fix right now) rather than with your problem area. Zoom in only after they raise the topic themselves. Zoom immediately only when you are certain the problem is a must-solve for that segment.
**APPLY WHEN:** New segment, new geography, or any segment whose economics differ from your proven one (e.g. hobbyists vs businesses).
**AVOID WHEN:** Established must-solve categories for that buyer (e.g. lead generation for small businesses) — starting broad there wastes the meeting.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — gym/fitness and blog-marketing dialogues showing manufactured "top problems".

### [FOUND:MATTERQ] Does-this-problem-matter battery
**Move:** When it is unclear whether you are selling a painkiller or a vitamin, probe seriousness rather than pain: do they make money from it, have they tried to make more, how much time per week, what tools and services do they already pay for, what are the three things they are trying to fix right now, what are they already doing about this.
**APPLY WHEN:** Ambiguous segments; hobby-vs-business boundary cases; before committing roadmap.
**AVOID WHEN:** Used as a survey — these are anchors to dig around, not a scored questionnaire.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the author's own blog used as the worked negative example.

### [FOUND:FAILPOINTS] Enumerate every failure point, including the boring one
**Move:** List all conditions that must simultaneously hold (need exists, buyer has budget, you can reach them, you can build it, you can grow supply/demand), and deliberately test the least interesting ones. Classify the business as market-risk or product-risk and adjust how much conversations can prove.
**APPLY WHEN:** Before investing weeks in discovery; whenever a customer's enthusiasm is conditional on your capability ("if you can get me X, I'll pay").
**AVOID WHEN:** Treating conditional enthusiasm as validation — "if you build it I'll buy" is near-tautological in ad, affiliate, marketplace and game businesses.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=product/saas/service · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — poor-schools budget example, farm-fertility tracker founder who lost ~3 months, nightlife-deals startups, video games as pure product risk.

### Repairing bad data in flight

### [FOUND:DEFLECT] Deflect the compliment, return to facts
**Move:** When praised, do not thank and bank it. Acknowledge briefly, name your own slip if you pitched, and immediately redirect: ask how they currently handle the thing, then follow their workflow. Watch for compliments hidden at the end of otherwise factual answers.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any meeting that is drifting toward warmth without facts.
**AVOID WHEN:** The praise comes with a commitment attached — then it is backed by currency and is worth more.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — multiple worked dialogues including a good negative outcome.

### [FOUND:ANCHORFLUFF] Anchor generic and future-tense claims
**Move:** On hearing "I always / I never / I would / I might", convert it to a specific: what is the state right now, when did it last break down, walk me through that. Expect the anchored version to contradict the self-image version.
**APPLY WHEN:** Anywhere; especially after a fluff-inducing question you asked yourself.
**AVOID WHEN:** Never treat the fluffy answer itself as data — the error is valuing the answer, not asking the question.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — inbox-zero and loyalty-card dialogues; plus a $10M loss attributed to trusting "I would definitely buy that".

### [FOUND:ACTNOW] Put the flake to an immediate small decision
**Move:** When someone claims a problem matters, invite them to act on it right now (download it, try it, look it up). Deferral to "next time" classifies them as a complainer rather than a customer.
**APPLY WHEN:** Consumer or low-friction contexts where acting takes seconds.
**AVOID WHEN:** Enterprise contexts where immediate action is impossible for procedural reasons — non-action there is not evidence.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led/inbound · acv=micro/low · stakeholders=solo · offer=product/saas/info-product · market=transactional
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — loyalty-app conversation in a queue.

### [FOUND:DIGREQUEST] Dig beneath a feature request
**Move:** Never log a requested feature and move on. Ask what it would let them do, how they cope without it, whether it should delay launch, and how it fits their day. Build against the motivation, which is usually cheaper than the request.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any feature request, from prospects or from paying enterprise customers.
**AVOID WHEN:** Obeying the request instead of understanding it — this is the documented failure.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led/expansion · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=product/saas · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — MTV asked for analytics; the real need was keeping their own clients happy with a weekly emailed report. ~3 months of work wasted, ~90% of the built dashboard irrelevant (self-reported).

### [FOUND:DIGEMOTION] Dig into any strong emotion
**Move:** Treat anger, embarrassment, excitement or resignation as a flag and stop to explore it: tell me more, what makes it so bad, why hasn't this been fixed, go on. Give permission for a brain dump rather than moving to the next scripted question.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any emotional spike, positive or negative.
**AVOID WHEN:** Emotion you induced yourself by leading them there — that is manufactured, not observed.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — the overheard founder who wrote down "worst part of my day" and moved on.

### [FOUND:PITCHABORT] Abort your own pitch
**Move:** The moment you notice a monologue, stop, apologise for slipping into pitch mode, and hand the floor back with a callback to what they were saying. If they insist on hearing about the product, promise it for the end of the meeting or an early demo, explaining you do not want to bias them yet.
**APPLY WHEN:** Learning-oriented meetings; you have been talking for minutes and they are nodding.
**AVOID WHEN:** An actual sales meeting where the product presentation is the point.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — a scripted recovery line the author reports using repeatedly.

### [FOUND:SHUTUP] Do not correct their mental model mid-answer
**Move:** When they start "it's similar to..." or raise an objection you have already solved, do not interrupt to fix them. Let the sentence finish — the correction costs you the view into their model and is annoying besides.
**APPLY WHEN:** After your idea has entered the conversation deliberately or accidentally.
**AVOID WHEN:** A factual misunderstanding is about to derail a live commitment decision late in a sales meeting.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** assertion — stated as a rule with reasoning, no case.

### [FOUND:LOVEMEH] Treat lukewarm as reliable, and stop selling into it
**Move:** Read "I'm not so sure" and "that's pretty neat" as clean negative data and accept it. Do not escalate the pitch until they say something nice. If they are still engaged, ask a few follow-ups to classify the apathy (problem too small, wrong segment, wrong implementation, pitch fatigue, bad day), then thank them and leave.
**APPLY WHEN:** Learning-stage conversations, before you depend on this deal for revenue.
**AVOID WHEN:** Late-stage transactional selling where the goal genuinely is the close, not the learning — the book flags this asymmetry explicitly.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — presented as a repeatedly-earned lesson, no dataset.

### Commitment and advancement

### [FOUND:CURRENCY] Price the commitment in currency terms
**Move:** Before asking, decide which currency you want and at what size. Time: a next meeting with defined goals, a wireframe feedback session, a non-trivial self-run trial. Reputation: intro to peers or the team, intro to a decision maker (boss, spouse, lawyer), a public testimonial or case study. Cash: letter of intent, pre-order, deposit. Weigh their answer by what it cost them; combined-currency commitments (paid team trial) are the strongest.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any meeting where a product, prototype or concrete offer has been shown.
**AVOID WHEN:** Pure problem-stage learning where you have nothing to advance toward.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led/outbound · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=product/saas/service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise/investor
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the book's central late-stage framework; presented as adapted from sales practice.

### [FOUND:FIXMEETING] Rescue a wishy-washy meeting by forcing a decision
**Move:** When you are being brushed off with praise, ask for a concrete commitment available today instead of accepting the stall — an intro to the boss/tech team/budget holder if early, or alpha-user status plus a team rollout and launch case study if further along. Aim for a clear yes or a clear no, without strong-arming.
**APPLY WHEN:** The meeting is ending in no-man's-land, neither rejection nor next step.
**AVOID WHEN:** It tips into pressure — the goal is a decision, not a conversion; an unexcited no is a good outcome.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led/outbound · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=product/saas/service · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — worked through eight meeting-ending phrases classified good/bad.

### [FOUND:CONCRETIZE] Convert a fuzzy promise into a specific one
**Move:** When offered generic future help ("I can introduce you to some people when you're ready"), ask who specifically, what "ready" means, and why it cannot happen now. Use the answer both to test sincerity and to define your own short-term milestone.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any offer of future help, intros, or "let me know when it launches".
**AVOID WHEN:** Pressing as if entitled to their network — the aim is distinguishing a real offer from politeness.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — from the good-meeting/bad-meeting analysis.

### [FOUND:TRIALCOST] Raise the price of a "free" trial
**Move:** If trying your product is nearly costless (log in, look around, forget), add cost so the trial carries signal: a promised case study after two weeks, a commitment to use it with the team for at least a week, or a card on file with a 30-day cancel window.
**APPLY WHEN:** Trials, pilots, freemium onboarding used as evidence of interest.
**AVOID WHEN:** Setup genuinely is expensive for the buyer (new software plus staff training) — the trial already carries cost.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led/inbound · acv=low/mid · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=saas · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — the author's own history of trying and abandoning many CRM tools.

### [FOUND:NOBLINDPITCH] Open even a sales meeting with learning questions
**Move:** Before presenting, spend a few minutes on open-ended questions about this specific buyer's goals, problems and budget. Then sell. Hard pitching first yields only binary feedback, useful for tweaks and useless for the big questions.
**APPLY WHEN:** Product-stage sales meetings, especially early ones where you are still learning the segment.
**AVOID WHEN:** Late-cycle meetings where the buyer expects a decision, not discovery.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led/outbound · acv=low/mid/high · stakeholders=few/committee · offer=saas/service/consulting · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** assertion — claimed to increase close rate; no supporting case or data.

### [FOUND:EARLYVANGELIST] Hunt the emotionally over-committed first buyer
**Move:** Filter for people who are disproportionately upset or excited, not merely agreeable. In B2B, look for the four markers (has the problem, knows they have it, has budget, has already built a makeshift solution). Keep those people close; do not expect the calm agreeable ones to write the first cheque, but keep them on the list.
**APPLY WHEN:** Choosing who to build with and who to sell to first.
**AVOID WHEN:** Generalising from them to the mainstream market — their willingness is by definition irrational-early.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=product/saas · market=consultative/enterprise
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — attributed to Steve Blank's earlyvangelist concept plus the author's own experience; smartphone-tripod designer who had sold about a dozen prototypes.

### [FOUND:ZOMBIE] Commitment-absence signals
**Move:** Treat these as evidence you are not pushing for commitment: a pipeline of zombie leads, product meetings that end in a compliment, meetings with no clear next step, meetings that merely "went well", and counterparties who have surrendered nothing of value.
**APPLY WHEN:** Reviewing pipeline health or a call where the outcome felt positive but nothing changed.
**AVOID WHEN:** Genuinely early exploratory conversations where no ask is yet appropriate — the process marks the commitment step as conditional.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led/outbound · acv=low/mid · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=product/saas/service · market=transactional/consultative
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — cheatsheet formalisation of the book's advancement material.

### [FOUND:CUTKNOT] Skip the process when a direct ask is available
**Move:** Before designing a validation plan, check whether you can simply contact the prospective customer directly and ask for the real thing; a phone call that books a trial beats a research design that estimates market size.
**APPLY WHEN:** The customer is trivially reachable and the ask is small; when the team has slipped into methodology discussion.
**AVOID WHEN:** The ask is large, the buying process is complex, or a single yes would not be informative — one booked trial is one data point, not validation.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led/outbound · acv=micro/low · stakeholders=solo · offer=service/consulting · market=transactional
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — the personal trainer at a segmentation workshop who called the police station and returned with a trial session booked in about twenty minutes.

### Capture, review and self-audit

### [FOUND:SYMTAG] Twelve-symbol note shorthand
**Move:** Tag each captured line with symbols for emotion, life-facts (pain, goal, obstacle, workaround, background) and specifics (feature request/purchasing criterion, money, named person or company, follow-up), inventing new symbols in the field as needed; combine emotion tags with pain tags since an embarrassed or angry pain carries more weight.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any discovery or sales conversation you intend to review later.
**AVOID WHEN:** Copying the author's exact glyph set matters more than using your own — he explicitly expects you to build your own.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the author's own field system across many conversations.

### [FOUND:CARDNOTE] One learning per index card
**Move:** Write one quote or learning per index card or post-it with its signal symbol, add date and interviewee name right after the meeting, and review by laying the cards out and sorting them; when a problem turns out not to matter, pull all pain-tagged cards and pick a different confirmed problem.
**APPLY WHEN:** Batch reviews with a team; when you need to re-cut past learning without new interviews.
**AVOID WHEN:** You need shared remote access or search — the alternative given is spreadsheets or Evernote with key signals in their own column.
**Deal-gate:** motion=any · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — the author's stated personal preference after trying notebooks and audio.

### [FOUND:RECASK] Asking to record
**Move:** Put the phone on the table at the start and ask permission framed as your own paranoia about missing something, with an explicit promise not to share or publish. Expect almost universal consent — but accept that it ends any casual framing.
**APPLY WHEN:** Formal scheduled interviews where you need verbatim material.
**AVOID WHEN:** Keeping the conversation casual is the point; also the author reports recordings tend to go unused because they cannot be sorted.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=solo/few · offer=any · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — author's own experience; the near-universal-consent claim is self-reported with no count.

### [FOUND:CAPTUREANY] Capture on whatever is at hand, transfer later
**Move:** When a conversation ambushes you, write on whatever surface exists (paper plates, beer mats, newspapers) and migrate it into permanent storage back at the office; if note-taking during the chat would be inappropriate, step away immediately afterwards and write it down.
**APPLY WHEN:** Casual settings — pubs, conferences, parties — where scheduled interviews would break the frame.
**AVOID WHEN:** The transfer step gets skipped; unmigrated notes are treated as lost.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=micro/low · stakeholders=solo · offer=any · market=transactional/stage
**Evidence:** practitioner-anecdote — author's own captures across informal settings.

### [FOUND:MOTIONS] Going-through-the-motions checklist
**Move:** Audit a conversation against the warning list — you talked more than they did, they complimented you or the idea, you pitched and left with no defined next step, no notes exist, notes were never reviewed with the team, an unexpected answer changed nothing, no question you asked scared you, you cannot name the big question this meeting served or why the meeting happened at all. Persistent presence of any item means the process is broken.
**APPLY WHEN:** After each discovery meeting or batch; as a coaching rubric for a team learning the craft.
**AVOID WHEN:** Used as a scorecard on a single unusual conversation — the diagnosis is keyed to persistence, not a one-off.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=product/saas · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — the author's standard self-audit, repeated in the cheatsheet.

### [FOUND:MISTAKELIST] Named conversation mistakes and their tells
**Move:** Learn the six mistake patterns by their spoken tells — fishing for compliments ("do you think it will work?"), exposing your ego / the Pathos Problem ("be honest, I can take it"), being pitchy ("no, you don't get it", "yes, but it also does this"), being too formal (thanking them for the interview, 1-to-5 scales, "let's set up a meeting"), being a learning bottleneck, and collecting compliments instead of facts and commitments ("everybody loves the idea") — and correct in-flight when you hear yourself.
**APPLY WHEN:** Self-review of recordings or notes; coaching a cofounder through their first calls.
**AVOID WHEN:** Used to punish rather than correct — the author frames slips as expected and repairable, including his own.
**Deal-gate:** motion=founder-led · acv=any · stakeholders=any · offer=product/saas · market=any
**Evidence:** practitioner-n-many — cheatsheet consolidation of the book's failure catalogue, plus the author's admission of slipping into pitch mode the day before writing.

## Where this book stops working

- **It is discovery craft, not deal execution.** Commitments, qualification and advancement transfer to a live pipeline; objection handling, negotiation, multi-threading through procurement, pricing negotiation and closing mechanics are simply absent. Using it as a sales methodology leaves the second half of the deal unarmed.
- **Product-risk businesses.** The book states its own limit: conversations validate market risk (want it, pay for it, enough of them) and cannot validate product risk (can you build it, grow supply and demand, retain). Ad networks, affiliate models, marketplaces and games get near-tautological "if you build it I'll buy" answers — see FAILPOINTS.
- **Late-cycle and transactional selling.** LOVEMEH's "accept the lukewarm no and leave" is explicitly scoped to learning-stage conversations; in a late transactional cycle where the close is the job, walking away on a soft signal is a defect, not discipline. MOMTEST3 likewise yields to commitments once a product exists.
- **High-ACV committee deals.** Casual-format moves (CASUALCHAT, CAPTUREANY, ACTNOW, CUTKNOT) are gated to micro/low ACV, solo or few stakeholders, transactional markets. In enterprise procurement, immediate action is procedurally impossible, so ACTNOW's non-action is not evidence, and a single booked trial (CUTKNOT) is not validation.
- **Consumer purchases give murkier signals than enterprise**, because the conversation process does not mirror the purchase process — the book names this asymmetry itself.
- **Deep industry experts** are the acknowledged exception to the no-opinions rule: their informed judgement has real content, and rigidly Mom-Testing them wastes the access.
- **Channel and gatekeeper motions** get only one move (SHELFTEST, single anecdote); partner-mediated distribution is otherwise unaddressed.
- **Equity-bearing venture assumptions.** ADVISORENGINE trades equity for introductions; irrelevant to a solo consultancy or a bootstrapped services business.
- **Expansion and retention** inside an existing account appear only as DIGREQUEST (feature requests from paying customers). Renewal, upsell and churn-recovery conversations are out of scope.

## What the book debunks

- **Asking whether the idea is good** is self-indulgent noise except with experts who have built similar businesses. Adding a number to a hypothetical ("would you pay $X") feels rigorous while remaining a hypothetical — the more dangerous version.
- **"I would definitely buy that"** carries the highest false-positive danger of any phrase in the book; it is future-tense fluff, not a pre-order. "Looks great, let me know when it launches" is a compliment plus stall.
- **"The meeting went really well" / "everyone loves it" / "we're getting a lot of positive feedback"** are warning phrases, not progress.
- **Doing discovery badly is worse than not doing it** — false positives feel like rigour and justify over-investing cash, time and team.
- **Soliciting criticism does not fix the Pathos Problem.** Musk, Reid Hoffman and Gordon Ramsay can invite brutal feedback because nobody fears hurting them; the tactic does not reproduce for ordinary founders.
- **Being pitchy manufactures agreement** — anyone will call an idea great if you are annoying enough.
- **Non-biasing ≠ useful.** Demographic and trivia questions are safe and worthless. A "#1 problem" elicited about a category the person does not care about is a false positive. Ranking exercises and 1-to-5 scales do not reveal whether the person cares at all — the scale is a symptom of the wrong mode.
- **The Meeting Anti-Pattern:** routing every opportunity into a calendar block wastes founder time and blinds you to serendipitous learning. A meeting with no stated purpose defaults to a sales meeting — triply bad: the buyer closes up about pricing, attention shifts to you, and you are unprepared to sell.
- **Failing framings:** "can I interview you", "can I get your opinion on what we're doing" (neediness, invites compliments), "do you have time for a quick coffee" (sets no expectation).
- **Feature requests are inputs, not instructions.** People know their problems, not the solutions. Obeying the request is the documented failure (MTV analytics).
- **"Our market is practically infinite"** is a warning sign, not an asset. Serving several segments at once yields a mediocre product for many and lets internal feature debates be won by invoking a hypothetical audience. Twenty interviews across twenty customer types is one data point each on twenty businesses.
- **Seniority is not representativeness** — impressive interviewees make good status reports and bad evidence; the self-deception is optimising who you can say you met.
- **The learning bottleneck.** "You worry about the product, I'll learn what we need", "because the customers told me so", "I don't have time to talk to people, I need to be coding" — customer statements are easy to misread, so "the customer said so" is an unfalsifiable trump card. Resistance to conversations is a speed instinct that measures speed in features shipped rather than risk removed.
- **Outsourced discovery** reliably returns softened signal; a hired interviewer glosses lukewarm or bad news, and founders assimilate disconfirming evidence poorly at second hand. Hiring people to arrange and run meetings is fine only if founders are present listening.
- **Notes fail on retrieval, not capture.** A sprawling general notebook and a pile of audio files die the same death; notes you never look at are worthless. Laptop note-taking reads as rude in-meeting.
- **Landing-page conversion metrics** are treated skeptically: the author credits Buffer's Joel Gascoigne with acting on the conversations from emailing every signup, not on the conversion rate.
- **Both extremes rejected:** skipping discovery, and turning discovery into months of full-time interviewing that substitutes for shipping. Getting stuck inside the process is itself a named failure.
- **Slicing is a fast tool, not a planning ritual** — theorising all day instead of contacting people is the failure mode; a segment that reads specific but has no access route is still broken.

## Numbers that matter

- **3** Mom Test rules; **3** bad-data types; **3** big learning questions per batch; **3** ranking criteria for segment selection; **3** ways to talk to the wrong people; **3** results of a good meeting (facts, commitment, advancement).
- **~$10M** lost by the first startup the author worked at, attributed to trusting future-tense purchase promises (self-reported).
- **≥$500k** estimated cost of one unasked legal/content-ownership question (self-reported).
- **MTV case:** ~90% of the built analytics dashboard irrelevant, ~3 months lost (self-reported).
- **~3 months** lost by a farm-fertility-tracker founder on conversations that could not de-risk a product-risk business (self-reported).
- **£100/month workaround vs £120,000/year agency** — a roughly 100× positioning gap from the same problem area.
- **$10/month to $10,000/month** willingness-to-pay spread inside one supposedly single segment (author's own example).
- **$50k illustration:** spending $5k to find a dead end is a good outcome; spending all $50k on one idea is not.
- **Meeting economics:** 1-hour meeting ≈ 4 hours fully loaded. Does the problem exist and matter: ~5 minutes. Typical useful conversation: 10–15 minutes. Industry-expert deep dive: an hour or more. Sales-stage meeting: 30+ minutes, allocated ~5 lateness / ~5 hello / ~5 questions / ~10 demo / ~5 next steps.
- **Cold outreach:** ~100 contacts → ~98 refusals → 2 conversations, framed as success.
- **Advisors:** 5 advisors at ~0.5% equity each, met monthly, producing roughly weekly intros (self-reported).
- **Immersion target:** speakers charging $5–50k per talk.
- **Prototype validation:** ~a dozen prototypes sold pre-launch by the smartphone-tripod designer (self-reported, second-hand).
- **Stop conditions:** 3–5 conversations for a simple industry and tight segment; more than 10 with scattered results means the segment is too fuzzy; expect to answer most customer questions within a week.
- **Process timings:** up to 1 hour of prep, never a week; 5 minutes of LinkedIn/site due diligence; 1–2 weeks of concentrated conversation before pushing for commitment; ~20 minutes from phone call to booked trial in the personal-trainer story (self-reported).
- **Meeting configuration:** 2 attendees optimal, 3+ overwhelming. One event or evening ≈ a dozen conversations (self-reported).
- **Notes:** 12 core symbols (3 emotion, 5 life, 4 specifics); a couple of minutes to date and label a batch of cards.
- **Cheatsheet counts:** 8–9 going-through-the-motions signals; 5 commitment-absence signals; 6 named mistake categories; 8 key skills. Segmentation example: "sales organisations with 25–250 reps" as a constraint that still leaves too much diversity.
- **Author context:** deals with Sony and MTV reached after ~3 years (self-reported). Teenage end users interviewed for the interactive-advertising product: **zero**.

## Blind spots of this book

- **Evidence base is one practitioner.** Every claim rests on the author's own companies plus founders he coached — no trials, no datasets, no counts beyond self-reported anecdote. Effect sizes are unknown; the diagnostics (mixed feedback → wrong segment; no commitment → nothing happened) are cheap-to-run checks, not validated predictors. Outside attributions (Lafley/Martin, Steve Blank, Neil Rackham, Brant Cooper, Amy Hoy) are acknowledgement, not evidence for any specific move.
- **Survivorship and selection.** The cases shown are ones where the technique produced a memorable outcome; there is no account of conversations that passed the Mom Test and still misled, or of businesses that succeeded while ignoring it.
- **No base rates.** How many good conversations convert to commitments, what a normal referral rate looks like, how often an anchored past-behaviour answer still turns out wrong — none of it is quantified, so the moves cannot be prioritised by expected value.
- **Deal execution is absent by design**, so the book gives no guidance on what to do after a commitment: sequencing multi-stakeholder approval, contracting, pricing negotiation, churn.
- **Nothing on scaled or asynchronous evidence.** Surveys, analytics, cohort behaviour, A/B tests and product telemetry are either dismissed or unaddressed; the method assumes a founder with time for face-to-face conversations and does not say what replaces it at volume.
- **Cultural and geographic uniformity.** All examples are UK/US, English-speaking, mostly Western startup contexts. Norms around directness, refusal, hospitality-driven agreement and the acceptability of cold approach vary, and the book does not consider it.
- **Regulated and gatekept industries** (healthcare, finance, defence, education procurement) appear only as access anecdotes; the compliance constraints that make casual conversation impossible are not addressed.
- **2013 vintage.** LinkedIn cold messaging, blogs and physical meetups are the assumed channels; nothing on communities, DMs at modern saturation, or the current response-rate reality of cold outreach.
- **The recording/notes system predates searchable transcription**, and the objection it raises to audio (unsortable) no longer holds in the same form.
- **Self-application is limited.** The book insists founders assimilate disconfirming evidence poorly at second hand, but its own remedy — the founder running the conversations — leaves the founder's own bias in place with only a note-taking cofounder as a check.
